## The Rule (from HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §XXX)

- A chieftain's ship was his *other body*. Ships had names — Óláfr Tryggvason's **Ormrinn langi**, the Long Serpent, the great dragon-ship of the age [Heim] — and a named ship carried its owner's luck and fame the way a sword carries its maker's. Shipwrights were master-craftsmen of the highest honor; a well-found ship was wealth, weapon, and status in one hull.
- **Naming a ship** (a ceremony, not an errand): when a PC first commands a vessel, they name it before witnesses — a **Charisma (Performance)** or plain declaration, but the name must *mean* something (kenning-craft encouraged, §XX). A named ship gains a **Ship-luck** rating equal to its captain's Hamingja (§XVI) — the vessel shares the luck-line. An unnamed ship: −1 on all storm-saves (§XIII) — the sea does not respect the anonymous.
- **Ship as status:** owning a seaworthy ship: **+2 Orðstír** (you are a man who can *go*); commanding a named longship in battle: **+1 Orðstír** per voyage survived. Losing a ship through cowardice or negligence (abandoning her in a storm you could have fought): **−2 Orðstír, −1 Hamingja** — the sagas judge the captain who outlives his ship *ashore*. Going down *with* a fought ship: a good death (§XIV) of the highest order — full inherited Orðstír, and the crew's loyalty becomes saga-verse.
- **Ship-maintenance** (the unglamorous teaching): each winter laid up, the ship needs work — tar, rope, sail-cloth, silver. A captain who pays it: the ship holds her luck. A captain who starves her: the GM rolls secretly each spring — on 1–2 on 1d6, something fails at the worst moment (a stay parts in a storm, a strake opens). The sea audits neglect.
- **Ship-burial** (for the great dead): laying a chieftain in their ship under the mound costs the ship itself — a fortune surrendered — but grants the dead **double** inherited Orðstír (§I) and the household **+2 Hamingja** for a year. The culture's most extravagant funeral, and its logic laid bare: *the dead take their tools, and the living inherit the glory of the giving* — the great ship-burials of Oseberg/Gokstad writ in oak (archaeology, not saga-text, flagged in the codex).

## Teaching Beat

Players learn that technology here is *identity*: the ship is not transport, it is the self extended over water. A captain's relationship with their vessel — named, maintained, mourned — teaches the Norse entanglement of craft, pride, and soul better than any lecture on "Viking ships."
