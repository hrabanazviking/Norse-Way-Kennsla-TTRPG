# Holmgang — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 8)

**Video:** `videos/ep08-holmgang.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep08-holmgang.md`

## I. Grounding — Why It Is True

*Hólmganga* — "island-going" — was the formal duel of the Norse world, and the sagas treat it as *law's sharp edge*: regulated killing with liturgy. In *Egils saga*, the duel with Ljot the Pale is staged with ritual precision: *"a fair plain near the sea, which was to be the place of combat. The ground was marked out by stones lying round in a ring"* [Eg ch.67]. Before the fighting, Ljot *"declared the law of combat, that he should ever after bear the name of dastard who should draw back outside the boundary stones"* [Eg ch.67] — the penalty for breaking the ring's law was a *name*, which in this culture was worse than a wound. Note the sequence the saga insists on: the ground is marked, the law is *declared aloud*, and only then do they close. Liturgy first, violence second.

Challenges were issued for insults, for women, for *lands and inheritances* — Ljot the Pale "amassed him wealth by duels," challenging landowners for their estates [Eg]. This is the crucial point the mechanics exist to teach: the duel was not barbarism escaping law, it was law *containing* violence, so that feuds could end without burning the district down. A district that watched two men settle a land-claim in the ring did not have to watch their kin burn each other's halls for a decade (§XX).

⚠️ **Tradition, not fact:** the hazel-pole ring and the **three-shield custom** (each duelist bringing three shields) come from later saga tradition; the historical record for the details is thin. The transcript and codex both flag this. The GM may simplify to a stone ring and one shield — the *law* of the duel is what matters, not the props.

**What is homebrew here:** the ring, the witnessed challenge, the stakes, and the "no weregild for the lawfully slain" principle are saga-attested. The numbers — −3 Orðstír for declining, +2 for a just victory, the two ritual constraints as game rules — are Volmarr's machinery. The first-blood/yielding definitions, the mercy rule, the champion rule, and the unlawful-challenge procedure below are the expansion's.

## II. The Complete Playable Rule

### At a 5E Table

**1. Challenge & stakes (before witnesses, always).** The challenger **names the grievance and the stakes** aloud: weregild, land, a public apology, a woman's hand — all valid saga stakes (§XI). Vague challenges are void: *"I challenge you for... reasons"* is not a challenge, it is noise, and the would-be challenged may laugh it off without penalty (expansion). The stakes are sworn like an oath (§IX): the GM records them, and breaking the sworn terms later is **oathbreaking** (*eiðrofi*). The challenged has **three days** to answer unless the insult demands immediacy (GM's call; níð-born challenges demand answer *now* — the sun is the deadline, §X).

**Who may challenge** (expansion): any free person may issue holmgang — karl or jarl, man or woman. But the challenged may demand the challenger *prove standing*: a thrall, an outlaw, or a níðingr has no standing to issue a lawful duel, and their "challenge" may be answered with contempt, not steel. A challenge between gross unequals (a jarl challenging a landless karl for his *life*) may be judged *unlawful* at the Thing afterward — the ring is for settling, not for hunting.

**Declining.** The challenged may decline — at the cost of **3 Orðstír** before every witness (cowardice witnessed, §I). Exception: if the challenge is **níð-born** (issued to answer níð, or carrying an ergi-accusation), declining is **social death**: the decliner takes 1 níð-mark immediately and loses 3 Orðstír — the equivalent of letting níð go unanswered by dusk (§X). There is no clean way to refuse that fight.

**Answering by champion** (expansion): the challenged may name a champion to fight in their place — but only with the *challenger's* witnessed consent, and the champion fights for the challenged's stakes. Hiring a champion without consent is cowardice (−3 Orðstír as declining); a champion who loses costs their *principal* the stakes, not their own honor — though a champion who wins gains +1 Orðstír for the deed.

**The champion's risk** (expansion):
- A champion who *dies* in the ring dies lawfully — no weregild owed, rites owed by the victor's side.
- A champion may not be compelled: fighting another's duel under threat or debt-bondage voids the duel's lawfulness.
- No one may champion a níðingr's cause — the ring does not lend its law to the honorless.

**2. The ground.** A ring marked with stones (or hazel poles ⚠️); seconds and witnesses stand outside. The ground is **consecrated** by the declaration of the law of combat — spoken aloud by the challenger or a witness, in the saga's own formula: *"he shall ever after bear the name of dastard who draws back outside the boundary stones"* [Eg ch.67]. A duel fought without the declaration is a brawl with stakes, not holmgang: its killing *is* owed weregild (expansion).

**Einvigi — the lesser duel** (expansion): the sagas also know *einvigi* — single combat *without* the holmgang liturgy: no consecrated ring, no spoken declaration, no sworn end-condition. Einvigi settles nothing legally; its killing is owed weregild like any other killing, and its victor gains no +2 Orðstír. If the table wants a duel without law, this is what they are choosing — and the Thing will treat it as what it is.

**The ring's edge** (expansion):
- The dastard-name falls on whoever *leaves* the ring before the sworn end — drawn back outside the boundary stones [Eg ch.67]. Leaving means crossing the stones *under your own will*: fleeing, flinching away, stepping out to catch breath.
- Being *thrown* out does not count — a duelist hurled past the stones by a blow may re-enter without shame, and the witnesses will say so. The law punishes cowardice, not physics.
- A duelist *driven* out who refuses to re-enter has left, whatever their feet claim. The witnesses decide, and they are not fools.
- In a duel sworn to death, there is no leaving — only winning, yielding (if the terms allow it), or dying. The ring is the world, until it ends.

**Duels at the Thing** (expansion):
- Holmgang may be fought *at* the Thing, before the assembly — the greatest audience the law allows, and the most binding witnesses.
- But weapons are peace-bound at the Thing (*þingfriðr*, §II): the duel's ring is *consecrated ground within* the peace, declared by the law-speaker. Fighting outside the declared ring, at the Thing, is a grave frith-breach no duel-law covers.
- A duel fought at the Thing and judged lawful is *unappealable*: the assembly watched, the assembly's peace held it, and no kin may reopen it. This is why the ambitious choose the Thing's field.

**The seconds' oath** (expansion):
- Before the arming, each second swears aloud to keep the ring's law: no interference, no warnings, no weapons passed mid-fight. The oath is witnessed — breaking it is oathbreaking (§IX), not mere meddling.
- A second's oath binds them *after* the duel too: a second who takes up the loser's quarrel and starts a new fight on the field has broken the ring's peace and committed a frith-breach of their own.
- Seconds are traditionally *kin or sworn friends* of their duelist — which is why the oath matters: the people most tempted to interfere are the ones who swear not to.

Each duelist traditionally brings **three shields** ⚠️ — if the table uses the custom, each shield is a real shield that can be sundered by a called blow (the attacker declares *"I sunder his shield"* before rolling; on a hit, the shield breaks instead of wounding — expansion). A duelist whose third shield breaks fights on without one. Seconds may carry spare weapons and messages, and nothing else: they do not coach, warn, or intervene.

**Weapons and armor in the ring** (expansion):
- What the sagas show is shield and sword [Eg ch.67] — but the law does not mandate the weapons. The duelists fight with what they bring, shown openly at the arming.
- Armor is allowed unless the sworn terms forbid it. A duelist who demands the foe fight unarmored while armored himself names an *unlawful* term — the challenged may refuse that term without refusing the duel.
- Missile weapons have no place in the ring: the duel is face-to-face by definition. A bow brought to holmgang is a confession that you mean murder, not law.
- A duelist who *loses* their weapon in the fight may yield, flee the ring (and take the dastard-name), or fight on bare-handed — the law does not pause for dropped steel. A second who shouts a warning has interfered (see below).

**The duel day, beat by beat** (expansion — the liturgy, in order):1. **The gathering.** Both parties come with seconds and witnesses. The ground is already marked.
2. **The naming.** The challenger names grievance and stakes aloud, before all witnesses.
3. **The declaration.** The law of combat is spoken over the ring — the dastard-formula [Eg ch.67]. Without this, it is not holmgang.
4. **The swearing.** The end-condition is named and sworn: first blood, yielding, or death. The GM records it.
5. **The arming.** Shields (three, if the custom is used ⚠️), weapons shown openly. Hidden weapons are níð-work — discovery voids the duel and marks the hider.
6. **The fight.** Normal combat under the two ritual constraints.
7. **The ending.** The instant the sworn condition is met, all fighting stops — mid-swing. The witnesses *say so aloud*.
8. **The reckoning.** Stakes transferred before witnesses; the victor's +2 Orðstír announced; the slain's rites arranged.

**3. The fight.** Run as **normal 5E combat**, with two ritual constraints:
- **No interference.** No ally may enter the ring, cast at a duelist, strike or aid from outside, or shout warnings. Interference is a **grave frith-breach** (§II: −2 Orðstír, +1 Wyrd-debt, a vengeance claim for the wronged side) and **voids the duel** — its result is legally nothing, the stakes are undecided, and any killing is murder.
- **The duel ends as sworn.** Before the first blow, the condition is named: **first blood** (the *first hit that deals damage* ends it instantly — the wounded duelist yields the stakes; a blow that deals no damage does not count), **yielding** (either duelist may drop their weapon and ask quarter; quarter asked must be *given* — refusing it breaks the sworn terms), or **death**. Breaking the sworn terms mid-duel is oathbreaking.

**Simultaneous falls** (expansion): if both duelists fall in the same exchange and the duel was sworn to first blood, the *first wound in the exchange* decides — the GM tracks order strictly in that round. If truly simultaneous (area effect, mutual strike), the duel is *undecided* and must be re-sworn or taken to the Thing.

**The mercy rule** (expansion): in a duel sworn to *death*, the victor may grant life to the beaten foe. The spared duelist still loses the stakes, **plus** owes the victor a life-debt (a debt-token of the highest order, §VI): they must answer one honorable call from the victor, or lose 2 Orðstír. Granting mercy in a death-duel is itself honorable (+1 Orðstír before witnesses) — restraint inside the ritual is worth more than the kill.

**4. Aftermath.** The victor gains **+2 Orðstír for a just duel** (lawful challenge, sworn terms kept, no interference, declaration spoken). The slain is owed **no weregild if the duel was lawful** — the ring consecrated the killing. An **unlawful killing in the ring** (interference, broken terms, striking a yielded foe, no declaration spoken) is **murder**, and the Thing will hear it (§VII) — with the witnesses' testimony carrying the case. The victor in an *unjust* duel gains no Orðstír and loses 2 instead (expansion: the ring does not wash what the law condemns).

**The witnesses' duties** (expansion):
- Witnesses stand outside the ring and *watch* — their testimony is what makes the duel lawful. A duel with no witnesses is a killing.
- A witness who sees interference must *name it aloud*, at once. Silent witnesses to a voided duel share the shame (−1 Orðstír each, GM's call).
- The witnesses confirm the ending: when first blood is drawn, they call the halt. If they disagree, the senior witness present decides.
- Witnesses are owed nothing but are honored: a victor who feasts the witnesses gains +1 Orðstír (witnessed generosity, §I).

**The slain duelist's rites** (expansion): a duelist lawfully slain is owed funeral rites by the *victor's* side — burning or mounding with their weapon, and the remembrance goblet at the next blót (§XVII, §IV). Denying rites to a lawfully slain foe is níð-adjacent (−1 Orðstír); the sagas remember who buried their enemies.

**Holmgang and the feud** (expansion):
- A lawful duel *settles* the named grievance: the loser's kin gain no blood-debt from the killing (§XX). The ring is where feuds go to die.
- An *unlawful* killing in the ring creates a blood-debt at **Burning** temperature immediately — the worst start a feud can have.
- Kin who reject a lawful duel's outcome and take vengeance anyway commit murder: the ring's settlement is law, and law rejected is feud reborn.
- A duel sworn to first blood that ends in accidental death (a weak duelist, a cruel cut) is still lawful — the ring consecrated the risk. The victor owes the rites, not the weregild.

**The stakes, transferred** (expansion):
- Stakes change hands *before witnesses, on the field* — not later, not privately. The witnesses watch the transfer the way they watched the fight.
- Land staked and lost is recorded at the next Thing (§VII): the transfer is spoken before the assembly so no kin can dispute it later. An unrecorded land transfer breeds the next feud.
- A public apology staked and lost must be *spoken*, in the victor's words, before the same witnesses — a mumbled apology is no payment, and the victor may demand it again, properly.
- Stakes already lost cannot be re-staked: the ring settles, it does not loop. Demanding a rematch over settled stakes is unlawful challenge.

**Women at the ring** (expansion):
- The sagas show women *whetting* — demanding, shaming, and driving men to answer insults — more often than fighting themselves [Njál]. A woman may publicly *demand* that holmgang be fought over her grievance, naming the challenger; the demand carries the weight of níð if ignored.
- A woman's hand named as stakes requires her *kin's* witnessed consent — she is not a prize the ring can award without her ætt's word. Staking her without it makes the challenge unlawful.
- Nothing in the law forbids a woman fighting in the ring herself, and the sagas remember shield-maidens — but a woman who issues holmgang is judged by the same −3 for declining and the same terms. The ring does not soften for anyone; that is why its judgments hold.

**Practice bouts** (expansion):
- Blunted-weapon practice in the ring settles nothing and risks nothing: no Orðstír gained or lost, no stakes, no law. The table should *feel* the difference — practice is play; holmgang is liturgy.
- A "practice bout" where one party secretly sharpens their blade is attempted murder, and the Thing will hear it as such. The ring's trust is absolute or it is nothing.

**After the duel: feast or funeral** (expansion):- The custom is that both parties — victor, and the loser's kin if he fell — share a meal after. Refusing the meal continues the quarrel; accepting it seals the settlement.
- The victor pays for the feast. This is not generosity — it is the price of having killed lawfully and living with it.
- If the duel was sworn to first blood and both walk away, the feast is a *reconciliation*: both sides gain +1 Orðstír (witnessed, §I) on top of the victor's +2. The district watches enemies eat together, and the feud-starves.

**Unlawful challenge** (expansion): if the challenge itself is unlawful — issued by a níðingr, a thrall, or an outlaw; or over stakes the challenger has no right to name (another man's land, another man's wife) — the challenged may decline **without** the −3 Orðstír, but must say so *before witnesses*, naming the unlawfulness aloud: *"You have no standing to name my land."* The Thing may later judge the challenge's lawfulness; a challenger whose challenge is judged unlawful loses 2 Orðstír for abuse of the rite.

### At an OSE Table

- The fight runs as **normal OSE combat** with the same two ritual constraints. First blood = the first wounding hit; yielding and quarter as written; the declaration of the law of combat spoken aloud.
- Declining: −3 Orðstír; níð-born decline = social death (1 níð-mark + −3 Orðstír). Victor: +2 Orðstír for a just duel; −2 for an unjust one. Lawful slain owed no weregild; unlawful killing = murder for the Thing.
- Shield-sundering, the mercy rule, champions, and the unlawful-challenge procedure are **identical**.
- **Everything else is identical** — the ring of stones holds in any century.

### Worked Example

*Illustrative only — hypothetical rolls.* At a feast, Ketill publicly calls Einar's father an oath-breaker — níð, before witnesses. Einar demands holmgang for the insult, stakes: a public apology and Einar's father's name cleared. This is níð-born: neither can decline without social death. They swear *first blood*, and Einar's brother speaks the law of combat over the stone ring.

In the ring, Ketill lands the first wounding blow (a roll of 14 + 5 = 19 against Einar's AC 16 — a hit for 6 damage). The duel ends *instantly*, mid-swing — Einar's raised axe freezes, because the law has spoken. Ketill has won the stakes: Einar must publicly apologize for the *challenge*, though the insult to his father stands as Ketill spoke it — that is what the ring decided. Einar lost the duel but kept his honor: he answered níð with steel, as the law demands, and no one calls him dastard.

Had Ketill struck again after the first blood, that second blow would have been murder — the witnesses would carry it to the Thing, and Ketill's victory would turn to −2 Orðstír and a murder charge. The ring gives; the ring also watches.

## III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

Players learn that even killing had *liturgy* — and that the duel existed so feuds could end *without* burning the district down. The −3 for declining teaches that honor is not a feeling but a *price list*; the níð-born exception teaches that some words, once spoken, can only be answered in the ring; the declaration-before-combat teaches that law is *spoken* before it is *done*. And the mercy rule teaches the strangest lesson of all: that the ritual's highest prize was not the kill but the restraint — the victor who stays his hand proves he was never the weapon's servant.
