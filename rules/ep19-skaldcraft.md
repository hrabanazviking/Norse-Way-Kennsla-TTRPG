## I. Grounding — Why It Is True

The skald was not an entertainer — he was a **mint**. A **drápa**, the formal praise-poem with its
refrain (*stef*), made a chieftain's deeds permanent and portable: in a world without written
histories, the poem *was* the record, and a king without a skald was a king the future would forget.
The reverse was equally true: **níðvísur**, scorn-verses, could flay a reputation alive — poetry as
a weapon with legal standing (§XVII). The saga proof is Egill Skalla-Grímsson: condemned to die by
King Eiríkr Bloodaxe, he saved his own head in a single night by composing *Höfuðlausn* —
"Head-Ransom" — a drápa so magnificent the king let him live [Eg]. Poetry was legal tender in the
economy of renown, and the codex's head-ransom rule is its one built-in miracle *because the saga
records it*.

This is also why the system demands the player **speak the stanza aloud** at the table: the poem's
power lived in performance, in breath and hearing. The saga economy ran on *heard* words — Hávamál's
own law, *"a noble name will never die, if good renown one gets"* [Háv 76–77]. The commission price
— "no less than a good sword" — is the culture's own valuation: a great poem was worth a great
weapon, because both won battles, one in the field and one in memory.

## II. The Complete Playable Rule

### At a 5E Table

**Composing.** In downtime (one evening of work), or through the night before the Thing, the player
declares a subject and a kind — **drápa** (praise) or **níðvísa** (scorn) — and makes a **Charisma
(Performance)** check, **DC 15**. The player must speak **at least a full stanza aloud at the
table** (four lines minimum; kennings encouraged). Each apt kenning the GM accepts grants **+1 on
the check (maximum +2)** — the GM keeps a kenning-list as reward-bait, and hearing a good one is its
own pleasure. Aided composition: a second person may assist (advantage) by keeping the beat,
supplying kennings, or holding the mead — the sagas knew the skald's craft was sometimes a duet.
Success mints the poem as an **item**: *"Drápa for Jarl X, by [skald]"* — a trade good of the renown
economy, sellable, giftable, stealable (stealing a poem is theft of honor — answerable).

**Drápa effects:**

- The **subject** gains **+1 Orðstír** among all who hear the poem performed. The skald chooses where to perform it — halls, Things, feasts. A drápa may be performed in multiple communities: the subject gains the +1 **once per distinct community, up to +3 total per poem** — renown spreads, but a single poem's praise thins with distance.
- The **skald** gains **+1 Orðstír per notable patron praised (max +3 per poem)**. Patrons *pay* for this — in silver, gifts, or protection — and the GM should price a commissioned drápa at **no less than a good sword** (treat a good sword as a valuable martial weapon, roughly 50+ gp in trade value; the price is a floor, not a ceiling — kings paid farms).
- A drápa performed for a *fallen* warrior at his funeral (§XIV) grants his **heir +1 starting Orðstír** — the poem is part of the burial goods.
- **Edge cases:** a drápa praising one who holds **2+ níð-marks** is an insult to the hearers — the skald risks **−1 Orðstír** for praising the shamed (the hall will say so). A drápa for the dead of *another* district earns the skald friends there — the GM should note it. Two rival drápur for rival patrons in the same hall: perform both, and let the table judge — the loser of the evening's favor loses nothing mechanically, but the hall remembers.

**Níðvísa effects:**

- The **subject** loses **1 Orðstír** among all who hear it, and must answer — by flyting (§X), holmgang (§XI), or a **counter-poem** (resolved as a flyting exchange, §X) — or take a **níð-mark** for letting scorn stand (§XVII).
- A níðvísa composed **falsely** (the skald *knows* the charge is untrue) earns the **skald 1 níð-mark**: the weapon cuts both ways. The GM determines falsity by the skald's knowledge, not the world's — a skald who believes a lie he was told is a fool, not a liar, but the verse still wounds.
- **Answering by counter-poem** is the skald's proper duel: both compose, both perform, the table judges — winner's verse stands, loser's is laughed out of the hall (loser −1 Orðstír among the hearers).

**Head-ransom rule.** A condemned PC (or an NPC the party champions) may, as Egill did, compose
through the night before execution. One Performance check at **disadvantage** (no sleep, terror,
dawn coming), **DC 20** — kennings still grant their +1s (max +2), because even at the edge of death
the craft is the craft. Success: the judge **must hear the poem**, and any ruler of honor is **bound
by custom to spare the skald** who has so honored him — the GM plays this straight. A *dishonorable*
judge (Orðstír below 5, or 1+ níð-marks) may break custom — but doing so costs **him** −2 Orðstír
before all witnesses, because the hall sees what the custom is worth to him. This is the codex's
**one built-in miracle**, and it works **once per judgment** — there is no second dawn.

### At an OSE Table

- **Composing:** **1d20 ≤ Charisma** (DC 15 in 5E terms); the player still speaks the full stanza aloud — kennings encouraged, and each apt kenning grants **−1 on the roll (maximum −2)**; the kenning-list is still reward-bait. An assistant grants **−2** (advantage's OSE form) by keeping the beat.
- A successful poem becomes the item *"Drápa for Jarl X, by [skald]"* — **identical**; commissioned drápur priced in **gp**, no less than a good sword (roughly 50+ gp in trade value — a floor, not a ceiling).
- **Drápa effects identical:** subject +1 Orðstír among hearers (once per distinct community, up to +3 per poem); skald +1 Orðstír per notable patron (max +3); funeral drápa grants the heir +1 starting Orðstír. Praising one with 2+ níð-marks risks the skald −1 Orðstír — identical.
- **Níðvísa effects identical:** subject −1 Orðstír among hearers, answer by flyting, holmgang, or counter-poem, or take a níð-mark; a false níðvísa earns the *skald* the níð-mark. Counter-poem duels resolved as flyting exchanges (§X), **1d20 ≤ Charisma** per round.
- **Head-ransom:** compose through the night; one check at **disadvantage** (roll twice, keep the worse) against a hard target — success binds the honorable judge to spare the skald, played straight; a dishonorable judge who breaks custom loses 2 Orðstír before the witnesses. Once per judgment — identical, exactly as codex.

### Worked Example

A 5E example (illustrative). During winter downtime, a PC skald composes a drápa for the jarl who
sheltered the party. She speaks four lines aloud at the table, working in two kennings the GM
accepts ("whale-road" for the sea, "battle-sweat" for blood) — that's +2 on her Charisma
(Performance) check. She rolls 14 + 5 + 2 = 21 against DC 15: the poem is minted as an item.
Performed at the jarl's Yule feast, the jarl gains +1 Orðstír among all who hear; performed again at
the spring Thing in a neighboring district, +1 there too (two communities, +2 of the +3 maximum).
The skald gains +1 Orðstír for the notable patron, and the jarl pays her a war-axe and silver — no
less than a good sword, as custom demands. Later, she turns the same craft to scorn: a níðvísa
against a oath-breaking rival, performed in his own hall. He loses 1 Orðstír among the hearers and
must answer by flyting, holmgang, or counter-poem — or wear the níð-mark. At an OSE table, the
composing roll is 1d20 ≤ Charisma with the kennings as −2; everything else identical.

## III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

Players learn why Viking kings kept poets the way modern states keep mints — and why insulting a
skald was dangerous. The party will start *commissioning* poems about themselves, which means they
start caring about being worth a poem about. The economy of praise does the teaching: honor becomes
something you can *hear* moving through a hall, and the table learns, in its bones, that in this
world a verse could ransom a head and a stanza could kill a name. The spoken-stanza rule is the
deepest teaching of all — it makes the player *do* the thing the culture did, with their own breath.
