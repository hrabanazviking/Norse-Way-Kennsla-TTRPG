## I. Grounding — Why It Is True

The land was *inhabited*. The **landvættir** — spirits of place — followed great men in crowds
[Heim], and they were owed respect as a matter of custom: ships were to unship their carved
figureheads before making landfall, lest the sight frighten the spirits ⚠️ (Landnámabók tradition —
later record, old custom). Egill Skalla-Grímsson knew their power well enough to weaponize it:
raising his níðstöng against King Eiríkr, he turned the curse explicitly *"á landvættir þær, er land
þetta byggva"* — "on the land-spirits who inhabit this land" — bidding them hound the king from the
country (verified in the Old Norse text, `old_norse/egils-saga-sagadb-old-norse.txt`). You do not
aim a curse at nothing.

The dead, too, remained neighbors. The mound was a dwelling, and the unquiet dead — the **draugr** —
walked when burial was denied, rites were skipped, or the dead had unfinished business. Glámr, the
shepherd who died unshriven and unmourned, rose to terrorize a valley until Grettir wrestled him
[Grett] (verified in corpus). Þórólfr bægifótr haunted his own farmstead from a botched burial
[Eyrb]. The lesson of both, and of the whole section, is the codex's own sentence: *the dead are a
maintenance obligation.* The honoring rite, the insult and its haunting, the draugr's business, the
seasonal ancestor-maintenance — these are Volmarr's mechanics built on that sentence. The history
says the neighbors are real; the game teaches you to pay the rent.

## II. The Complete Playable Rule

### At a 5E Table

**Honoring the landvættir.** When entering new territory, founding a farm, or before a voyage: a
small offering — food, drink poured out, a silver bit — and a spoken greeting. **(Expansion)** "New
territory" means crossing into a district where you are unknown, or ground no one of your kin has
walked. Cost: trivial — a mouthful, a poured cup, a silver bit (a few sp at most). Effect: **+1
Hamingja** (§XVI; caps at 10), and the GM notes the land as *friendly*. **(Expansion)** Friendly
land, in play: finding shelter is at advantage; weather-rolls tilt kindly (the GM steps one storm
severity down, or grants +2 on related checks). The note lasts the season, or until you leave.
Skipping it where custom demands: **−1 Hamingja** and the land as *cold* — the GM is entitled to one
petty misfortune. **(Expansion)** Roll or choose 1d6: 1, a horse goes lame; 2, a food cache spoils;
3, a tool or oar breaks at the worst moment; 4, a water skin fouls; 5, the trail is lost — half a
day's sail wasted; 6, a sudden squall on a fine day. The misfortune should be *traceable*: the table
should be able to connect it to the skipped greeting. That connection is the lesson.

**Insulting the landvættir.** Fouling a holy spring, cutting a sacred grove, raising a níðstöng
*falsely* on their ground: **−2 Hamingja** and a **haunting**. **(Expansion)** An *honest* níðstöng
— raised for a true grievance per §XVII — does not insult the landvættir; it *enlists* them against
the target. Falseness is the insult. The GM runs minor phenomena: livestock sicken, tools break,
milk sours, sleep is troubled — no benefit from one long rest in seven — until atonement is made: a
proper blót at the site (§IV) plus a gift of real value. **(Expansion)** "Real value" means at least
the price of a good weapon, or a service the GM names; the blót must be public, before witnesses —
private apology does not reach the spirits. **(Expansion)** The haunting falls on the household or
ship's company, not only the insulter: the spirits fine the roof, not just the hand. One haunting
per insulted site; each needs its own atonement; the long-rest penalty does not stack, but neither
does one blót cover two sites.

**The unquiet dead.** Any death without proper rites (§XIV) — no funeral, no remembrance-cup, the
body left unburied — risks the dead *walking*. The GM rolls secretly: on **1–2 on 1d6**, the dead
returns as a **draugr** (use the wight or ghast stat block, SRD, reskinned — bloated, grave-cold,
impossibly strong). A draugr is not a random monster: it has *business*, and it cannot be
permanently laid until the business is settled *and* the rites are performed. **(Expansion)** Roll
or choose the business 1d6: 1, it guards its grave-goods; 2, it punishes its killer; 3, it finishes
its feud; 4, it claims an unpaid debt; 5, it was denied its inheritance; 6, it died unavenged and
demands it. Laying it means settling that business *in the world* — paid, answered, or fought out —
and then the rites: burning or mounding with goods, and the remembrance-cup drunk at the next blót
(§XIV). **(Expansion)** There is no spell that lays the unquiet; only settlement and rites. Killing
it twice without rites just makes it angrier: each destruction without rites, it returns in 1d6
nights with +2 to attack and damage — and now it haunts the killer's household too.

**Ancestor maintenance** (the reward side). Keeping a family's mound, drinking minni to the dead at
sumbel (§XIX), and speaking their names at the Thing: each observed season of this grants the
household **+1 Hamingja** (the luck-line, §XVI) — the dead, properly kept, *lend* their luck to the
living. **(Expansion)** Observed at the seasonal turn (§XII/XV): the mound tended (repaired, swept,
offered to), the minni drunk, the names spoken — each participating household member gains +1
Hamingja (cap 10), once per season. Neglect for a full year: **−1 Hamingja** and restless dreams
(§XXIII) until the mound is tended — **(Expansion)** the GM runs a dream-roll at every long rest
taken at home until the tending is done. **(Expansion)** A landless household honors the dead at the
Thing's common mound or a roadside shrine; the luck-line does not require land, only attention.

**Moving the mound.** **(Expansion)** When a household relocates, the ancestors may be carried: a stone
from the old mound, set in the new with offerings and the names spoken aloud, keeps the luck-line
unbroken — but the first season at the new mound grants no +1 Hamingja. The dead are settling in,
and the living must give them time.

**Shipboard observance.** **(Expansion)** Before a voyage, the greeting-offering may be poured to Rán
and the sea-vættir together with the §XIV silver offering — one rite, both customs honored. The GM
notes the sea *friendly* for the crossing: storm severity stepped down once, as §XXV's friendly-land
effect. Sailors who honor the road are remembered by it.

### At an OSE Table

- Honoring the landvættir: a small offering and spoken greeting (new territory, farm-founding,
  before a voyage) — +1 Hamingja (cap 10), land noted *friendly* (shelter at advantage; weather
  tilts kindly); skipped, −1 Hamingja and the land *cold* with its 1d6 petty misfortunes —
  **identical**.
- Insulting the landvættir: −2 Hamingja and a haunting (minor phenomena; no benefit from one long
  rest in seven) until a proper public blót plus a gift of real value, priced in **gp**; honest
  níðstöng enlists rather than insults; one haunting per site — **identical**.
- The unquiet dead: secret 1–2 on 1d6 after a death without rites → the dead walks as a draugr — use
  the OSE **wight or ghoul** entry, reskinned; the 1d6 business table **identical**; it cannot be
  permanently laid until the business is settled *and* the rites performed; each rite-less
  destruction returns it in 1d6 nights at +2 attack and damage, haunting the killer's household —
  identical.
- Ancestor maintenance: mound kept, minni at sumbel, names at the Thing → +1 Hamingja per
  participating household member per observed season; a full year's neglect → −1 Hamingja and
  restless dreams (a dream-roll at every home rest) until tended; the landless honor at the common
  mound — **identical**.

### Worked Example

*Illustrative — hypothetical rolls, not a real session.* The party founds a farm on a new fjord. The
goði pours out ale, speaks the greeting, lays a silver bit on a stone: +1 Hamingja each, and the GM
notes the land *friendly*. Two seasons later a hot-headed PC cuts oaks in the grove above the spring
for a hall-beam — a sacred grove, and everyone knew it. −2 Hamingja, and the haunting begins: the
goats sicken, an axe-head flies off mid-swing, and one long rest in seven gives no benefit until
atonement. The atonement blót is public, the gift is a good sword laid at the spring's edge.
Meanwhile an NPC farmhand dies in a rockfall and the party, hurried, skips his funeral. The GM
secretly rolls 1d6 = 1 — he walks. The draugr's business (rolled 2): it punishes its killer — but
there is no killer, only neglect; the GM rules its business is *the denied rites themselves*, and it
will not rest until they are performed with goods and the remembrance-cup. The party burns it once
without rites — it returns in four nights, stronger, and now it comes to *their* hall. The second
time, they do it right. At an OSE table every number above is the same; only the draugr wears the
wight-or-ghoul entry instead of the SRD's.

## III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

The landscape becomes *populated* — every hill potentially watched, every ancestor a creditor.
Players learn the animist economy: the world is full of persons, most of them not human, and all of
them keep accounts. The genius of the section is that it prices *both* directions: honor is cheap
and profitable, insult is expensive and haunting, neglect compounds like interest. After a season of
play, parties start greeting new land unprompted — not because the rules say so, but because the one
time they didn't, the goats sickened. That is not superstition at the table. That is the culture,
learned the way the culture learned it: by keeping the accounts.
