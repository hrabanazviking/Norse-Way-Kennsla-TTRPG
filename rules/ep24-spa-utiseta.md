## I. Grounding — Why It Is True

The völva's spá — the prophecy spoken from the high seat — is the oldest recorded voice in the
tradition: *Völuspá*, "the Seeress's Prophecy," opens the Poetic Edda with a witch's vision of the
world's making and unmaking, summoned by Óðinn himself and beginning, in Bellows's rendering,
*"Hearing I ask from the holy races"* [PE] (verified: Poetic Edda, Völuspá st. 1). *Spá* was not
fortune-telling as entertainment; it was *consultation with consequence*. The seeker came with a
real question and paid a real price — and the answer came veiled, because fate does not speak
plainly to those it is about to bind.

**Útiseta** — "sitting out" — was the related rite of sitting alone outdoors through the night, at a
mound or crossroads, to win wisdom or speech with spirits. The codex flags this honestly and so do
we: ⚠️ it is attested in later sources and the rite's details are thin — treat what follows as
tradition-shaped mechanics, not history. The one-question limit, the riddle-answer, the small price
and the wyrd-price, the forbidden questions answered with silence — these are Volmarr's
systematization of the tradition's *shape*: divination rationed by cost, because a culture that
believed prophecy worked could not afford to make it cheap.

## II. The Complete Playable Rule

### At a 5E Table

**Asking the spá** (a völva NPC, or a PC with the seiðr-path, §V). The seeker states *one* question
aloud, before witnesses. **(Expansion)** At least three witnesses, and the question is spoken once,
in exact words — the way oaths are spoken (§IX): exactness matters, because the answer will hold you
to what you *said*, not what you meant. The völva enters trance (an hour; the hall waits — play the
waiting). **(Expansion)** Play the waiting: no one speaks; each witness may once whisper a fear
aloud, which the völva may or may not take up. The hour is part of the price.

The GM answers with a **riddle-answer**: true, but veiled — a verse, an image, a kenning-chain. The
answer is *never* a plan; it is a weather-report of wyrd. **(Expansion)** GM craft: the riddle must
be *answerable in hindsight* — write the true meaning down and seal it (a folded note, a private
file). If the table cannot see, afterward, that the answer was true, it was not a riddle; it was
noise, and the völva owes the price back.

**The price** — fate does no free samples:
- *The small price:* a gift worthy of the answer — silver, a weapon, a service named on the spot
  (§VI). **(Expansion)** The GM judges worth against the question's weight; a question of counsel
  wants silver, a question of doom wants something that hurts to give. Stingy payment = muddled
  answer (the GM gives two meanings, one true — as §XXIII).
- *The wyrd-price:* after any spá, the seeker gains **+1 Wyrd-debt** (§III). Knowing the shape of
  doom binds you tighter to it. **(Expansion)** Wyrd-debt cannot be declined — the binding is in
  the *knowing*, not the asking. Note the interaction: at 5 Wyrd-debt the Norns draw three runes
  openly as an unavoidable doom (`SYSTEM_VARIANTS.md` Ep 6) — the spá-seeker walks toward that
  reading faster than other PCs. Count accordingly.
- *The forbidden questions:* asking *when* you die, or demanding the gods change what is woven — the
  völva answers with silence, and the seeker loses **1 Hamingja** for the presumption. Some
  threads are not for pulling. **(Expansion)** A second forbidden question the same night: the
  völva refuses all further questions, and the presumption, witnessed, costs **−1 Orðstír** among
  the witnesses.

**(Expansion) The völva's risk.** The völva herself takes on spiritual risk — which is why wise
völur sometimes *refuse* to answer, and the culture respected the refusal. If she refuses *before*
the trance, no price is paid and the gift is returned. If she refuses *mid-trance* — the spirits
troubled — the small price is kept but no wyrd-debt accrues: no answer, no binding. If a völva (NPC
or PC) knowingly speaks *false* spá, she takes +1 Wyrd-debt herself and risks a níð-mark for
oath-breaking (§XVII): prophecy is a sworn utterance, and lies in it are perjury against fate.

**Útiseta** (a PC-declared night rite). The character sits out alone from dusk to dawn at a mound,
shore, or crossroads, fasting. **(Expansion)** The rite's conditions, all required: the site must be
a place of significance (§XXIII); *alone* means no companions within sight or earshot; *fasting*
means no food or drink; no sleep, and no leaving a few paces' circle — the sitter is a post planted
in the ground until dawn. **Constitution save DC 13** at dawn, or gain one level of **exhaustion**
(the night is cold and the spirits are not kind). Success — truly sitting the whole night: the GM
grants **one true dream-image** (§XXIII — the interpretation rules apply) *or* a whispered answer to
a single question, at the cost of **+1 Wyrd-debt**. **(Expansion)** The whisper is plainer than spá
— a sentence, not a riddle — but narrower: one image, or one sentence, and nothing more. Útiseta may
not be repeated for the same question — rewording counts; the spirits are not fooled — and the
second sitting brings only wind: no roll, no answer, the night simply spent. **(Expansion)** The
dawn exhaustion save still applies on a failed second sitting. The cold does not care about your
question.

**(Expansion) Three sealed examples** for the GM's shelf — each with the question, the sealed true
meaning, and the riddle as spoken:

1. *"Will my voyage prosper?"* — Sealed: the ship returns; the cargo is lost to wreckers.
   Riddle: *"The keel comes home; the hold does not."*
2. *"Who betrayed my brother?"* — Sealed: his oath-helper did it for silver.
   Riddle: *"The hand that held his shield now holds the knife's price."*
3. *"Should I take the jarl's offer?"* — Sealed: the offer is honest, but the jarl dies within the
   year and his heir will not honor it. Riddle: *"The gift is clean; the giver's thread is short."*

**(Expansion) Spá for the group.** One question, one seeker — but a jarl may ask on behalf of his
people, a steersman on behalf of his crew. The wyrd-debt falls on the *asker* alone. The witnesses
share the hearing, not the binding. Choose who asks the way you choose who bleeds.

### At an OSE Table

- Asking the spá: one question aloud before witnesses (three at least), in exact words; an hour of
  trance — play the waiting — and a riddle-answer, true, veiled, never a plan; the GM's sealed
  true meaning — **identical**; the rite is system-neutral.
- The small price: a worthy gift (silver, weapon, named service), priced in **gp**; stingy payment =
  muddled answer (two meanings, one true) — identical.
- The wyrd-price: +1 Wyrd-debt after any spá, undeferrable; the Norn's-reading interaction at 5 —
  **identical**; forbidden questions (when you die; demanding the gods change what's woven)
  answered with silence, seeker −1 Hamingja; a second forbidden question refused with −1 Orðstír
  among witnesses — identical.
- The völva's risk and refusal etiquette — **identical**, as above.
- Útiseta: alone from dusk to dawn at a mound, shore, or crossroads (a place of significance),
  fasting, no sleep, no leaving the circle; **Save vs Death** at dawn or gain one level of
  exhaustion; success grants one true dream-image or a whispered answer at +1 Wyrd-debt; never
  repeated for the same question — the second sitting brings only wind, and the cold still bites —
  **identical**.

### Worked Example

*Illustrative — hypothetical rolls, not a real session.* Before a winter voyage, the steersman asks
the völva: *"Does the whale-road take my ship or spare it?"* — spoken once, before five witnesses,
with a silver arm-ring laid at her feet. The hall waits the hour. The GM's sealed meaning: *the ship
survives; the steersman's brother, who sails with him, does not.* The riddle-answer: *"The road
keeps what it is given; count your crew when you come home, and count again."* The seeker gains +1
Wyrd-debt — the binding is in the knowing. Had he asked instead *"When do I die?"* — silence, and −1
Hamingja for the presumption, the hall watching. Later, his sister sits útiseta at her father's
mound for the same question about the voyage: at dawn she rolls Constitution, 12 + 1 = 13 — she
holds — and the GM whispers one true image: a helm floating. The interpretation rules of §XXIII do
the rest. At an OSE table the dawn save is Save vs Death; the wind charges the same price either
system.

## III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

Divination becomes *expensive*, which teaches why the culture rationed it. Players learn to save
their one question for the one that matters — and to fear the answer slightly, which is the correct
relationship with prophecy. The riddle-answer trains a second skill: living with a true sentence you
cannot yet parse, the way the saga characters lived with theirs. And útiseta teaches the loneliest
lesson in the codex: that some knowledge can only be bought with a night of your own life, spent
cold and alone — and that fate does not enjoy being nagged.
