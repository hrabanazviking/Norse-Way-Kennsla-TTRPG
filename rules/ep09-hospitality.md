# Hospitality — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 9)

**Video:** `videos/ep09-hospitality.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep09-hospitality.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §VIII` — this file expands that section; additions the codex leaves open are labeled.

## I. Grounding — Why It Is True

The first seven stanzas of *Hávamál* are not poetry — they are a **manual**, and the table should hear them as one [Háv 1–7]: look about before entering; *"Hail to the giver! a guest has come; / Where shall the stranger sit?"*; then the host's duties in plain words — *"Fire he needs who with frozen knees / Has come from the cold without; / Food and clothes must the farer have, / The man from the mountains come"*; *"Water and towels and welcoming speech / Should he find who comes to the feast."* The guest's conduct is legislated too: the wise guest sits silent, watches, listens, *"A man shall not boast of his keenness of mind, / But keep it close in his breast"* — and *"If renown he would get, and again be greeted, / Wisely and well must he act."* Note that last line: hospitality is *how renown is made*. The door is an engine of honor.

The time limit is set in stone as well: *"Forth shall one go, nor stay as a guest / In a single spot forever; / Love becomes loathing if long one sits / By the hearth in another's home"* [Háv 35]. In the saga world, *gestrísni* was sacred: harming a guest annihilated the host's honor utterly — the hearth was the innermost frith-circle (§II), and blood under a host's roof was a cosmic crime, not a private quarrel. The sagas are full of strangers at doors because winter made the rule load-bearing: in a land of ice and wilderness, refusing the traveler was a kind of killing.

**What is homebrew here:** the guest-rite's content (fire, food, seat), the three-day limit, the weapon-binding, and the sacredness of guest-right are all attested. The *numbers* — +1/−1 Orðstír, the grievance claim as a game object, the winter-refusal debt, the host's protection duty — are Volmarr's machinery (and the expansion's) for making the door a mechanic.

## II. The Complete Playable Rule

### At a 5E Table

**The guest-rite (the host's duty).** When PCs arrive as guests, the GM runs the guest-rite — **roleplayed, not handwaved**. The host *must* offer, in order: **fire** (warmth, a place by the hearth — the frozen traveler is brought in from the cold *first*), **food and drink** (a real meal, not scraps; water and towels and welcoming speech for the feaster [Háv 4]), and **a seat** (a named place at the table — being seated last, or left standing, is a slight the table will notice).

**Seating is a language** (expansion):
- The seat *by the host* is honor; the seat *by the door* is tolerance; *no seat* is refusal.
- Moving a guest to a lower seat mid-feast is a public insult (−1 Orðstír to the host, and the guest gains a grievance claim).
- A guest who *takes* the high seat unbidden commits a breach of the rite — not frith-breach, but presumption (−1 Orðstír among the witnesses).

**How well was it performed?** (expansion — the GM's checklist, judged openly):
- Fire, food, seat all offered promptly and in order: **well performed** → +1 Orðstír (witnessed generosity, §I).
- Offered grudgingly, late, or piecemeal (the meal is scraps, the seat is a stool by the door): **poorly performed** → no gain, no loss, but the guests notice — the GM says so aloud.
- **Withheld** (no fire, no food, or the door barely opened): −1 Orðstír, and the guests gain a **grievance claim** — a formal, speakable claim they may press at the next Thing for apology and a gift, or carry as justification if the feud turns hot (§VII, §XX). The claim is public: the table hears it spoken.
- **Lavish** (meat, ale, gifts for the road, the high seat offered): +1 Orðstír **and** each guest owes a **debt-token of thanks** (expansion: gratitude is a debt, §VI — the host's generosity becomes an obligation the guest must answer).

**The host's protection** (expansion): the guest-rite is a *sanctuary*, and the host is its warden. If a third party threatens or attacks the guest under the host's roof, the host **must** defend the guest — standing aside is a hearth-frith breach by omission (−2 Orðstír, +1 Wyrd-debt, as §II). This is why hosts disarmed guests at the door: the peace of the hall was the *host's* honor on the line.

**Refused outright in winter** (expansion): turning a traveler from the door in winter weather is the act the sagas treat as near-monstrous. The refuser loses 1 Orðstír **and** gains 1 Wyrd-debt — the cold the traveler would have died in is laid at the refuser's wyrd. In fair seasons, refusal costs only the −1 Orðstír and the grievance claim.

**The guest's duties** (mechanical, not advisory):
1. **Do not overstay.** After **three days**, the host may name a departure day **without insult** — no Orðstír cost, no slight taken [Háv 35]. A guest who refuses the named day loses 1 Orðstír (churlishness witnessed) and the host may withdraw the guest-rite without penalty (expansion). A guest who *asks* to stay longer, and is granted it, owes a debt-token of thanks for the extension.
2. **Do not boast.** A boast under the host's roof triggers the **braga-goblet rule** (§IV): it is binding, rated in boldness, and failure costs Orðstír equal to its boldness [Háv 6]. The roof does not make the boast smaller.
3. **Weapons peace-bound.** Swords stay sheathed, spears stacked, axes hung — under the host's roof. **Drawing steel under a roof is a hearth-frith breach** (§II): −2 Orðstír, +1 Wyrd-debt, and a vengeance claim for the host — even if no blow lands. The *threat* breaks the circle. (The host may *require* weapons stacked at the door; refusing to yield them is refusing the guest-rite, and the host may refuse entry without penalty — expansion.)
4. **Do not steal, spy, or seduce under the roof** (expansion): any of these is a hearth-frith breach as above. The guest-rite is a sanctuary, not an opportunity. Discovery later is judged as harshly — the breach counts from the act, not the discovery.
5. **Gifts for the road** (expansion): a guest who departs without thanks — no word, no token, no spoken gratitude — loses 1 Orðstír among those who hear of it. *"If renown he would get, and again be greeted, wisely and well must he act"* [Háv 4].

**The grievance claim, step by step** (expansion):
1. **The speaking.** The wronged guest speaks the claim before witnesses, naming host, place, and the withheld rite. An unspoken claim is not a claim — honor exists only in the eyes of others (§I).
2. **The carrying.** The claim travels with the guest. It may be pressed at the next Thing (§VII) for a formal apology and a gift of real value.
3. **The pressing.** At the Thing, the claim is pleaded like any suit: oath-helpers, witnesses, the crowd's mood. A proven claim costs the host the apology, the gift, *and* 1 Orðstír publicly.
4. **The alternative.** The guest may instead declare the claim *settled* by accepting the host's later hospitality — the door reopens, the ledger closes. Forgiveness, witnessed, is also honor.

**Guest-right violation.** Harming a guest — or a guest harming the host — is a **hearth-frith breach**, the gravest circle (§II). The violator's name is **spoken at the next Thing** (the law-speaker names them before the assembly, §VII), and **no weregild can be refused** — meaning: whatever weregild the Thing sets, the violator *must* pay; they have no standing to haggle, counter-claim, or refuse. Refusal after sentencing is outlawry-ground (§VII). Additionally the violator gains **+1 Wyrd-debt** (§II) — the cosmos watched too.

**The wandering rule.** A PC traveling **alone in winter** may claim guest-right at **any steading** — and the steading must honor it. This is why the sagas are full of strangers at doors: survival *required* it, and the mechanics above are why hosts complied. A winter traveler who is *properly* received owes the host the guest's duties in full — including the three-day clock. (Traveling *with* an armed band in winter is not wandering — it is an arrival, and the steading may treat it as one: the guest-rite is for the traveler, not the troop. Expansion.)

**A model guest-rite scene** (expansion — beats for the GM, in order):
1. **The approach.** The traveler is seen coming. The host comes to the door — the rite begins at the threshold, not the table.
2. **The hailing.** "Hail to the giver! A guest has come" [Háv 2] — or the table's own words. Names are exchanged. A guest who will not give a name is owed the rite anyway — but watched.
3. **The fire.** The frozen traveler is brought to the hearth *first*, before questions. This is the detail that teaches.
4. **The seating.** A named place. The high seat is never the guest's; the seat of honor by the host is the mark of true welcome.
5. **The meal.** Food, drink, water, towels, welcoming speech [Háv 4]. The host eats *with* the guest — sharing the meal is sharing the peace.
6. **The news.** Guest's news for host's news — the exchange that makes the rite an institution, not a charity.
7. **The binding.** Weapons stacked, peace declared aloud: "You are under my roof's frith." The GM names the circle (§II) — the table should hear it.

**Hospitality between PCs** (expansion): when one PC hosts the others, the same rite runs — roleplayed, not handwaved. A PC host who performs it well gains the +1 Orðstír *from the table's witnessing* — honor exists only in the eyes of others (§I), and the table counts. A PC guest who violates guest-right against a PC host triggers the full breach: the name spoken at the next Thing, no weregild refused. The rules do not soften for friendship; that is the point.

**The host's table as intelligence** (expansion): the guest-rite is also how news travels — the host is *owed* the guest's news, and the guest is owed the host's. A guest who withholds vital news (a feud brewing, a raiding fleet sighted) and harm follows has broken the rite's spirit: −1 Orðstír when it comes out. The hearth is a sanctuary *and* a news-exchange; both are sacred.

**When the host is your enemy** (expansion):
- Guest-right does not check the feud ledger: a traveler may claim the rite even at a foe's steading, and the foe *must* honor it. The sagas turn on this hinge repeatedly.
- The host who honors the rite for an enemy gains **+2 Orðstír** instead of +1 — the table witnesses the harder generosity, and honor weighs difficulty (§I).
- Neither party may use the rite as a trap: violence planned *for* the guest's arrival (or the host's table) is hearth-frith breach with **+1 Wyrd-debt** on top of the breach costs — the cosmos punishes the perversion of sanctuary especially (§II, §III).
- The guest under a foe's roof is doubly bound: any breach of the guest's duties there is judged at the Thing with no sympathy and no mitigation.

**Hospitality and the circles of frith** (expansion):
- The hearth is the *innermost* circle (§II) — which is why its breach is the gravest. The guest-rite is frith *made personal*: the host extends their own circle around a stranger.
- A steading that keeps the guest-rite faithfully for a full year gains a reputation the GM should track: travelers seek it out, skalds praise it, and the host gains +1 Orðstír at each seasonal turn the district witnesses it (§XII). The door, kept open, becomes renown.
- Conversely, a steading *known* for withholding the rite finds fewer travelers, colder welcomes abroad, and −1 on reaction rolls (5E: disadvantage on Charisma checks) with strangers who have heard — the door, kept shut, becomes the story told about you.

**The guest-gift** (expansion):
- The host who sends the guest off with a *parting gift* — food for the road, a cloak, a token — creates a debt-token of thanks (§VI) even where none existed. Parting generosity is how the rite *reproduces itself*: the guest, now debtor, must one day host in turn.
- A guest who *refuses* the parting gift without a spoken reason insults the host (−1 Orðstír to the refuser, §VI: refusing a gift is itself an insult unless a reason is spoken before witnesses).
- The cheapest worthy parting gift is *food for three days* — exactly the length of the rite's clock [Háv 35]. The symmetry is the teaching: the host feeds the road the guest must walk.

**The vulnerable at the door** (expansion):
- The rite binds hardest where need is greatest: turning away children, the elderly, or the wounded in winter is *double* shame — −2 Orðstír and +1 Wyrd-debt. The sagas judge this the act of a níðingr-in-spirit, and the table should feel the weight.
- A host who takes in the vulnerable *beyond* the three days — fostering the orphan through winter, nursing the wounded traveler whole — gains +1 Orðstír at the seasonal turn, witnessed by the district (§XII). This is how reputations are built that outlive the builder.

**Hospitality at the Thing** (expansion):- During the Thing (§VII), the host-district owes the guest-rite to *all* attendees — the assembly is a gathering of travelers, and the law knows it. The þingvöllr has booths, fires, and shared meals for exactly this reason.
- Violence between guests at the Thing violates *both* guest-right and þingfriðr — the penalties stack: hearth-frith breach costs *plus* the Thing's own judgment. The law protects itself first (§VII), and its guests second, and both absolutely.

**The door-warden's questions** (expansion):
- The host may ask three things at the threshold, and custom expects them: *your name, your errand, your news*. A guest who answers all three plainly is owed the full rite without suspicion.
- The guest may *withhold* their errand — "my errand is my own" — and still be owed fire, food, and seat. But the host is then entitled to watchfulness: the withholding is noted, and the host's protection duty does not extend to consequences of the hidden errand.
- A guest who gives a *false* name has broken the rite's spirit from the first word: discovery is a hearth-frith breach by deception (as §II), and the host's vengeance claim is doubled in the telling — the sagas despise the liar at the door especially.

**Guest-right and outlaws** (expansion):
- An outlaw (*vargr*, §VII/§XXII) has no frith — and therefore no guest-right to claim. A steading may turn the known outlaw away without penalty; indeed, sheltering one *knowingly* shares the sentence.
- But a host who receives a traveler *not knowing* they are outlawed, and performs the rite in good faith, is **not** guilty of sheltering: the rite was given to a guest, and the guest's hidden outlawry is the guest's breach, not the host's. The host gains a vengeance claim against the deceiver.
- The outlaw who claims guest-right by *lying* about their sentence commits a double breach — against the rite and against the law's sentence — and the Thing, when it hears, will add it to the sentence's weight.

**The morning after** (expansion):
- Departure is a rite too: the host walks the guest to the boundary of the homefield — not the door, the *boundary*. The walk is the closing of the circle the arrival opened.
- At the boundary, host and guest exchange final words: thanks spoken, news wished well, the road blessed. A departure without the walk is not a breach — but the table should notice who bothers and who doesn't.
- A guest who departs *in anger* (over a slight, real or imagined) without speaking it leaves the grievance to fester: the GM should treat the unspoken slight as a claim that will surface at the worst time — because that is what unspoken slights do (§XX).

**The host's absence** (expansion):
- If the host is away, the *household* owes the rite: the húsfreyja, the eldest karl present, even the eldest child old enough to speak the welcome. The door does not get to be empty.
- A household member who performs the rite in the host's absence earns the +1 Orðstír *in the host's name* — the honor accrues to the steading, and the steading remembers who held its door.
- A traveler turned away with "the master is not home" has been *withheld* the rite: −1 Orðstír to the steading and a grievance claim, as written. Absence is not an excuse the law recognizes — the hearth does not go cold because the master is hunting.

**Strangers at the door — the GM's winter toolkit** (expansion):
- In winter, *every* isolated steading should get a knock at least once. The wandering rule is not background — it is an encounter generator the sagas used constantly.
- The stranger's *news* is the reward for opening the door: plot hooks arrive as travelers, because that is how news traveled.
- A stranger who refuses to give a name, keeps weapons close, or will not eat is a *story*, not a guest — the GM is signaling. The host who still performs the rite earns the +1 regardless.
- The table should *feel* the cost: in deep winter, every mouth at the fire is a mouth from the stores. The +1 Orðstír is the culture's answer to that arithmetic.

### At an OSE Table

- **Identical.** The guest-rite is system-neutral: +1 Orðstír for a generous host (lavish: plus debt-tokens of thanks), −1 and a grievance claim for a withholding one; the three-day clock, the weapon-binding, the host's protection duty, and the hearth-frith breach as written. Winter refusal: −1 Orðstír and +1 Wyrd-debt either system.

### Worked Example

*Illustrative only — hypothetical rolls.* Caught by a blizzard on the fell road, the party staggers to an isolated steading at dusk. The farmer, Birgir, meets them at the door — played out at the table, not skipped. He brings them to the fire first, sets out dried mutton and ale, and seats the travelers by name: the rite is **well performed**. Birgir's player marks +1 Orðstír, witnessed by the table.

On the third night, the party's skald — deep in his cups — boasts he will drink Birgir under the table and take his silver arm-ring as forfeit. The GM rules this a boast under the roof: braga-goblet rule, boldness 2, written down. The skald fails his Constitution save (a roll of 9 + 2 = 11 against DC 13 — failure); he loses 2 Orðstír before every witness at the hearth, and Birgir keeps his arm-ring *and* gains a story he will tell for years.

On the fourth morning Birgir names their departure day without insult — and the party, taught by the skald's example, departs with spoken thanks and a debt-token of gratitude each. Three seasons later, when Birgir's steading is threatened by a feud, those tokens come due — and the party remembers whose fire saved them in the blizzard.

## III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

Players feel, in their bones, why hospitality was *law*: in a land of winter and wilderness, the door you close might be your own, next year. The guest-rite teaches reciprocity as survival technology — the host's generosity is not kindness but *infrastructure*, and the guest's restraint is the payment. The host's protection duty teaches that sanctuary is *work*, not sentiment: the peace of the hall is the host's honor on the line, which is why the sagas' hosts disarmed their guests with such care. When a PC later stands at their own door in a storm, deciding whether to open it, the mechanics have already taught them what the sagas knew: the hearth is the first circle of frith, and everything else — the Thing, the oath, the duel — is built on it.
