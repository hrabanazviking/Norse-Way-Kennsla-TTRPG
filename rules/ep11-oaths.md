## I. Grounding — Why It Is True

The *eiðr* — the spoken oath — really was the load-bearing beam of Norse society. There were 
no written contracts in the 9th century; a man's sworn word, witnessed, was the contract. The Edda 
itself treats the oath sworn on a ring as *the most sacred of oaths*: Hávamál's stanza 110 has 
Óðinn himself swearing on his ring, and Bellows's note records that Óðinn "violated the most 
sacred of oaths, that sworn on his ring" [PE: Hávamál 110]. If the gods answer for broken oaths, 
men answer for them too.

The saga model is Haraldr Fairhair's vow, sworn before his father's old friend Guthorm at a feast: 
*"I make the solemn vow, and take God to witness... that never shall I clip or comb my hair until I 
have subdued the whole of Norway, with scat, and duties, and domains; or if not, have died in the 
attempt"* [Heim, ch. 4]. Ten years later, his hair uncut and uncombed, Earl Ragnvald cut it and 
gave him the name Fairhair [Heim, ch. 23]. The vow was public, conditional, and terminal — it 
ended only in triumph or death. That is the shape of a Norse oath: it names the exact terms, it 
names the power it is sworn by, and witnesses carry it.

What is Volmarr's homebrew: the *mechanic* — advantage on one check per day while pursuing the 
oath, the numeric Orðstír drop to 1, the 3 Wyrd-debt, the GM holding players "to the letter." No 
saga hands out dice bonuses. The history is the shape of the oath; the numbers are the game making 
that shape *bite*.

## II. The Complete Playable Rule

### At a 5E Table

**Swearing an oath.**
1. The oath must be **spoken aloud by the player, in exact words, before witnesses** (at least one 
named NPC or fellow PC who hears and can repeat it). It names the **power sworn by**: a god, one's 
ancestors, one's weapon, the oath-ring on the altar, or the drink in the cup.
2. The oath must be **verifiable**: the table can tell whether it has been fulfilled or broken. "I 
will protect her" is vague — vague oaths bind vaguely, and the GM is empowered to rule a vague 
oath half-fulfilled. "I will see her safe to Kaupang by the next full moon, or my life is the 
price" is an oath.
3. **Record it.** The GM writes the exact words in the campaign record. Anyone at the table may 
later read it back aloud.

**The strength it lends.** While the oath remains unbroken and unfulfilled, the PC has **advantage 
on one check per day** that directly serves the oath. "Directly serves" means the action's success 
or failure materially advances the sworn task — the GM decides, and announces it ("the oath lends 
you strength") so the table hears it. The oath can serve one oath at a time: a PC pursuing two 
sworn oaths must choose which one the day's strength serves.

**Keeping it.** An oath is fulfilled when its letter is satisfied before witnesses. Fulfillment 
before witnesses grants **+1 Orðstír** (keeping an oath at cost is a deed before witnesses, §I) 
— +2 if the cost was real and public (danger, great expense, public shaming risk).

**Breaking it.**
- The breaker is *eiðrofi*, oath-breaker: **Orðstír drops to 1 immediately**, no gradual decay.
- All **debt-tokens owed *to* them are void** (§VI) — who honors a debt to an oath-breaker?
- The breaker gains **3 Wyrd-debt** (§III) — the GM notes it; wyrd remembers.
- The table remembers: NPCs will speak the old oath back at them for years. The GM should make this 
happen at least once per story arc, before witnesses.

**Restoration.** Only a great public atonement begins restoration:
- Pay **triple weregild** (weregild.json ladder) to the party wronged by the broken oath, publicly 
at the Thing, with witnesses; or
- Perform a **deed of saga-weight** — defined as a deed that would on its own grant +3 Orðstír 
(a great battle, saving the district, a gift of kingly scale).
Restoration is only the *beginning*: Orðstír then rebuilds at the normal rate, and the breaker 
never again gains the one-check-per-day strength from *that* oath.

**Oath conflicts.** Two oaths in direct conflict: the earlier-sworn oath takes precedence in law 
(the later was sworn foolishly, and that foolishness is itself a deed before witnesses — −1 
Orðstír). The GM must warn a player *before* the second oath is spoken that it conflicts.

**The braga-goblet.** Boasts sworn over the cup at sumbel (§IV) are full oaths — exact words, 
witnesses, all of it. The GM writes them down. The table will hold the player to them. Sumbel 
boasts are the most common way PCs swear accidentally.

**False-swearing and compurgation.** At the Thing (§VII), an oath sworn falsely is discovered only 
by wyrd — but the GM notes it, and Wyrd-debt accrues. Oath-helpers (*compurgators*) do not make 
an oath stronger mechanically; they make it *publicly expensive* — each oath-helper of good 
Orðstír who stands with a false oath risks their own standing if the falsehood surfaces.

### At an OSE Table

- The spoken form — exact words, witnesses, named power — is **identical**; a vague oath binds 
vaguely in any century.
- The strength it lends: **+2 on one roll per day** directly serving the oath (or roll twice, keep 
the better, if the table prefers) — `SYSTEM_VARIANTS.md` Ep. 11.
- Breaking: *eiðrofi* — Orðstír to 1 immediately, debt-tokens owed *to* them void, 3 
Wyrd-debt; atonement as codex §IX. **Identical.**
- Fulfillment before witnesses: +1 Orðstír (+2 at real cost) — Orðstír is system-neutral.
- Restoration, oath conflicts, and the braga-goblet: **identical** — the law is the law.
- False-swearing at the Thing: Wyrd-debt accrues as in 5E; compurgators as codex §VII.

### Worked Example

*Illustrative only — hypothetical rolls, not a real session.* Áskell swears, before the whole 
hall at the sumbel: "By Þórr's hammer I name him, I will return Snorri's stolen sword before 
Yule, or my spear-arm is forfeit." The GM writes it down. Two days later Áskell trails the thieves 
through a storm; the GM calls for Wisdom (Survival). Áskell's player says "my oath lends strength" 
— with advantage, a roll of 14 and 4 is played as 14; +3 = 17 beats DC 15. The oath held. He 
returns the sword at the Yule feast before witnesses: +2 Orðstír (cost was real). Had he instead 
sold the sword and denied it, he would be *eiðrofi* — Orðstír to 1, three debt-tokens owed him 
voided, 3 Wyrd-debt — and the GM would have the stolen sword's owner speak his exact words back 
at him in the next session.

**Conditional oaths.** Haraldr's form — "until X, or death" — is the strongest oath-shape: it 
names its own ending. A conditional oath (fulfilled when X happens) lends its strength only while X 
is still achievable. If X becomes impossible (the sworn man dies of fever, the ship is lost with 
all hands), the oath is **released by wyrd** — no penalty, but no strength either, and the table 
should mark the release aloud ("wyrd has taken the oath from you").

**Oaths by the dead and the unborn.** Swearing by one's ancestors binds the *kin*, not just the 
swearer: if the oath concerns kin-honor, the GM may rule that breaking it costs every sworn kinsman 
at the table 1 Orðstír. This is why men thought twice before naming their fathers.

**The oath-ring.** Districts keep an **oath-ring** on the altar or the goði's arm — swearing 
with a hand on the ring is the heaviest form. An oath sworn on the ring, broken, adds **+1 
Wyrd-debt** (4 total) — the ring remembers. Stealing or desecrating an oath-ring is sacrilege: 
treat as oathbreaking by the thief, with the district as the wronged party.

## III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

Modern players promise constantly and casually — "I'll come," "I'll do it," "I swear" — and 
nobody holds them to anything. The oath mechanic reverses the polarity: the moment a player learns 
that a sloppy boast at the sumbel becomes a recorded legal obligation, the whole table's speech 
*changes*. Players start qualifying their words, pausing before they speak, weighing whether they 
can pay. That pause — the weight of words becoming actions — is the Viking Age entering the 
player's nervous system, not as lecture but as learned caution. When the GM finally reads a PC's 
old oath back to them in a crowded hall, the player doesn't learn a fact about Norse culture; they 
*feel* it.
