## Orðstír — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 1)

**Video:** `videos/ep01-ordstir.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep01-ordstir.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §I` — this file expands that section; additions the codex leaves open are labeled.

### I. Grounding — Why It Is True

In the Viking Age, honor was not a private feeling — it was *public wealth*. The *Hávamál* says it with an axeblow's plainness: *"Cattle die, and kinsmen die, and so one dies one's self; but a noble name will never die, if good renown one gets"* [Háv 76–77]. A man's worth was reckoned in what others said of him, aloud, where all could hear. Secret deeds earned nothing; a gift given in darkness bought no standing — which is why the same poem spends stanzas 40–42 on the economy of giving: *"Gift-givers' friendships are longest found… and gifts with gifts requite"* [Háv 40–42]. Renown was minted by *drengskapr* — the warrior's code of courage, generosity, fairness, and cheerfulness in hardship — and annihilated by its opposite, *níð*: cowardice, treachery, perversion, everything the culture spat at.

What is **Volmarr's homebrew invention** and what is attested history? The culture of reputation-as-currency is thoroughly attested: the sagas are full of men whose good name was their whole capital, of flytings fought because an unanswered insult cost a man his standing, of kings keeping skalds precisely to mint renown into portable poetry. The *numbers* are the invention — the 0–20 scale, all PCs beginning at 5, honor spent one-for-one as a mechanical bonus, half passing to the heir on a good death. No 9th-century goði tracked a sheet marked "Orðstír 12." But the rule is faithful: it models, with dice, the exact economy the sagas describe.

### II. The Complete Playable Rule

#### At a 5E Table

**Tracking.** Each PC has **Orðstír 0–20**. All begin at **5** (known in their district, nothing more). The score is **public at the table** — written on a shared sheet or chalked where everyone can see it — because honor exists only in the eyes of others.

**Witness rule *(addition to the codex)*.** A deed counts as "before witnesses" only if at least **one free adult who is not of the PC's own household** saw it and can speak of it. Deeds done before kin alone earn nothing *until* the tale is sung at a gathering (feast, Thing, sumbel) — then it counts, because the hall has witnessed it. Deeds done in total secrecy earn nothing ever: no eyes, no honor. This is the culture's own logic, codified.

**Gain +1** (GM's call, announced aloud so the table witnesses it — the table is the hall):
- Open courage in battle (holding the line, covering a retreat, striking the great blow).
- A generous gift given openly — at least a notable gift (~1 ounce of silver or a thing of worth); lavish hospitality given to guests.
- Wise counsel spoken at the Thing that carries the assembly.
- Keeping an oath at real cost.
- Composing or commissioning a praise-poem for another (§XX).
- Victory in holmgang (§XI).

*(Addition to the codex:)* A single deed cannot earn more than +1. At most **+2 per session** — fame needs time to travel. The GM announces each gain: *"The table witnesses: +1."*

**Loss −1 to −3** (GM's call, announced with the reason):
- Cowardice before witnesses: −2.
- Oathbreaking: −3 (and see §IX's *eiðrofi*).
- Theft by stealth: −2. *(Note: open raiding — víking — is honorable; theft is sneaking.)*
- Insult left unanswered before witnesses: −1.
- Miserliness (withholding hospitality, hoarding): −1.
- Killing a man already beaten who yields: −2.

**Spend — honor is currency, not a score.** Before any Charisma check where your name matters (Persuasion, Intimidation, Performance; gathering warriors, swaying the Thing, hiring ships, being believed), you may **spend Orðstír 1-for-1 as a bonus to the roll**. Spent points return only through new deeds — you cannot hoard reputation; you must keep earning it.
*(Additions to the codex:)* You may spend no more than your current total (you cannot spend into debt), and **no more than +5 on a single roll** — one man's name carries only so far. The spend is declared *before* the roll, aloud: *"I spend 2 of my name."* The table hears it, because the world must hear it.

**Thresholds.**
- **At 0:** *níðingr*-adjacent — no one follows you, no one believes your oath, innkeepers seat you last. (Three níð-marks, §XVII, makes it formal.)
- **At 15+:** saga-famous — strangers know your name, kings seek your counsel, your enemies must answer your insults or lose face themselves *(addition to the codex: enemies who refuse a lawful flyting challenge from a saga-famous PC lose 1 Orðstír before witnesses)*.

**Inheritance.** When a PC dies well (see §XIV, the saga-death), **half their Orðstír, rounded down**, passes to their **named heir or sworn companion** as that character's starting Orðstír. A PC who dies by cowardice, oathbreaking, or straw-death leaves nothing — no inheritance, and the table knows it. *(Addition to the codex:)* The heir must be named aloud at the table before the death, or in the PC's funeral rites (§XIV) — honor cannot be inherited from a corpse nobody claims.

**Interactions.** Orðstír lost to frith-breach (§II), sumbel-vow breaking (§XIX), or ergi-accusations (§XVIII) follows those sections' costs; this section's spend mechanic is the universal one. A níðingr's ( §XVII) Persuasion checks fail automatically where honor is weighed — no amount of spent Orðstír can buy back a ruined name.

#### At an OSE Table

Spend **1 Orðstír → +1 on a 2d6 reaction roll** where your name matters, or **−2 on a roll-under CHA check** (1d20 ≤ Charisma made easier). The same caps apply: no more than +5 to a single reaction roll, declared before rolling, and only where the speaker's name is known or is made known ("tell them who I am").

At 0 — níðingr-adjacent: hirelings will not serve and retainers leave. At 15+ — saga-famous: strangers' reaction rolls start one step friendlier *(addition to the codex: treat an initial reaction roll as if one result friendlier than rolled, minimum "friendly")*. Everything else identical — honor is honor in any century.

#### Worked Example

Astrid (Orðstír 9) stands at the Thing to plead for her accused brother. She wants the crowd moved. Before rolling, she says aloud: *"I spend 3 of my name"* — dropping to 6 — and rolls Charisma (Persuasion): the die shows 14, +3 from her Charisma and spent honor, and her +2 proficiency, for 19. Had the roll failed, the 3 points would still be gone — spent reputation does not return, only new deeds earn it back. *(Illustrative numbers; a real roll may fall where it falls.)*

### III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

Players discover, through their own resource management, that in this world **reputation is the real hit points**. They start performing generosity and courage *because the mechanics reward being seen doing it* — scheduling their giving for the feast, their courage for the shield-wall, their counsel for the crowded Thing — which is exactly how the culture worked. Nobody lectures them about Viking-Age shame-culture; the spend-and-earn loop *is* the lecture. When a player realizes they must risk their name to use their name — that spending down to 0 is possible and terrifying — they have understood *orðstír* better than any essay could teach.

## Frith — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 2)

**Video:** `videos/ep02-frith.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep02-frith.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §II` — this file expands that section; additions the codex leaves open are labeled.

### I. Grounding — Why It Is True

Frith (*friðr*) is not the absence of war. It is the *active sacred state* of right order — peace, trust, prosperity — held alive by everyone's conduct, the way a fire is held alive by tending. Breaking frith was a cosmic crime, not merely a social one: the breaker became *vargr í veum*, "wolf in the sanctuary," outlaw from gods and men alike. The term is old — in Egils saga, Eyvindr "had slain in the sanctuaries (*vegit í véum*), and was become a *vargr*" [Eg] — an outlaw in the full religious sense.

What is **Volmarr's homebrew invention** and what is attested history? The underlying institution is thoroughly historical: the *vé* as inviolable sanctuary, the Thing's enforced peace (weapons peace-bound at assembly), the weregild system that priced every limb and life, and the deep dread of the kin-slayer. The **eight circles** as a named, ordered schema — hearth-frith, the vé, kin-frith (*ættarfriðr*), oath-frith (*eiðafriðr*), thing-frith (*þingfriðr*), guest-frith (*gestafriðr*), land-frith, frith with the dead (*dauðafriðr*) — is Volmarr's canon ordering of real institutions into a playable ladder (the video's narration names six of them as shorthand; the full eight are below). The mechanical costs — Orðstír drops, Wyrd-debt points — are his invention, faithful to the culture's logic.

### II. The Complete Playable Rule

#### At a 5E Table

**The circles.** Every scene takes place inside one or more frith-circles. The GM names the circle aloud when it matters: *"You are under hearth-frith here."* The eight, in order of sanctity:

1. **Hearth-frith** (*heimafriðr*): no violence, theft, or insult under another's roof. The host's fire is sacred ground.
2. **The Vé** (*vé*): hof, grove, altar, marked sanctuary. Weapons peace-bound; no blood shed.
3. **Kin-frith** (*ættarfriðr*): the peace of blood — strongest of the social circles. Feuds are settled, not slaughtered, within the ætt.
4. **Oath-frith** (*eiðafriðr*): peace sworn between oath-bound — foster-brothers, sworn companions, oath-helpers. To break it is *eiðrofi* (oathbreaking, §IX).
5. **Thing-frith** (*þingfriðr*): the peace of the assembly. Weapons peace-bound at the Thing — the law protects itself first.
6. **Guest-frith** (*gestafriðr*): host and guest owe each other peace. Harming a guest annihilates the host's standing *(addition to the codex: see note on the guest's duties — the guest who strikes first breaches it against the host)*.
7. **Land-frith** (*landfriðr*): peace with the landvættir — offerings on entering or settling; no fouling of holy waters or groves.
8. **Frith with the dead** (*dauðafriðr*): the dead buried with rites and left undisturbed. Grave-robbery and skipped funerals break it — and the dead may walk (§XXV).

*(The full ladder with per-circle claim descriptions also lives in `engine/data/homebrew/frith_circles.json`; the costs below are the codex's.)*

**Nested circles *(addition to the codex)*.** Scenes almost always sit in several circles at once (a feast at the host's home = hearth + guest + oath circles). A breach is graded by the **gravest** circle violated — one breach, one cost, the highest applies. Drawing steel on your host's oath-brother at his feast is a hearth-breach, not three separate breaches.

**Breach.** Violence, theft, or insult committed inside a circle is a **frith-breach**, graded by sanctity: **hearth and vé are gravest.**

- **Cost — social:** the breacher's Orðstír drops immediately — **−2 for hearth/vé, −1 for lesser circles**. The wronged party gains a **weregild or vengeance claim** (§VI–VII) that cannot be refused at the Thing. Three breaches, or one grave breach, and the community may declare the breacher ***vargr*** — outlaw in all but name until settled.
- **Cost — cosmic:** the breacher gains **+1 Wyrd-debt** per breach (see §III) — invisible to all but seeresses, drawing ill luck the way blood draws sharks. The GM may spend a PC's Wyrd-debt to impose **disadvantage on one roll per point**, described as the world turning cold: a rope frays, a horse shies, a friend mishears.
- **Declared violence is different *(addition to the codex)*:** a formally declared holmgang (§XI) inside no circle's protection, or war openly declared, is not a frith-breach. Frith punishes the *unwarranted*, the *treacherous*, the *sneaking* blow — not lawful violence conducted in the open.

**Mending.** Frith is restored **only by public settlement**: weregild paid at the Thing before witnesses, or a formal apology with gifts before witnesses. Private apologies mend nothing. *(Addition to the codex:)* A settlement requires **both parties present** at the Thing (or a lawful proxy of equal standing) and **at least seven free witnesses**; the terms are spoken aloud and remembered. Until mended, the breach stands — and the wronged party's claim accrues interest in *honor*, never in silver: an unpaid claim costs the breacher 1 Orðstír per season unanswered, because the community watches who settles and who stalls.

#### At an OSE Table

Breaking frith → **−2 on reaction rolls** within the offended circle until weregild is paid (paid in gp — the OSE economy already speaks this language). Outlawry (declared *vargr*) additionally bars the outlaw from hirelings and strongholds — no retainers will serve, no hall takes them in. Wyrd-debt spends in OSE as the GM forcing a **reroll at +2 difficulty** *(addition to the codex: shift the roll-under target −2, the OSE analogue of disadvantage)* or as a **−2 on a reaction roll**, described the same way — the world turning cold. Everything else identical.

#### Worked Example

At a Thing, tempers boil and Þorvaldr draws his seax on a rival — inside *þingfriðr*, a grave sanctity circle. The GM names it: *"Steel in the circle — þingfriðr is broken."* Þorvaldr loses 2 Orðstír immediately, gains 1 Wyrd-debt, and the wronged man gains an unrefusable claim at the Thing. Later that session, when Þorvaldr tries to haggle with a shipmaster, the GM spends the Wyrd-debt: *"The rope on the cargo net frays as you speak — disadvantage on the check."* Mending comes at the next Thing: weregild paid in silver before seven witnesses, terms spoken aloud. *(Illustrative sequence; real dice land where they land.)*

### III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

Players internalize that peace was not a default — it was **sacred technology**, maintained by ritual conduct the way a harbor is maintained by pilots. Violating it offended the *cosmos*, not just the victim; the Wyrd-debt mechanic makes the invisible visible at exactly the right moment — not when the crime happens, but later, when a rope frays for no reason and the table goes quiet. The modern instinct ("it's just between us") dies fast, and the instinct that replaces it — check the circles before you draw — is nine centuries old.

## Hamingja — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 3)

**Video:** `videos/ep03-hamingja.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep03-hamingja.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §XVI` — this file expands that section; additions the codex leaves open are labeled.

### I. Grounding — Why It Is True

In the sagas, luck is not chance — it is a **quality of the soul**, visible to those who know how to look. Kings and great chieftains are said to have mighty *hamingja*; when a man's luck turns, the saga says so plainly, and his friends grow quiet — because a luckless man drags others down with him [Grett; Heim; Njál]. Hamingja could be **inherited**, could follow a family, could *leave*. It favored the bold, the generous, the oath-keepers, and it fled the coward, the miser, the oath-breaker. *Gipta* — good fortune — was spoken of the way we speak of health: a real thing, cultivated or squandered. The sagas even show luck passing between people: men married into lucky families, and a chief's fortune covered his followers like a cloak.

What is **Volmarr's homebrew invention** and what is attested history? The living-luck concept is thoroughly historical — the inherited luck-lines, the luck that abandons the coward, the sagas' plain statements that this man was *gæfusamr* (lucky) and that one not. The *numbers* — the 0–10 scale, all PCs beginning at 4, one point spent to reroll, the +1-to-death-saves aura — are Volmarr's invention. No saga gives luck hit points. But the rule reproduces the culture's causal logic exactly: luck is not what happens to you, it is *what you are*, and what you are is built from conduct.

### II. The Complete Playable Rule

#### At a 5E Table

**Tracking.** Each PC has **Hamingja 0–10**. All begin at **4** — unproven, unspoiled. Hamingja is **semi-secret**: the GM tracks it openly, but in-world no one *announces* their luck. NPCs judge it by watching you. *(Addition to the codex: the table may know the number, but PCs should never say it aloud in character — "My luck is 7" is as wrong for the period as "my hit points are 34." Describe it: bright-eyed, ill-starred, fortune's favorite, dogged.)*

**Spend — the reward made tangible.** Before any d20 roll is resolved, you may **spend 1 Hamingja to reroll it and take the new result** — this is the universe leaning toward you. You may do this **after seeing the roll but before the outcome is declared**. *(Clarifications as additions to the codex:)*
- The reroll **replaces** the roll — you take the new result, better or worse. Luck is a living thing, not a calculator; the codex's wording ("take the new result") is the rule.
- *(Table variant:)* if your table prefers the gentler OSE-file phrasing ("take the better"), play it so — but name it as a variant, because the risk is the point.
- One Hamingja spent per roll, one reroll per roll — luck cannot be stacked on a single throw.
- You may spend to reroll **a foe's d20** too, forcing *them* to take the new result — fortune's cloak covers you and slips from your enemy. (The foe spends nothing; it is *your* luck at work.)
- A natural 20 rerolled this way may come up worse — the gods have a sense of humor, and the table must feel the risk to respect the price.

**Gain +1** (announced by the GM as *"your luck grows"* — never silently; the luck-line is public in its effects):
- Winning a battle you entered freely.
- A generous gift given openly (§VI).
- Keeping an oath at real cost (§IX).
- Fulfilling a sumbel-vow (§XIX).
- Honoring the landvættir or the dead properly (§XXV).
- Heeding a true dream (§XXIII).
- Composing a praise-poem for another (§XX).

*(Additions to the codex:)* Each distinct qualifying act earns +1; the GM may award at most **+2 per session** to prevent farming. Unlike Orðstír, Hamingja needs **no audience** — the universe saw it — but the act must be *real*: cost paid, not cost performed. A "generous gift" of copper to a beggar while keeping the silver earns nothing and everyone at the table knows it.

**Loss −1** (announced as *"your luck thins"*):
- Cowardice before witnesses.
- Breaking an oath.
- Theft by stealth.
- Killing one who has yielded.
- Ignoring a true dream.
- Insulting the landvættir.
- Miserliness at the feast-table.

**At 0 — luckless.** Once per session, the GM may *force* you to **reroll a success and take the new result** — fortune itself has turned its face away. NPCs sense it: **disadvantage on Charisma checks to recruit followers or secure trust** until Hamingja rises above 0. *(Addition to the codex:)* A luckless PC's forced reroll should be described, not hidden — "your foot finds the one loose stone" — so the table learns the shape of a turned back.

**At 8+ — luck-bright.** Allies within **30 feet** gain **+1 to their first death saving throw of the day** — men *want* to stand near the lucky, as the sagas say they did. *(Addition to the codex:)* The aura does not aid the luck-bright one themselves, and it ends the moment their Hamingja drops below 8. NPC hirelings will follow a luck-bright PC for standard pay where they would otherwise demand hazard rates — the codex's logic extended to the morale economy.

**Bequest.** A dying character may **bequeath remaining Hamingja to kin, foster-kin, or sworn companions, point for point**. *(Addition to the codex:)* The bequest must be spoken aloud — a dying breath, a last word — before witnesses; an unwitnessed bequest does not carry. Luck, like land, is inherited — which is why men married into lucky families (§XXVI).

#### At an OSE Table

Identical. Spend 1 Hamingja → reroll any d20 and take the new result, or force a foe to reroll. At 0: the GM forces one reroll of a success per session; −2 on reaction rolls to recruit or earn trust. At 8+: allies within 30 feet gain +1 to their first death save of the day. Rerolls are system-agnostic — the universe leans the same in every century.

#### Worked Example

Bjorn (Hamingja 6) rolls to shove open a storm-swollen gate while the draugr closes in: the die shows 4. Before the GM declares the outcome, Bjorn says *"I spend one of my luck"* — dropping to 5 — and rerolls: a 16. The gate gives. Two scenes later he has entered battle freely and won; the GM announces *"your luck grows"* — back to 6. Had he spent down to 0 instead, the next session the GM might force him to reroll a hard-won success. *(Illustrative numbers; real dice land where they land.)*

### III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

Players stop treating luck as the dice and start treating it as **conduct**. The cautious, selfish PC watches their rerolls dry up while the generous oath-keeper's pile grows — and the table learns, in its bones, why the sagas call some men "lucky" the way we'd call them "good." The reroll-before-resolution timing teaches the rest: luck is not what saves you after disaster, it is what *leans* — a small, living edge, spent in the breath before the outcome lands.

## Sumbel — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 4)

**Video:** `videos/ep04-sumbel.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep04-sumbel.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §XIX` — this file expands that section; additions the codex leaves open are labeled.

### I. Grounding — Why It Is True

The feast-hall ran on ritual drinking. At the funeral-feast (*erfi*), the **bragarfull** — the vow-cup, the "best cup" — was drunk, and over it men spoke solemn vows (*heitstrenging*): to do some great deed, to avenge kin, to win renown [Heim; ⚠️ the Old English *Beowulf* preserves the fullest picture of the rite, a cognate custom across the Germanic world]. Before it came the **minni** — memorial cups drunk to Óðinn first (*Óðins minni*), then to Njörðr, to the dead, to the king — each toast a small act of remembrance binding the drinkers to what they honored. A vow spoken over the cup, before witnesses, with drink in hand, was **binding as an oath** — and breaking it was oathbreaking with the hall as witness.

What is **Volmarr's homebrew invention** and what is attested history? The rite itself — minni-toasts in order, the bragarfull, the *heitstrenging* — is historical, attested in the kings' sagas and cognate poetry. The mechanics — the escalating Constitution-save drink ladder, +2 Orðstír / +1 Hamingja for a kept vow awarded at the next sumbel, the doubled oathbreaker penalties — are Volmarr's invention, and they are faithful to the rite's logic: the hall remembered, and the man who broke his word before the cup was ruined *before the same witnesses*.

### II. The Complete Playable Rule

#### At a 5E Table

**The rite — played, not hand-waved.** (Ritual order also in `engine/data/homebrew/sumbel.json`.)

1. The **host calls the sumbel** and hallows the cups. The host must provide the drink — a host who skimps on the sumbel loses 1 Orðstír before his own hall *(addition to the codex)*.
2. **Minni-cups** are drunk in order: the gods (*Óðins minni* first), the ancestors, the honored dead, the host's chosen. Each drinker may speak a few words over their minni — a memory, a praise. **Minni kept:** any PC who drinks a minni to their own dead and speaks true words over it gains **+1 Hamingja** (§XXV) — remembrance is maintenance of the luck-line. *(Addition to the codex: each PC may claim this once per sumbel; empty flattery gains nothing — the GM judges whether the words were true.)*
3. The **bragarfull** is filled. Each warrior in turn **may** lay hand on the cup and speak a **vow** aloud: *"I vow that before next Yule I shall…"*

**The drink** (SRD-clean): each full cup is a **Constitution saving throw, DC 10 + 2 per cup already drunk**, or gain the **poisoned** condition until a long rest. The ladder, cup by cup:

| Cups already drunk | Save DC |
|---|---|
| 0 | 10 |
| 1 | 12 |
| 2 | 14 |
| 3 | 16 |
| 4+ | 18+ *(+2 per cup, no cap — the mead always wins eventually)* |

The GM is encouraged to let vows grow bolder as the cups deepen — the mead loosens tongues, and the culture *knew* it. **Drunk vows still bind** *(addition to the codex)*: "the mead made me say it" is no defense — the hall heard it. Only vows spoken *before* the bragarfull round, or not at all, carry no weight.

**Speaking no vow.** Declining the vow-cup carries **no penalty** — a wise warrior who knows his limits is respected. *(Addition to the codex.)* Only the vow **spoken** binds. The shame is not in silence; it is in the spoken word betrayed.

**Vow types** (from the codex's ritual data — lawful forms a vow may take):
- **Vengeance-vow:** avenge kin, settle a blood-debt by blood or weregild.
- **Deed-vow:** win renown by a named exploit (a raid, a voyage, a holmgang).
- **Oath-vow:** swear loyalty, fosterage, or a marriage-alliance before the hall.
- **Forfeit-vow:** stake a possession, a ship-share, or silver on the deed — lost if the vow fails *(addition to the codex: the stake is named in the vow and held by the host)*.

*(Additions to the codex — vow adjudication:)* The GM **writes every vow down** with its deadline (named in the vow — "before next Yule," "before the ice breaks"). A vow fulfilled **in part** earns nothing until completed — but a vow honestly pursued and honorably *abandoned* (the foe died of plague; the ship burned by lightning — wyrd intervened) may be discharged at the next sumbel by the vow-speaker's public account, accepted by the hall's acclaim, with no penalty and no reward. The hall judges; the GM referees.

**Keeping a sumbel-vow:** **+2 Orðstír and +1 Hamingja**, awarded *at the next sumbel* where the deed is **recited before witnesses**. The hall drinks to you. This is the single fastest honorable gain in the codex — because public vows kept are the engine of saga-fame. *(Addition to the codex: the recital is a performance — if another PC's praise-poem (§XX) tells the tale, both earn: the vow-keeper gains the vow reward, the skald gains the drápa's reward. The hall loves a doubled telling.)*

**Breaking a sumbel-vow:** oathbreaker penalties at double weight (§IX) — **−3 Orðstír (minimum 1), +2 Wyrd-debt, 1 níð-mark**, and **−1 Hamingja**. The hall remembers: NPCs will bring it up, unkindly, for years *(addition to the codex: for at least one full in-game year, and at every subsequent sumbel the vow-breaker attends, the GM may have an NPC raise it — the table should dread the cups)*. Breaking a vow also stains the luck-line of any oath-helpers who stood by it — no mechanical cost to them, but the culture notes it, and the GM should have the world note it.

#### At an OSE Table

Vow kept: +2 Orðstír, +1 Hamingja — identical. Vow broken: oathbreaker penalties become **−2 on reaction rolls** among witnesses (instead of disadvantage), −2 Orðstír, one níð-mark. The drink ladder: **Save vs. Poison** at cumulative −2 per cup already drunk *(addition to the codex: the OSE analogue of the escalating DC; alternatively roll 1d20 ≤ Constitution with the effective score −2 per cup)*. Everything else — the rite, the vow types, the binding of drunk words — identical. The feast is system-neutral.

#### Worked Example

At the Yule sumbel, Einarr lays his hand on the bragarfull — his third cup (Constitution save DC 14; he rolls 11 + 2 = 13 and gains the poisoned condition, which only sharpens his tongue) — and vows: *"Before the ice breaks I shall take the weregild owed my brother's blood-debt, in silver or in steel."* The GM writes it down. Come spring, Einarr returns with the silver paid at the Thing. At the next sumbel he recites the deed; the hall drinks; +2 Orðstír, +1 Hamingja. Had he come home empty-handed with no honest account, the penalties would fall before the same witnesses who heard the vow. *(Illustrative numbers; real dice land where they land.)*

### III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

The feast becomes a *mechanical* event, not set-dressing. Players learn to **weigh their words before the cup touches their lips**, because a boast tonight is a binding quest tomorrow — and because the fastest honor in the game hangs on keeping it. The table will start policing its own vows — talking a drunk companion out of an impossible heitstrenging, or quietly taking notes on what was promised — which is exactly what the hall did. The sumbel teaches that in this culture, *speech is action*, and the cup is its witness.

## Níð & the Níðingr — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 5)

**Video:** `videos/ep05-nid.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep05-nid.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §XVII` — this file expands that section; additions the codex leaves open are labeled.

### I. Grounding — Why It Is True

*Níð* was the Norse word for the unspeakable: cowardice, perversion, treachery, the whole catalogue of unmanliness and dishonor. To call a man *níðingr* was the gravest insult in the language — and it was not mere name-calling. Egill Skalla-Grímsson, wronged by King Eiríkr Bloodaxe, raised a **níðstöng** — a scorn-pole: a horse's head set on a pole, carved with runes of curse, turned toward the king's land, calling on the land-spirits to drive him out [Eg]. The saga gives the moment whole — the corpus text (Green 1893) records Egill taking "a hazel-pole… then he took a horse's head and fixed it on the pole. After that, in solemn form of curse, he thus spake: *'Here set I up a curse-pole, and this curse I turn on king Eric and queen Gunnhilda… This curse I turn also on the guardian-spirits who dwell in this land, that they may all wander astray… till they have driven out of the land king Eric and Gunnhilda.'*" Then "on the pole he cut runes, expressing the whole form of curse." The pole was law, poetry, and war-magic in one act. Later law-codes treated the worst *níð*-words as grounds for killing without penalty ⚠️ [Grágás, 13th c. — later than our period, but the dread is older].

What is **Volmarr's homebrew invention** and what is attested history? The níðstöng rite, Egill's curse-pole, the social death of the níðingr, the unanswered-insult rule — all historical, attested in saga and law. The **níð-mark system** — three marks as a counted currency of shame, each mark announced by the GM and witnessed by the table, fading only by public atonement — is Volmarr's invention. No saga says "he had two níð-marks." But the mechanic reproduces exactly what the sagas show: shame that accumulates, is *counted by the community*, and reaches a point of no return.

### II. The Complete Playable Rule

#### At a 5E Table

**Níð-marks.** Certain acts earn a **níð-mark** — the GM announces it; the table witnesses it. Shame, like honor, needs an audience. **Three marks and you are níðingr — socially dead.** (Full act list also in `engine/data/homebrew/nid_penalties.json`; the codex's numbers govern.)

Acts earning marks and shame-costs:
- Cowardice in battle before witnesses: **1 mark, −2 Orðstír, −1 Hamingja**.
- Refusing lawful holmgang when challenged: **1 mark, −2 Orðstír, −2 Hamingja**.
- Oathbreaking (witnessed): **1 mark, −2 Orðstír, +1 Wyrd-debt**.
- Breaking a sumbel-vow: **1 mark, −3 Orðstír, +2 Wyrd-debt, −1 Hamingja** (§XIX).
- Theft by stealth: **1 mark, −2 Orðstír** (open raiding is honorable; sneaking is not).
- Killing one who has yielded: **no mark** unless a formal accusation follows — but −2 Orðstír, −1 Hamingja; the deed stains *(addition to the codex: the accusation may be raised at any gathering within a year)*.
- Leaving an unanswered insult, flyting, or níðvísa standing: **1 mark, −1 Orðstír**.
- Raising a false níðstöng (against one who has not wronged you): **2 marks** — the weapon cuts the raiser.
- Proven ergi-act (witnessed cowardice, coercive/underhand seiðr): **1 mark, −2 Orðstír, −1 Hamingja** (§XVIII).
- Answering an ergi-accusation with silence or flight: **1 mark, −2 Hamingja**.
- Betraying foster-kin: **2 marks**.
- Secret vengeance (murder by night, then denied): **1 mark** — and the blood-debt still stands (§XXI).
- Composing a knowingly false níðvísa: **1 mark** — the weapon cuts the skald too (§XX).
- Mocking the gods' rites or the dream-world publicly: **no mark**, but −1 Orðstír, −1 Hamingja; contempt for the sacred thins luck.

*(Addition to the codex:)* Marks are **announced at the moment of the act's witnessing**, never retroactively in secret — *"The table witnesses: a níð-mark."* A mark earned before witnesses who are all later slain still stands if *anyone* who saw it lives to speak; shame needs only one surviving tongue.

**Níðingr status** (0 Orðstír is the warning; 3 marks is the sentence):
- Your **oath is worthless**: automatic failure on Persuasion checks where honor is weighed; no one will stand as your oath-helper at the Thing.
- You **may be killed without weregild** ⚠️ — the law's extremity, presented as the world believes it. The GM should use this as **threat, rarely as event**; a killing that actually happens this way starts a feud (§XXI) with *someone*, because even a níðingr has kin or the killing was unjust.
- You **cannot raise Hamingja above 3** while níðingr — luck will not follow the shamed. *(Addition to the codex: existing Hamingja above 3 is not stripped, but it cannot be regained once spent below the cap.)*
- **Redemption is possible but costly:** the table must witness the deed that washes you, and the GM sets the price in blood, silver, or poetry. *(Addition to the codex: a single great deed removes **one** mark — the níðingr climbs out one mark at a time, never all at once. Holmgang vindication against the accuser removes the mark born of that accusation specifically.)*

**Raising the níðstöng** (a PC-declared act, **never casual**): requires a **horse's head** (or carved likeness), a **pole**, and a **spoken/carved níð-verse** — the player must speak at least a couplet aloud at the table (flyting rules, §X). Effect: the target loses **2 Orðstír immediately among all who hear of it**, and the **landvættir turn** — the target suffers **−1 Hamingja until the pole is answered or avenged** [Eg].
- **Answering the pole *(addition to the codex)*:** the target (or their kin) may answer by (a) holmgang challenge against the raiser, (b) a counter-verse spoken before equal witnesses, or (c) cutting the pole down and raising their own — each clears the Hamingja penalty once done *publicly*. Until answered, the penalty stands, and the land itself is cold to the target (the GM should show it: game scarce, weather sour).
- **Raising a pole falsely** (against one who has not wronged you) earns the **raiser 2 níð-marks** — the community judges the cause, and the GM should let the table argue it.
- *(Addition to the codex:)* A níðstöng raised against a **níðingr** is wasted breath — the socially dead cannot be shamed further; the pole earns the raiser nothing and the table's contempt for the cruelty.

#### At an OSE Table

Each níð-mark: **−2 per mark on reaction rolls** with those who know (instead of disadvantage on Persuasion/Deception). Níðingr (3 marks): oath worthless, killable without weregild, Hamingja capped at 3 — plus **retainers abandon you and hirelings cost double**, if any will take your coin at all. The níðstöng works identically: −2 Orðstír among all who hear of it, −1 Hamingja until answered or avenged; a false pole earns the raiser 2 marks. Shame is system-neutral.

#### Worked Example

During a feast, Skapti publicly accuses Halfdan of fleeing the shield-wall at Hafrsfjord — naming the act, before witnesses: a formal ergi-adjacent charge. Halfdan laughs it off and changes the subject. The GM: *"Silence before witnesses — a níð-mark."* Halfdan now has 1 mark: −2 on reaction rolls with all who heard (OSE) or disadvantage on Persuasion/Deception with them (5E). Two sessions later he is caught stealing from a guest's pack — stealthy theft: a second mark. The table can see where this road ends, and so can he. His only clean exits: a great witnessed deed, or holmgang against Skapti. *(Illustrative sequence; real dice land where they land.)*

### III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

Players learn that in this culture, **words are weapons with legal standing**. The threat of the pole disciplines behavior more than any alignment system — because shame here is not a feeling, it is a *status*, and statuses have teeth. Nobody at the table will leave an insult unanswered once they have watched a mark land; nobody will reach for the sneaking knife once they understand the difference the culture drew between the open raid and the hidden theft. The níðstöng scene — the player speaking their own curse-couplet aloud, the table going still — teaches more about Viking-Age speech-magic than any lore dump: language *did things*, and everyone in the hall knew it.

## Wyrd & Ørlög — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 6)

**Video:** `videos/ep06-wyrd.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep06-wyrd.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §III` — this file expands that section; additions the codex leaves open are labeled.

### I. Grounding — Why It Is True

The Norns are not a metaphor in the sources. In *Völuspá*, at the root of Yggdrasil: *"Thence come the maidens mighty in wisdom, / Three from the dwelling down 'neath the tree; / Urth is one named, Verthandi the next,— / On the wood they scored,— and Skuld the third. / Laws they made there, and life allotted / To the sons of men, and set their fates"* [PE: Völuspá 20]. Urðr (what has become), Verðandi (what is becoming), Skuld (what shall be) carve *ørlög* — literally "primal law" — laid down at birth. Snorri's Prose Edda keeps the same doctrine: the Norns come to every child born and shape its life [SnE]. Fate is not a script you read; it is a weave you are caught in.

The heroic response is not escape but *meeting*: *"One thing I know that never dies, / The fame of a dead man's deeds"* [Háv 78], and before it: *"Cattle die, and kinsmen die, / And so one dies one's self; / But a noble name will never die, / If good renown one gets"* [Háv 76–77]. Straw-death (dying in bed) was the dread; a good death was the crown. Óðinn himself bought his wisdom by paying a price — *"offered I was / To Othin, myself to myself"* [Háv 139] — which is why wyrd-debt can only be cleared by paying in kind: the cosmos keeps accounts, and the currency is deeds.

⚠️ *What the sources do not give us:* no saga describes a counted "wyrd-debt" or a formal Norn-reading rite at a set threshold. The Norse felt fate as atmosphere, not arithmetic. The counting is Volmarr's game machinery — honest homebrew built on an attested worldview, and labeled as such wherever it appears below.

**What is homebrew here:** the Norns, ørlög, and the honor-in-death ethic are attested. The *mechanics* — the birth rune drawn at creation, Wyrd-debt as a counted stat, the Norn's reading at 5 debt, the saga-death inheritance formula — are Volmarr's invention, built to make fate playable at the table.

- **Mocking a true dream or spá:** 1 (expansion — contempt for the gods' postal service, §XXIII–XXIV).

It is **invisible to all but seeresses** — a völva, seið-worker, or wise one may perceive another's Wyrd-debt with a Wisdom (Insight) check, DC 15 (expansion). Reading *your own* debt requires a spá or útiseta (§XXIV): the rite names the price, as rites do.

The GM may **spend** a PC's Wyrd-debt 1-for-1 to impose **disadvantage on one roll**, describing it as the world turning cold: a rope frays, a horse shies, a friend mishears (§II). Spent debt stays spent — it is a price, not a loan. The GM should spend openly (*"your wyrd-debt turns against you here"*) so the table learns the cause-and-effect, not secretly.

**What the debt feels like** (expansion — atmosphere the GM may use, never mechanics the players can game):

| Debt | The world around the debtor |
|---|---|
| 1–2 | Nothing any but a völva would notice. Perhaps the debtor's shadow seems a half-step slow. |
| 3–4 | Animals go quiet around them. Milk sours a day early. Small things go missing and turn up broken. |
| 5+ | The Norn's reading has been spoken — the air before a storm. Children stare. Old folk make the warding sign. |

**Debt and the kin-line** (expansion): Wyrd-debt is personal — it does not pass to heirs. But a PC who dies with debt *unmet and unconfessed* leaves a stain the table remembers: their heir begins with the birth rune **Hagalaz** (hail from a clear sky) at the GM's discretion. The Norns keep long books.

**The Norn's reading.** At **5 Wyrd-debt**, the GM performs a reading, openly at the table: **three runes drawn** and interpreted as a **doom the PC cannot dodge — only meet well**. Example: *"Thurisaz — the thorn. Before winter's end you will face a foe you cannot defeat. How you face him is yours."* The doom is binding in *price*, never in *choice*: the GM may never use it to force a decision, only to name what the decision will cost. The doom must be *specific enough to recognize, open enough to meet freely* — name the shape (a foe, a storm, a choice between kin and oath), never the move.

- **Fleeing the doom** counts as cowardice before witnesses (−1 Orðstír, §I), and the debt remains. The Norns are patient.
- **Meeting it** — facing it openly, whatever the outcome — clears **all** Wyrd-debt. If the doom is met and survived with witnesses watching: **+2 Orðstír** (a deed of saga-weight, expansion). If the PC dies to it facing the foe: it counts as a **saga-death** automatically, no further test.
- **A second reading** (expansion): if debt climbs to 5 again after a reading was met, the Norns read again — but the second doom is *heavier*: the GM draws three runes and the doom now names a *kin-member or sworn companion* as part of its price. Fate escalates with those who will not learn.

**The reading, spoken aloud** (expansion — example wordings; the GM should speak these slowly, laying the runes as they speak):
- *"Ansuz, Nauthiz, Hagalaz. A word you speak this winter will bind you. Need will teach you what the word cost. And hail comes from a clear sky — the ruin will not be your fault, but the word will be."*
- *"Tiwaz, Isa, Perthro. Justice frozen: a judgment you must give, and give rightly, though it cost you. The cup is shaken — chance will have its say in how it lands."*
- *"Eihwaz, Laguz, Othala. The narrow place is water: you will cross it, or your kin will lose what your fathers kept. Endure. Water finds its way; so must you."*
The pattern: two runes name the *shape*, the third names the *price*. Never name the choice.

**Clearing debt otherwise:** a great public deed (GM's call, witnessed) or a blót of real cost (a *great* offering or better, §IV) clears all Wyrd-debt. Private atonement clears nothing — the Norns are not fooled by secrecy. **Spá and útiseta do not clear debt** — they only *name* it (§XXIV); knowing the shape of doom binds you tighter (+1 Wyrd-debt for the asking, as the codex already rules).

**Saga-death.** A PC who dies **in battle, keeping oaths, protecting kin — weapon in hand, facing the foe** — earns a saga-death:
1. The table composes, or the GM delivers, a **short verse** (at least four lines) of their deeds. The verse should be *speakable* — kennings encouraged, plain words allowed.
2. **Half their Orðstír (rounded down)** passes to their **named heir or sworn companion** as starting Orðstír (§I).
3. The player's **next character begins with Inspiration and +2 Orðstír**, "born under a bright name."

The heir must have been **named before death** — in a will spoken at sumbel, in a witnessed declaration, or in the death scene's last words (expansion). If no heir was named, the inheritance goes to the PC's *sworn companion* of longest standing (GM's call); if none exists, the verse is still spoken, but nothing passes — the line ends, and the table feels it. A PC who dies by cowardice, oathbreaking, or straw-death leaves nothing: no verse, no inheritance, and the table knows it.

**Dying well vs. dying usefully** (expansion): a saga-death requires *how*, not *why*. Dying to hold a door so kin escape qualifies; dying in a duel you provoked for vanity does not — the GM judges the *manner* (oaths kept, kin protected, foe faced), not the cause's wisdom. A glorious fool's death is still a saga-death if he died facing the foe with his oaths intact.

#### Quick Reference — Wyrd at a Glance

- **Birth rune:** drawn once at creation; GM invokes 1/session (advantage on a fitting roll, or a whispered warning).
- **Debt sources:** frith-breach +1 · failed boast +1 · mocked dream/spá +1 · refused warning +1 · coercive magic (per §V) · *eiðrofi* +3.
- **GM spends debt:** 1 point → disadvantage on one roll (5E) / +2 harder on roll-under (OSE), spent openly.
- **At 5 debt:** the Norn's reading — three runes, a doom to meet, never a choice to force.
- **Clearing:** meet the doom · great public deed · costly blót (great+ offering, petition names the clearing).
- **Saga-death:** verse spoken · half Orðstír to the *named* heir · next character Inspiration + +2 Orðstír (OSE: one free reroll).

#### At an OSE Table

- **Birth-rune invocation:** **−2 on a roll-under check** (instead of advantage) on one fitting roll per session, or the whispered warning (identical). Refusing a true warning still gains 1 Wyrd-debt.
- **Perceiving debt:** a völva or wise one rolls **1d20 ≤ Wisdom − 2** to sense another's Wyrd-debt.
- **Spending debt:** the GM spends 1 Wyrd-debt for **+2 on a roll-under check** (harder) against the PC — the world turning cold either system.
- **Saga-death:** your next character begins with **one free reroll** (instead of Inspiration) and +2 Orðstír. The verse, the half-inheritance, the heir-naming rule, and the second-reading escalation are identical.
- **Everything else is identical** — wyrd is wyrd in any century.

#### Worked Example

*Illustrative only — hypothetical rolls.* Brandr the fighter (Wyrd-debt 4) breaks hearth-frith in a tavern brawl, striking a man under a host's roof. That is his fifth debt point. The GM lays three runes on the table: *Hagalaz, Nauthiz, Tiwaz.* "Hail from a clear sky," the GM says. "Before the sea opens in spring, a ruin you did not earn will find you — and a hard choice will follow it. Justice will cost you."

In the next session, Brandr's player asks for advantage on a Persuasion roll to talk down a jarl; the GM spends 1 Wyrd-debt and imposes disadvantage instead — *"your wyrd-debt turns against you here"* — describing the jarl's hound growling at nothing behind Brandr. The roll fails. The table has learned the cause-and-effect.

In spring, a rival burns Brandr's boathouse. Brandr could flee to the hills (cowardice: −1 Orðstír, debt stays) — instead he walks into the Thing, names the arsonist, and demands holmgang (§XI). Win or lose, the doom is *met*: all debt cleared, and if he falls facing the foe with his oaths intact, the table speaks his verse and his heir takes half his honor. Wyrd named the price. Brandr chose how to pay it.

### III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

Players stop fearing character death and start fearing *meaningless* death — the exact inversion of modern play instincts. The birth rune teaches them that the world has opinions about them from the first session; the Norn's reading teaches that a doom *named* is a doom that can be *met* — which is the entire Norse heroic ethic compressed into one mechanic; and the saga-death teaches the oldest lesson of the North: what dies is the body, what lives is the name — but only if the table watched you earn it. The GM's discipline — naming prices, never forcing choices — is the craft lesson underneath: fate as a teacher, not a railroad.

## Blót — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 7)

**Video:** `videos/ep07-blot.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep07-blot.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §IV` — this file expands that section; additions the codex leaves open are labeled.

### I. Grounding — Why It Is True

Our template is the saga account of the great sacrifice at Hlader, and it is worth reading whole, because every mechanic below comes out of it [Heim ch.16]: the bondes gather at the temple with ale and cattle; all blood is called *hlaut*, collected in hlaut-vessels, and sprinkled on altars, temple walls, and *the people themselves* with hlaut-staves; the flesh is boiled into savory meat for those present; the fire burns in the middle of the temple floor, and the full goblets are handed across it — first **Óðinn's goblet** for victory and power, then **Njörðr's and Freyja's** for peace and a good season, then the **braga-goblet** over which vows were sworn, then the remembrance goblet for dead friends. The chief who made the feast *blessed* the goblets and the meat. Sigurd the earl paid the whole festival's expenses out of his own hand, "which was very much celebrated" — open-handedness at the blót was itself renown.

The logic of the rite is older than the sagas: *"I ween that I hung on the windy tree… With the spear I was wounded, and offered I was / To Othin, myself to myself"* [Háv 139–141]. Even the Allfather paid to receive. *Do ut des* — I give so that you give — but the gods answer only in signs: ravens circling widdershins, a sudden fair wind, ale souring in the cup. They never appear. Canon law holds: gods act only through omens, dreams, weather.

**What is homebrew here:** the blót's structure (proper place, real offering, witnesses, goblets in order, the braga-goblet as oath-venue) is attested [Heim ch.16]. ⚠️ The three great seasonal blóts (Yule, Dísablót in spring, Sigrblót before the raiding season) follow the saga pattern, but exact historical details of ritual are debated — the *structure* (gather, sacrifice, feast, vow) is well attested, the calendar specifics are tradition, not fact. The omen table, the offering tiers, the god-specific petitions, and the boast-boldness numbers are Volmarr's (or the expansion's) game machinery.

### II. The Complete Playable Rule

#### At a 5E Table

**Requirements.** A blót needs three things, all non-negotiable: (1) a **proper place** — a hof, a grove, a vé, or a burial mound; a kitchen table in a rented room is not a blót-stead; (2) an **offering of real value** — livestock, silver, a crafted thing — recorded in silver weight; (3) **witnesses**. The *goði* or eldest present leads. A blót with no witnesses is not a blót — it is littering with extra steps (expansion: the community must *see* the giving, because reciprocity is public).

**The blót-stead** (expansion — the proper places, and what each lends the rite):
- **The hof (temple):** the full liturgy — fire in the middle of the floor, kettles hung, goblets handed across the fire [Heim ch.16]. The hof's blót is the district's blót; its omen speaks for the district.
- **The grove:** older than any building. A grove-blót is quieter, older-feeling — the GM should play it with fewer words and more weather. The landvættir are nearer here (§XXV).
- **The vé (sanctuary):** marked holy ground. Violence within the vé during the rite is the gravest frith-breach the table knows (§II) — the blót does not pause for it; the blót *witnesses* it.
- **The burial mound:** the dead are present. The remembrance goblet here carries double weight: name the mound's dead truly and gain +1 Hamingja as written — the ancestors are listening closer (§XXV).

**Offering tiers** (expansion; silver weight recorded on the character sheet):

| Offering | Silver weight | Omen roll |
|---|---|---|
| **Meager** — a token, a cup of ale, words only | under ½ oz | **disadvantage** (the gods notice stinginess) |
| **Adequate** — a good beast's share, honest silver | ½–4 oz | straight roll |
| **Great** — a full ox, a horse, a crafted weapon, a king's gift | 4+ oz | **advantage** |
| **Kingly** — Sigurd-of-Hlader scale: the host pays *everything* | 20+ oz or a horse + feast | advantage, and the host gains +1 Orðstír for open-handedness (§I) |

**Hosting economics** (expansion):
- The host pays for the feast *and* the offering — this is why Sigurd's generosity was "very much celebrated" [Heim ch.16]. A blót is wealth *converted* into standing.
- Co-hosting splits the cost and shares the +1 Orðstír (great blóts only; lesser blóts grant no Orðstír for hosting, only the omen).
- A goði who leads the rite well (Charisma/Performance check, DC 13, played with real spoken words) grants the *omen roll* advantage once per blót even on an adequate offering — the gods listen to a good speaker (expansion).
- A blót performed *for* you by a hired goði works mechanically, but earns no Orðstír: the community saw your silver, not your hands.

**The rite, in order** (expansion of the saga sequence — the table plays it, not handwaves it):
1. **The gathering & the hlaut** — the offering is given; blood (or its stand-in: ale poured out, if the table prefers) is sprinkled on altar and people with the hlaut-staves. Every witness is marked. No one watches from the doorway.
2. **The feast** — the meat is eaten *by those present*. No doggy-bags for the gods; the shared meal *is* the communion. A guest who will not eat with the company insults the rite (−1 Orðstír, expansion).
3. **The goblets, in order** — Óðinn's (victory and power), Njörðr's and Freyja's (peace and good seasons), then the **braga-goblet**, then the remembrance goblet for dead friends. Each is blessed by the leader and drunk in turn. **Refusing the cup is an insult** (−1 Orðstír before witnesses, §IV).
4. **The braga-goblet round** — oaths and boasts are sworn aloud (§IX). A **boast** is rated in **boldness 1–5** when sworn (GM's call, announced: *"boldness 3"*). If the boast fails, the boaster loses Orðstír **equal to its boldness**. The GM writes every braga-goblet vow down. The table will hold you to them.
5. **The remembrance goblet** — drunk to dead friends by name (expansion). Naming the dead at the blót is ancestor maintenance (§XXV): a PC who drinks the remembrance goblet to a named ancestor and speaks one true thing about them gains **+1 Hamingja** until the next seasonal turn. The dead lend their luck to those who remember them.

**Petitioning by goblet** (expansion): the first three goblets are not decoration — each names the blót's *intent*, and the omen roll is read against it. Óðinn's goblet petitions victory and power (blóts before battle, holmgang, or perilous undertakings); Njörðr's and Freyja's petition peace and good seasons (blóts for harvest, sailing, household fortune). A blót that petitions Óðinn and rolls an ill omen (1–7) means the undertaking is *ill-starred*: the GM may spend it as one free disadvantage on a roll during that undertaking, the way Wyrd-debt is spent (§III). The gods' answer has teeth.

**The omen roll.** After the rite, the GM rolls **d20 on the omen table** below (codex's `omens.json`, expanded here). Results are *never* a god appearing. The GM interprets in-world; the players interpret at the table. A great offering grants advantage; a stingy one, disadvantage. The GM **never explains** what the omen means — that is the players' work, and arguing about it at the table is the intended play.

| d20 | The sign |
|---|---|
| 1 | Ale sours in every cup at once. Something is wrong with this plan. |
| 2 | Ravens circle **widdershins** over the stead — against the sun's road. |
| 3 | A sudden cold wind kills the fire; it will not relight till the rite is done again. |
| 4 | A dream comes to the youngest present: drowning water, a black sail. |
| 5 | The hlaut will not take — it beads and runs off the altar stone. |
| 6 | A horse shies at nothing on the road home. |
| 7 | The feast-meat is tough and joyless; laughter dies early. |
| 8 | Clouds stack like shields in the direction of your intent. |
| 9 | A raven lands on the hof roof and watches the whole rite in silence. |
| 10 | The wind drops to nothing; the smoke rises straight as a spear. |
| 11 | A stranger's dog follows the party home and will not leave — a small, strange luck. |
| 12 | The ale foams high and sweet; the cups empty laughing. |
| 13 | Ravens circle **sunwise**, calling three times. |
| 14 | A fair wind rises at dawn, straight from the gods' quarter. |
| 15 | The fire burns blue-green for a breath, then gold again. |
| 16 | An old ewe bears twins in the night after the blót. |
| 17 | A white hart — or its track — crosses your path within three days. |
| 18 | The remembrance goblet tastes of honey though none was added. |
| 19 | Two ravens land on the boundary stones and face outward, on guard. |
| 20 | The sun breaks through storm-cloud at the rite's end and strikes the altar. The season will remember this. |

(1–7: ill-omened; 8–13: ambiguous — the gods are listening but undecided; 14–20: favored.)

**Omens in campaign play** (expansion):
- Record every omen and its interpretation *in the campaign log*, with the date. The table's later arguments about what the ravens meant are the intended play — and the GM should occasionally prove an old reading right.
- An ill omen (1–7) is not a veto: it is a *warning*. Sailing anyway is allowed — and very Norse. The warning just means the GM is entitled to spend it.
- Omens are *never* retroactively explained by the GM. If the players misread the signs, the misreading stands. That is also very Norse.
- A natural 20 on the omen roll is a season-defining sign: let its effects echo for the whole season (fair winds, good hunting, the district's mood lifted).
- A natural 1 is the opposite: name it at the next Thing. The district will want to know why the ale soured.

**Boasts and vows — edge cases** (expansion):
- A boast sworn **drunk** is still binding. The ale does not excuse the mouth (§IX: the GM holds the player to the *letter*).
- A boast that proves **impossible** (the ransom fleet sank; the king is dead) is not *failed* — it is *released*, but only if the boaster speaks the release before the same witnesses and pays 1 Orðstír for the presumption. Silence keeps the debt.
- A **vow sworn over the braga-goblet** is an oath in full (§IX): while pursuing it, advantage on one check per day; breaking it is *eiðrofi*.

**What the GM writes down** (expansion — the braga-goblet ledger):
- The *exact words* of every oath and boast. Vague oaths bind vaguely (§IX) — the GM holds the player to the letter.
- The boldness rating (1–5) of every boast, announced at the swearing.
- The witnesses present. A vow with no witnesses is not a vow — it is a wish.
- The season and the blót. Old vows resurface: the GM should re-read the ledger before each seasonal turn.

**The three great blóts.** Yule (midwinter), Dísablót (spring), Sigrblót (before the summer sailing) ⚠️. A PC who **hosts or co-hosts** one gains +1 Orðstír (witnessed generosity, §I); a PC who pointedly *refuses* to attend their district's blót without cause loses 1 Orðstír (the community notices absence the way it notices a closed door). **Skipping all three in a year** without cause (outlawry, exile, war) costs 1 Hamingja as well — the luck-line thins for those who never feed it (expansion).

| Blót | Season | Petition | The saga pattern |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Yule** | Midwinter | Peace, the year's turning, the dead remembered | The great midwinter feast; oaths sworn for the coming year |
| **Dísablót** | Spring | Fertility, the dísir, good seasons | Held as the fields wake; for peace and growth |
| **Sigrblót** | Before the summer sailing | Victory and power (Óðinn's goblet first) | Sacrifice for success in the summer's undertakings |

**A blót of real cost** clears all Wyrd-debt (§III) — but only if the offering was *great* or better, and only if the blót's petition *names* the clearing ("we give that the debt be lifted"). The Norns do not accept coupons, and they do not accept vague ones.

**Blót at a burial mound** (expansion): a blót held at a mound, with the remembrance goblet drunk to the mound's dead by name, counts as ancestor maintenance for the season (§XXV) — the two rites fold into one, as they did.

**When the blót goes wrong** (expansion):
- If the rite is interrupted by violence, the blót is *void* — the offering is wasted, the omen unrolled, and the interrupter has committed frith-breach in a vé (grave: −2 Orðstír, +1 Wyrd-debt, §II).
- If the goði misspeaks the blessings or skips a goblet, the eldest witness may *correct* the rite aloud — the blót proceeds, but the goði loses 1 Orðstír for sloppiness before the gods. The gods notice liturgy.
- If no goði or elder is present, the eldest *free person* present leads — necessity consecrates. A blót led by a child, with no adult present, is not a blót: the gods are not mocked.
- An omen of 1 (ale sours in every cup) during a *great* blót is a district event: the GM should have neighboring steadings hear of it within the week. Ill omens travel faster than good ones.

#### At an OSE Table

- **Omen roll:** a great offering lets you **roll twice and take the better omen**; a stingy one, **roll twice and take the worse**. (Roll the d20 twice; the GM picks which result stands.)
- **Remembrance goblet:** +1 Hamingja as written — Hamingja is system-neutral.
- **Petitioning:** an ill-starred Óðinn-blót gives the GM one free **+2 (harder) on a roll-under check** during the petitioned undertaking.
- **Everything else is identical** — the gods take silver in any century.

#### Worked Example

*Illustrative only — hypothetical rolls.* Before sailing for the summer, the party holds a Sigrblót at a grove, petitioning Óðinn's goblet for victory on the voyage. They offer a young ox and 6 ounces of silver — a **great** offering. The rite is played in order; over the braga-goblet, Astrid boasts she will bring home a king's ransom from the west — boldness 4, written down by the GM. Over the remembrance goblet, the party's eldest names her grandfather and speaks one true thing about him: +1 Hamingja until the seasonal turn.

Afterward the GM rolls the omen d20 twice (great offering): the two rolls are 4 and 15. The better stands: **15 — the fire burns blue-green for a breath, then gold again.** The table argues about it for ten minutes — exactly as intended — and reads it as favor.

Astrid's boast now hangs over the whole voyage: if she returns empty-handed, she loses 4 Orðstír before every witness who heard her swear. If the fleet is lost to storm and the boast becomes *impossible*, she may speak its release before the same witnesses for 1 Orðstír — but only if she speaks. Silence keeps the debt.

### III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

Players learn *reciprocity as cosmology*: you do not pray for favors, you *exchange* with powers greater than you — and the exchange has a price list, recorded in silver weight. The omen table trains them to read the world as alive: ravens, wind, and sour ale become information, not set dressing. The braga-goblet teaches the oldest lesson at the feast — never let the ale write checks your sword has to cash — and the remembrance goblet teaches the quietest one: the dead are still at the table, and they notice who remembers.

## Holmgang — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 8)

**Video:** `videos/ep08-holmgang.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep08-holmgang.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §XI` — this file expands that section; additions the codex leaves open are labeled.

### I. Grounding — Why It Is True

*Hólmganga* — "island-going" — was the formal duel of the Norse world, and the sagas treat it as *law's sharp edge*: regulated killing with liturgy. In *Egils saga*, the duel with Ljot the Pale is staged with ritual precision: *"a fair plain near the sea, which was to be the place of combat. The ground was marked out by stones lying round in a ring"* [Eg ch.67]. Before the fighting, Ljot *"declared the law of combat, that he should ever after bear the name of dastard who should draw back outside the boundary stones"* [Eg ch.67] — the penalty for breaking the ring's law was a *name*, which in this culture was worse than a wound. Note the sequence the saga insists on: the ground is marked, the law is *declared aloud*, and only then do they close. Liturgy first, violence second.

Challenges were issued for insults, for women, for *lands and inheritances* — Ljot the Pale "amassed him wealth by duels," challenging landowners for their estates [Eg]. This is the crucial point the mechanics exist to teach: the duel was not barbarism escaping law, it was law *containing* violence, so that feuds could end without burning the district down. A district that watched two men settle a land-claim in the ring did not have to watch their kin burn each other's halls for a decade (§XX).

⚠️ **Tradition, not fact:** the hazel-pole ring and the **three-shield custom** (each duelist bringing three shields) come from later saga tradition; the historical record for the details is thin. The transcript and codex both flag this. The GM may simplify to a stone ring and one shield — the *law* of the duel is what matters, not the props.

**What is homebrew here:** the ring, the witnessed challenge, the stakes, and the "no weregild for the lawfully slain" principle are saga-attested. The numbers — −3 Orðstír for declining, +2 for a just victory, the two ritual constraints as game rules — are Volmarr's machinery. The first-blood/yielding definitions, the mercy rule, the champion rule, and the unlawful-challenge procedure below are the expansion's.

### II. The Complete Playable Rule

#### At a 5E Table

**1. Challenge & stakes (before witnesses, always).** The challenger **names the grievance and the stakes** aloud: weregild, land, a public apology, a woman's hand — all valid saga stakes (§XI). Vague challenges are void: *"I challenge you for... reasons"* is not a challenge, it is noise, and the would-be challenged may laugh it off without penalty (expansion). The stakes are sworn like an oath (§IX): the GM records them, and breaking the sworn terms later is **oathbreaking** (*eiðrofi*). The challenged has **three days** to answer unless the insult demands immediacy (GM's call; níð-born challenges demand answer *now* — the sun is the deadline, §X).

**Who may challenge** (expansion): any free person may issue holmgang — karl or jarl, man or woman. But the challenged may demand the challenger *prove standing*: a thrall, an outlaw, or a níðingr has no standing to issue a lawful duel, and their "challenge" may be answered with contempt, not steel. A challenge between gross unequals (a jarl challenging a landless karl for his *life*) may be judged *unlawful* at the Thing afterward — the ring is for settling, not for hunting.

**Declining.** The challenged may decline — at the cost of **3 Orðstír** before every witness (cowardice witnessed, §I). Exception: if the challenge is **níð-born** (issued to answer níð, or carrying an ergi-accusation), declining is **social death**: the decliner takes 1 níð-mark immediately and loses 3 Orðstír — the equivalent of letting níð go unanswered by dusk (§X). There is no clean way to refuse that fight.

**Answering by champion** (expansion): the challenged may name a champion to fight in their place — but only with the *challenger's* witnessed consent, and the champion fights for the challenged's stakes. Hiring a champion without consent is cowardice (−3 Orðstír as declining); a champion who loses costs their *principal* the stakes, not their own honor — though a champion who wins gains +1 Orðstír for the deed.

**The champion's risk** (expansion):
- A champion who *dies* in the ring dies lawfully — no weregild owed, rites owed by the victor's side.
- A champion may not be compelled: fighting another's duel under threat or debt-bondage voids the duel's lawfulness.
- No one may champion a níðingr's cause — the ring does not lend its law to the honorless.

**2. The ground.** A ring marked with stones (or hazel poles ⚠️); seconds and witnesses stand outside. The ground is **consecrated** by the declaration of the law of combat — spoken aloud by the challenger or a witness, in the saga's own formula: *"he shall ever after bear the name of dastard who draws back outside the boundary stones"* [Eg ch.67]. A duel fought without the declaration is a brawl with stakes, not holmgang: its killing *is* owed weregild (expansion).

**Einvigi — the lesser duel** (expansion): the sagas also know *einvigi* — single combat *without* the holmgang liturgy: no consecrated ring, no spoken declaration, no sworn end-condition. Einvigi settles nothing legally; its killing is owed weregild like any other killing, and its victor gains no +2 Orðstír. If the table wants a duel without law, this is what they are choosing — and the Thing will treat it as what it is.

**The ring's edge** (expansion):
- The dastard-name falls on whoever *leaves* the ring before the sworn end — drawn back outside the boundary stones [Eg ch.67]. Leaving means crossing the stones *under your own will*: fleeing, flinching away, stepping out to catch breath.
- Being *thrown* out does not count — a duelist hurled past the stones by a blow may re-enter without shame, and the witnesses will say so. The law punishes cowardice, not physics.
- A duelist *driven* out who refuses to re-enter has left, whatever their feet claim. The witnesses decide, and they are not fools.
- In a duel sworn to death, there is no leaving — only winning, yielding (if the terms allow it), or dying. The ring is the world, until it ends.

**Duels at the Thing** (expansion):
- Holmgang may be fought *at* the Thing, before the assembly — the greatest audience the law allows, and the most binding witnesses.
- But weapons are peace-bound at the Thing (*þingfriðr*, §II): the duel's ring is *consecrated ground within* the peace, declared by the law-speaker. Fighting outside the declared ring, at the Thing, is a grave frith-breach no duel-law covers.
- A duel fought at the Thing and judged lawful is *unappealable*: the assembly watched, the assembly's peace held it, and no kin may reopen it. This is why the ambitious choose the Thing's field.

**The seconds' oath** (expansion):
- Before the arming, each second swears aloud to keep the ring's law: no interference, no warnings, no weapons passed mid-fight. The oath is witnessed — breaking it is oathbreaking (§IX), not mere meddling.
- A second's oath binds them *after* the duel too: a second who takes up the loser's quarrel and starts a new fight on the field has broken the ring's peace and committed a frith-breach of their own.
- Seconds are traditionally *kin or sworn friends* of their duelist — which is why the oath matters: the people most tempted to interfere are the ones who swear not to.

Each duelist traditionally brings **three shields** ⚠️ — if the table uses the custom, each shield is a real shield that can be sundered by a called blow (the attacker declares *"I sunder his shield"* before rolling; on a hit, the shield breaks instead of wounding — expansion). A duelist whose third shield breaks fights on without one. Seconds may carry spare weapons and messages, and nothing else: they do not coach, warn, or intervene.

**Weapons and armor in the ring** (expansion):
- What the sagas show is shield and sword [Eg ch.67] — but the law does not mandate the weapons. The duelists fight with what they bring, shown openly at the arming.
- Armor is allowed unless the sworn terms forbid it. A duelist who demands the foe fight unarmored while armored himself names an *unlawful* term — the challenged may refuse that term without refusing the duel.
- Missile weapons have no place in the ring: the duel is face-to-face by definition. A bow brought to holmgang is a confession that you mean murder, not law.
- A duelist who *loses* their weapon in the fight may yield, flee the ring (and take the dastard-name), or fight on bare-handed — the law does not pause for dropped steel. A second who shouts a warning has interfered (see below).

**The duel day, beat by beat** (expansion — the liturgy, in order):1. **The gathering.** Both parties come with seconds and witnesses. The ground is already marked.
2. **The naming.** The challenger names grievance and stakes aloud, before all witnesses.
3. **The declaration.** The law of combat is spoken over the ring — the dastard-formula [Eg ch.67]. Without this, it is not holmgang.
4. **The swearing.** The end-condition is named and sworn: first blood, yielding, or death. The GM records it.
5. **The arming.** Shields (three, if the custom is used ⚠️), weapons shown openly. Hidden weapons are níð-work — discovery voids the duel and marks the hider.
6. **The fight.** Normal combat under the two ritual constraints.
7. **The ending.** The instant the sworn condition is met, all fighting stops — mid-swing. The witnesses *say so aloud*.
8. **The reckoning.** Stakes transferred before witnesses; the victor's +2 Orðstír announced; the slain's rites arranged.

**3. The fight.** Run as **normal 5E combat**, with two ritual constraints:
- **No interference.** No ally may enter the ring, cast at a duelist, strike or aid from outside, or shout warnings. Interference is a **grave frith-breach** (§II: −2 Orðstír, +1 Wyrd-debt, a vengeance claim for the wronged side) and **voids the duel** — its result is legally nothing, the stakes are undecided, and any killing is murder.
- **The duel ends as sworn.** Before the first blow, the condition is named: **first blood** (the *first hit that deals damage* ends it instantly — the wounded duelist yields the stakes; a blow that deals no damage does not count), **yielding** (either duelist may drop their weapon and ask quarter; quarter asked must be *given* — refusing it breaks the sworn terms), or **death**. Breaking the sworn terms mid-duel is oathbreaking.

**Simultaneous falls** (expansion): if both duelists fall in the same exchange and the duel was sworn to first blood, the *first wound in the exchange* decides — the GM tracks order strictly in that round. If truly simultaneous (area effect, mutual strike), the duel is *undecided* and must be re-sworn or taken to the Thing.

**The mercy rule** (expansion): in a duel sworn to *death*, the victor may grant life to the beaten foe. The spared duelist still loses the stakes, **plus** owes the victor a life-debt (a debt-token of the highest order, §VI): they must answer one honorable call from the victor, or lose 2 Orðstír. Granting mercy in a death-duel is itself honorable (+1 Orðstír before witnesses) — restraint inside the ritual is worth more than the kill.

**4. Aftermath.** The victor gains **+2 Orðstír for a just duel** (lawful challenge, sworn terms kept, no interference, declaration spoken). The slain is owed **no weregild if the duel was lawful** — the ring consecrated the killing. An **unlawful killing in the ring** (interference, broken terms, striking a yielded foe, no declaration spoken) is **murder**, and the Thing will hear it (§VII) — with the witnesses' testimony carrying the case. The victor in an *unjust* duel gains no Orðstír and loses 2 instead (expansion: the ring does not wash what the law condemns).

**The witnesses' duties** (expansion):
- Witnesses stand outside the ring and *watch* — their testimony is what makes the duel lawful. A duel with no witnesses is a killing.
- A witness who sees interference must *name it aloud*, at once. Silent witnesses to a voided duel share the shame (−1 Orðstír each, GM's call).
- The witnesses confirm the ending: when first blood is drawn, they call the halt. If they disagree, the senior witness present decides.
- Witnesses are owed nothing but are honored: a victor who feasts the witnesses gains +1 Orðstír (witnessed generosity, §I).

**The slain duelist's rites** (expansion): a duelist lawfully slain is owed funeral rites by the *victor's* side — burning or mounding with their weapon, and the remembrance goblet at the next blót (§XVII, §IV). Denying rites to a lawfully slain foe is níð-adjacent (−1 Orðstír); the sagas remember who buried their enemies.

**Holmgang and the feud** (expansion):
- A lawful duel *settles* the named grievance: the loser's kin gain no blood-debt from the killing (§XX). The ring is where feuds go to die.
- An *unlawful* killing in the ring creates a blood-debt at **Burning** temperature immediately — the worst start a feud can have.
- Kin who reject a lawful duel's outcome and take vengeance anyway commit murder: the ring's settlement is law, and law rejected is feud reborn.
- A duel sworn to first blood that ends in accidental death (a weak duelist, a cruel cut) is still lawful — the ring consecrated the risk. The victor owes the rites, not the weregild.

**The stakes, transferred** (expansion):
- Stakes change hands *before witnesses, on the field* — not later, not privately. The witnesses watch the transfer the way they watched the fight.
- Land staked and lost is recorded at the next Thing (§VII): the transfer is spoken before the assembly so no kin can dispute it later. An unrecorded land transfer breeds the next feud.
- A public apology staked and lost must be *spoken*, in the victor's words, before the same witnesses — a mumbled apology is no payment, and the victor may demand it again, properly.
- Stakes already lost cannot be re-staked: the ring settles, it does not loop. Demanding a rematch over settled stakes is unlawful challenge.

**Women at the ring** (expansion):
- The sagas show women *whetting* — demanding, shaming, and driving men to answer insults — more often than fighting themselves [Njál]. A woman may publicly *demand* that holmgang be fought over her grievance, naming the challenger; the demand carries the weight of níð if ignored.
- A woman's hand named as stakes requires her *kin's* witnessed consent — she is not a prize the ring can award without her ætt's word. Staking her without it makes the challenge unlawful.
- Nothing in the law forbids a woman fighting in the ring herself, and the sagas remember shield-maidens — but a woman who issues holmgang is judged by the same −3 for declining and the same terms. The ring does not soften for anyone; that is why its judgments hold.

**Practice bouts** (expansion):
- Blunted-weapon practice in the ring settles nothing and risks nothing: no Orðstír gained or lost, no stakes, no law. The table should *feel* the difference — practice is play; holmgang is liturgy.
- A "practice bout" where one party secretly sharpens their blade is attempted murder, and the Thing will hear it as such. The ring's trust is absolute or it is nothing.

**After the duel: feast or funeral** (expansion):- The custom is that both parties — victor, and the loser's kin if he fell — share a meal after. Refusing the meal continues the quarrel; accepting it seals the settlement.
- The victor pays for the feast. This is not generosity — it is the price of having killed lawfully and living with it.
- If the duel was sworn to first blood and both walk away, the feast is a *reconciliation*: both sides gain +1 Orðstír (witnessed, §I) on top of the victor's +2. The district watches enemies eat together, and the feud-starves.

**Unlawful challenge** (expansion): if the challenge itself is unlawful — issued by a níðingr, a thrall, or an outlaw; or over stakes the challenger has no right to name (another man's land, another man's wife) — the challenged may decline **without** the −3 Orðstír, but must say so *before witnesses*, naming the unlawfulness aloud: *"You have no standing to name my land."* The Thing may later judge the challenge's lawfulness; a challenger whose challenge is judged unlawful loses 2 Orðstír for abuse of the rite.

#### At an OSE Table

- The fight runs as **normal OSE combat** with the same two ritual constraints. First blood = the first wounding hit; yielding and quarter as written; the declaration of the law of combat spoken aloud.
- Declining: −3 Orðstír; níð-born decline = social death (1 níð-mark + −3 Orðstír). Victor: +2 Orðstír for a just duel; −2 for an unjust one. Lawful slain owed no weregild; unlawful killing = murder for the Thing.
- Shield-sundering, the mercy rule, champions, and the unlawful-challenge procedure are **identical**.
- **Everything else is identical** — the ring of stones holds in any century.

#### Worked Example

*Illustrative only — hypothetical rolls.* At a feast, Ketill publicly calls Einar's father an oath-breaker — níð, before witnesses. Einar demands holmgang for the insult, stakes: a public apology and Einar's father's name cleared. This is níð-born: neither can decline without social death. They swear *first blood*, and Einar's brother speaks the law of combat over the stone ring.

In the ring, Ketill lands the first wounding blow (a roll of 14 + 5 = 19 against Einar's AC 16 — a hit for 6 damage). The duel ends *instantly*, mid-swing — Einar's raised axe freezes, because the law has spoken. Ketill has won the stakes: Einar must publicly apologize for the *challenge*, though the insult to his father stands as Ketill spoke it — that is what the ring decided. Einar lost the duel but kept his honor: he answered níð with steel, as the law demands, and no one calls him dastard.

Had Ketill struck again after the first blood, that second blow would have been murder — the witnesses would carry it to the Thing, and Ketill's victory would turn to −2 Orðstír and a murder charge. The ring gives; the ring also watches.

### III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

Players learn that even killing had *liturgy* — and that the duel existed so feuds could end *without* burning the district down. The −3 for declining teaches that honor is not a feeling but a *price list*; the níð-born exception teaches that some words, once spoken, can only be answered in the ring; the declaration-before-combat teaches that law is *spoken* before it is *done*. And the mercy rule teaches the strangest lesson of all: that the ritual's highest prize was not the kill but the restraint — the victor who stays his hand proves he was never the weapon's servant.

## Hospitality — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 9)

**Video:** `videos/ep09-hospitality.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep09-hospitality.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §VIII` — this file expands that section; additions the codex leaves open are labeled.

### I. Grounding — Why It Is True

The first seven stanzas of *Hávamál* are not poetry — they are a **manual**, and the table should hear them as one [Háv 1–7]: look about before entering; *"Hail to the giver! a guest has come; / Where shall the stranger sit?"*; then the host's duties in plain words — *"Fire he needs who with frozen knees / Has come from the cold without; / Food and clothes must the farer have, / The man from the mountains come"*; *"Water and towels and welcoming speech / Should he find who comes to the feast."* The guest's conduct is legislated too: the wise guest sits silent, watches, listens, *"A man shall not boast of his keenness of mind, / But keep it close in his breast"* — and *"If renown he would get, and again be greeted, / Wisely and well must he act."* Note that last line: hospitality is *how renown is made*. The door is an engine of honor.

The time limit is set in stone as well: *"Forth shall one go, nor stay as a guest / In a single spot forever; / Love becomes loathing if long one sits / By the hearth in another's home"* [Háv 35]. In the saga world, *gestrísni* was sacred: harming a guest annihilated the host's honor utterly — the hearth was the innermost frith-circle (§II), and blood under a host's roof was a cosmic crime, not a private quarrel. The sagas are full of strangers at doors because winter made the rule load-bearing: in a land of ice and wilderness, refusing the traveler was a kind of killing.

**What is homebrew here:** the guest-rite's content (fire, food, seat), the three-day limit, the weapon-binding, and the sacredness of guest-right are all attested. The *numbers* — +1/−1 Orðstír, the grievance claim as a game object, the winter-refusal debt, the host's protection duty — are Volmarr's machinery (and the expansion's) for making the door a mechanic.

### II. The Complete Playable Rule

#### At a 5E Table

**The guest-rite (the host's duty).** When PCs arrive as guests, the GM runs the guest-rite — **roleplayed, not handwaved**. The host *must* offer, in order: **fire** (warmth, a place by the hearth — the frozen traveler is brought in from the cold *first*), **food and drink** (a real meal, not scraps; water and towels and welcoming speech for the feaster [Háv 4]), and **a seat** (a named place at the table — being seated last, or left standing, is a slight the table will notice).

**Seating is a language** (expansion):
- The seat *by the host* is honor; the seat *by the door* is tolerance; *no seat* is refusal.
- Moving a guest to a lower seat mid-feast is a public insult (−1 Orðstír to the host, and the guest gains a grievance claim).
- A guest who *takes* the high seat unbidden commits a breach of the rite — not frith-breach, but presumption (−1 Orðstír among the witnesses).

**How well was it performed?** (expansion — the GM's checklist, judged openly):
- Fire, food, seat all offered promptly and in order: **well performed** → +1 Orðstír (witnessed generosity, §I).
- Offered grudgingly, late, or piecemeal (the meal is scraps, the seat is a stool by the door): **poorly performed** → no gain, no loss, but the guests notice — the GM says so aloud.
- **Withheld** (no fire, no food, or the door barely opened): −1 Orðstír, and the guests gain a **grievance claim** — a formal, speakable claim they may press at the next Thing for apology and a gift, or carry as justification if the feud turns hot (§VII, §XX). The claim is public: the table hears it spoken.
- **Lavish** (meat, ale, gifts for the road, the high seat offered): +1 Orðstír **and** each guest owes a **debt-token of thanks** (expansion: gratitude is a debt, §VI — the host's generosity becomes an obligation the guest must answer).

**The host's protection** (expansion): the guest-rite is a *sanctuary*, and the host is its warden. If a third party threatens or attacks the guest under the host's roof, the host **must** defend the guest — standing aside is a hearth-frith breach by omission (−2 Orðstír, +1 Wyrd-debt, as §II). This is why hosts disarmed guests at the door: the peace of the hall was the *host's* honor on the line.

**Refused outright in winter** (expansion): turning a traveler from the door in winter weather is the act the sagas treat as near-monstrous. The refuser loses 1 Orðstír **and** gains 1 Wyrd-debt — the cold the traveler would have died in is laid at the refuser's wyrd. In fair seasons, refusal costs only the −1 Orðstír and the grievance claim.

**The guest's duties** (mechanical, not advisory):
1. **Do not overstay.** After **three days**, the host may name a departure day **without insult** — no Orðstír cost, no slight taken [Háv 35]. A guest who refuses the named day loses 1 Orðstír (churlishness witnessed) and the host may withdraw the guest-rite without penalty (expansion). A guest who *asks* to stay longer, and is granted it, owes a debt-token of thanks for the extension.
2. **Do not boast.** A boast under the host's roof triggers the **braga-goblet rule** (§IV): it is binding, rated in boldness, and failure costs Orðstír equal to its boldness [Háv 6]. The roof does not make the boast smaller.
3. **Weapons peace-bound.** Swords stay sheathed, spears stacked, axes hung — under the host's roof. **Drawing steel under a roof is a hearth-frith breach** (§II): −2 Orðstír, +1 Wyrd-debt, and a vengeance claim for the host — even if no blow lands. The *threat* breaks the circle. (The host may *require* weapons stacked at the door; refusing to yield them is refusing the guest-rite, and the host may refuse entry without penalty — expansion.)
4. **Do not steal, spy, or seduce under the roof** (expansion): any of these is a hearth-frith breach as above. The guest-rite is a sanctuary, not an opportunity. Discovery later is judged as harshly — the breach counts from the act, not the discovery.
5. **Gifts for the road** (expansion): a guest who departs without thanks — no word, no token, no spoken gratitude — loses 1 Orðstír among those who hear of it. *"If renown he would get, and again be greeted, wisely and well must he act"* [Háv 4].

**The grievance claim, step by step** (expansion):
1. **The speaking.** The wronged guest speaks the claim before witnesses, naming host, place, and the withheld rite. An unspoken claim is not a claim — honor exists only in the eyes of others (§I).
2. **The carrying.** The claim travels with the guest. It may be pressed at the next Thing (§VII) for a formal apology and a gift of real value.
3. **The pressing.** At the Thing, the claim is pleaded like any suit: oath-helpers, witnesses, the crowd's mood. A proven claim costs the host the apology, the gift, *and* 1 Orðstír publicly.
4. **The alternative.** The guest may instead declare the claim *settled* by accepting the host's later hospitality — the door reopens, the ledger closes. Forgiveness, witnessed, is also honor.

**Guest-right violation.** Harming a guest — or a guest harming the host — is a **hearth-frith breach**, the gravest circle (§II). The violator's name is **spoken at the next Thing** (the law-speaker names them before the assembly, §VII), and **no weregild can be refused** — meaning: whatever weregild the Thing sets, the violator *must* pay; they have no standing to haggle, counter-claim, or refuse. Refusal after sentencing is outlawry-ground (§VII). Additionally the violator gains **+1 Wyrd-debt** (§II) — the cosmos watched too.

**The wandering rule.** A PC traveling **alone in winter** may claim guest-right at **any steading** — and the steading must honor it. This is why the sagas are full of strangers at doors: survival *required* it, and the mechanics above are why hosts complied. A winter traveler who is *properly* received owes the host the guest's duties in full — including the three-day clock. (Traveling *with* an armed band in winter is not wandering — it is an arrival, and the steading may treat it as one: the guest-rite is for the traveler, not the troop. Expansion.)

**A model guest-rite scene** (expansion — beats for the GM, in order):
1. **The approach.** The traveler is seen coming. The host comes to the door — the rite begins at the threshold, not the table.
2. **The hailing.** "Hail to the giver! A guest has come" [Háv 2] — or the table's own words. Names are exchanged. A guest who will not give a name is owed the rite anyway — but watched.
3. **The fire.** The frozen traveler is brought to the hearth *first*, before questions. This is the detail that teaches.
4. **The seating.** A named place. The high seat is never the guest's; the seat of honor by the host is the mark of true welcome.
5. **The meal.** Food, drink, water, towels, welcoming speech [Háv 4]. The host eats *with* the guest — sharing the meal is sharing the peace.
6. **The news.** Guest's news for host's news — the exchange that makes the rite an institution, not a charity.
7. **The binding.** Weapons stacked, peace declared aloud: "You are under my roof's frith." The GM names the circle (§II) — the table should hear it.

**Hospitality between PCs** (expansion): when one PC hosts the others, the same rite runs — roleplayed, not handwaved. A PC host who performs it well gains the +1 Orðstír *from the table's witnessing* — honor exists only in the eyes of others (§I), and the table counts. A PC guest who violates guest-right against a PC host triggers the full breach: the name spoken at the next Thing, no weregild refused. The rules do not soften for friendship; that is the point.

**The host's table as intelligence** (expansion): the guest-rite is also how news travels — the host is *owed* the guest's news, and the guest is owed the host's. A guest who withholds vital news (a feud brewing, a raiding fleet sighted) and harm follows has broken the rite's spirit: −1 Orðstír when it comes out. The hearth is a sanctuary *and* a news-exchange; both are sacred.

**When the host is your enemy** (expansion):
- Guest-right does not check the feud ledger: a traveler may claim the rite even at a foe's steading, and the foe *must* honor it. The sagas turn on this hinge repeatedly.
- The host who honors the rite for an enemy gains **+2 Orðstír** instead of +1 — the table witnesses the harder generosity, and honor weighs difficulty (§I).
- Neither party may use the rite as a trap: violence planned *for* the guest's arrival (or the host's table) is hearth-frith breach with **+1 Wyrd-debt** on top of the breach costs — the cosmos punishes the perversion of sanctuary especially (§II, §III).
- The guest under a foe's roof is doubly bound: any breach of the guest's duties there is judged at the Thing with no sympathy and no mitigation.

**Hospitality and the circles of frith** (expansion):
- The hearth is the *innermost* circle (§II) — which is why its breach is the gravest. The guest-rite is frith *made personal*: the host extends their own circle around a stranger.
- A steading that keeps the guest-rite faithfully for a full year gains a reputation the GM should track: travelers seek it out, skalds praise it, and the host gains +1 Orðstír at each seasonal turn the district witnesses it (§XII). The door, kept open, becomes renown.
- Conversely, a steading *known* for withholding the rite finds fewer travelers, colder welcomes abroad, and −1 on reaction rolls (5E: disadvantage on Charisma checks) with strangers who have heard — the door, kept shut, becomes the story told about you.

**The guest-gift** (expansion):
- The host who sends the guest off with a *parting gift* — food for the road, a cloak, a token — creates a debt-token of thanks (§VI) even where none existed. Parting generosity is how the rite *reproduces itself*: the guest, now debtor, must one day host in turn.
- A guest who *refuses* the parting gift without a spoken reason insults the host (−1 Orðstír to the refuser, §VI: refusing a gift is itself an insult unless a reason is spoken before witnesses).
- The cheapest worthy parting gift is *food for three days* — exactly the length of the rite's clock [Háv 35]. The symmetry is the teaching: the host feeds the road the guest must walk.

**The vulnerable at the door** (expansion):
- The rite binds hardest where need is greatest: turning away children, the elderly, or the wounded in winter is *double* shame — −2 Orðstír and +1 Wyrd-debt. The sagas judge this the act of a níðingr-in-spirit, and the table should feel the weight.
- A host who takes in the vulnerable *beyond* the three days — fostering the orphan through winter, nursing the wounded traveler whole — gains +1 Orðstír at the seasonal turn, witnessed by the district (§XII). This is how reputations are built that outlive the builder.

**Hospitality at the Thing** (expansion):- During the Thing (§VII), the host-district owes the guest-rite to *all* attendees — the assembly is a gathering of travelers, and the law knows it. The þingvöllr has booths, fires, and shared meals for exactly this reason.
- Violence between guests at the Thing violates *both* guest-right and þingfriðr — the penalties stack: hearth-frith breach costs *plus* the Thing's own judgment. The law protects itself first (§VII), and its guests second, and both absolutely.

**The door-warden's questions** (expansion):
- The host may ask three things at the threshold, and custom expects them: *your name, your errand, your news*. A guest who answers all three plainly is owed the full rite without suspicion.
- The guest may *withhold* their errand — "my errand is my own" — and still be owed fire, food, and seat. But the host is then entitled to watchfulness: the withholding is noted, and the host's protection duty does not extend to consequences of the hidden errand.
- A guest who gives a *false* name has broken the rite's spirit from the first word: discovery is a hearth-frith breach by deception (as §II), and the host's vengeance claim is doubled in the telling — the sagas despise the liar at the door especially.

**Guest-right and outlaws** (expansion):
- An outlaw (*vargr*, §VII/§XXII) has no frith — and therefore no guest-right to claim. A steading may turn the known outlaw away without penalty; indeed, sheltering one *knowingly* shares the sentence.
- But a host who receives a traveler *not knowing* they are outlawed, and performs the rite in good faith, is **not** guilty of sheltering: the rite was given to a guest, and the guest's hidden outlawry is the guest's breach, not the host's. The host gains a vengeance claim against the deceiver.
- The outlaw who claims guest-right by *lying* about their sentence commits a double breach — against the rite and against the law's sentence — and the Thing, when it hears, will add it to the sentence's weight.

**The morning after** (expansion):
- Departure is a rite too: the host walks the guest to the boundary of the homefield — not the door, the *boundary*. The walk is the closing of the circle the arrival opened.
- At the boundary, host and guest exchange final words: thanks spoken, news wished well, the road blessed. A departure without the walk is not a breach — but the table should notice who bothers and who doesn't.
- A guest who departs *in anger* (over a slight, real or imagined) without speaking it leaves the grievance to fester: the GM should treat the unspoken slight as a claim that will surface at the worst time — because that is what unspoken slights do (§XX).

**The host's absence** (expansion):
- If the host is away, the *household* owes the rite: the húsfreyja, the eldest karl present, even the eldest child old enough to speak the welcome. The door does not get to be empty.
- A household member who performs the rite in the host's absence earns the +1 Orðstír *in the host's name* — the honor accrues to the steading, and the steading remembers who held its door.
- A traveler turned away with "the master is not home" has been *withheld* the rite: −1 Orðstír to the steading and a grievance claim, as written. Absence is not an excuse the law recognizes — the hearth does not go cold because the master is hunting.

**Strangers at the door — the GM's winter toolkit** (expansion):
- In winter, *every* isolated steading should get a knock at least once. The wandering rule is not background — it is an encounter generator the sagas used constantly.
- The stranger's *news* is the reward for opening the door: plot hooks arrive as travelers, because that is how news traveled.
- A stranger who refuses to give a name, keeps weapons close, or will not eat is a *story*, not a guest — the GM is signaling. The host who still performs the rite earns the +1 regardless.
- The table should *feel* the cost: in deep winter, every mouth at the fire is a mouth from the stores. The +1 Orðstír is the culture's answer to that arithmetic.

#### At an OSE Table

- **Identical.** The guest-rite is system-neutral: +1 Orðstír for a generous host (lavish: plus debt-tokens of thanks), −1 and a grievance claim for a withholding one; the three-day clock, the weapon-binding, the host's protection duty, and the hearth-frith breach as written. Winter refusal: −1 Orðstír and +1 Wyrd-debt either system.
- Full dual-system reference: `SYSTEM_VARIANTS.md`.

#### Worked Example

*Illustrative only — hypothetical rolls.* Caught by a blizzard on the fell road, the party staggers to an isolated steading at dusk. The farmer, Birgir, meets them at the door — played out at the table, not skipped. He brings them to the fire first, sets out dried mutton and ale, and seats the travelers by name: the rite is **well performed**. Birgir's player marks +1 Orðstír, witnessed by the table.

On the third night, the party's skald — deep in his cups — boasts he will drink Birgir under the table and take his silver arm-ring as forfeit. The GM rules this a boast under the roof: braga-goblet rule, boldness 2, written down. The skald fails his Constitution save (a roll of 9 + 2 = 11 against DC 13 — failure); he loses 2 Orðstír before every witness at the hearth, and Birgir keeps his arm-ring *and* gains a story he will tell for years.

On the fourth morning Birgir names their departure day without insult — and the party, taught by the skald's example, departs with spoken thanks and a debt-token of gratitude each. Three seasons later, when Birgir's steading is threatened by a feud, those tokens come due — and the party remembers whose fire saved them in the blizzard.

### III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

Players feel, in their bones, why hospitality was *law*: in a land of winter and wilderness, the door you close might be your own, next year. The guest-rite teaches reciprocity as survival technology — the host's generosity is not kindness but *infrastructure*, and the guest's restraint is the payment. The host's protection duty teaches that sanctuary is *work*, not sentiment: the peace of the hall is the host's honor on the line, which is why the sagas' hosts disarmed their guests with such care. When a PC later stands at their own door in a storm, deciding whether to open it, the mechanics have already taught them what the sagas knew: the hearth is the first circle of frith, and everything else — the Thing, the oath, the duel — is built on it.

## The Stranger's Reception — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 10)

**Video:** `videos/ep10-stranger.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep10-stranger.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §XXXIII` — this file expands that section; additions the codex leaves open are labeled.

**Canon warning:** everything below this header reproduces Volmarr's §XXXIII rulings *exactly* — the 1d6 table, the class and alignment weights, the keep-most/least-extreme dice rule. These are his law for this table. The GM does not rebalance them, soften them, or add a "they take it in stride" result. Expansions are labeled and never alter the core dice.

### I. Grounding — Why It Is True

The sagas know no casual tolerance of the otherworldly. When the uncanny walks into a human settlement, the response is never a shrug — it is terror or worship, and sometimes both in the same hour. Grettir's neighbors meet the draugr Glámr — a dead shepherd who walks — with pure dread: the haunting at Thorhallsstad empties the farm of its courage long before it empties it of sheep, and no one in the district treats the walking dead as a neighbor [Grett chs.32–37]. The álfar of the Eddas are mighty and perilous beings to be placated with offerings, not befriended over ale [PE]. A shape-changer or finngálkn in the sagas draws fascination and fear in equal measure. ⚠️ *Saga evidence is anecdotal rather than systematic: the polarization is the pattern — terror at one pole, worship at the other, and nothing calm in between. The table below is Volmarr's construction on that pattern.*

This is the mechanical price of the casting law (canon §2): player characters are human, and other races are vanishingly rare. The table below is what "vanishingly rare" *costs*. A table of elves and tieflings could never feel the old fear, and the old fear is load-bearing. Rarity is not a restriction on fun; it is the price of wonder.

**What is homebrew here:** the polarization (awe and terror, no calm middle) is the saga pattern. The 1d6 table, the extremity weights, and the keep-most/least-extreme dice rule are Volmarr's rulings, reproduced exactly — they are canon for this table and are not to be "balanced" by the GM. The per-result scene guidance, the tie-break, familiarity, and unstable-Hamingja rules below are the expansion's.

### II. The Complete Playable Rule

#### At a 5E Table

**Trigger.** Whenever a **visibly or known** non-human being (álfr, draugr, jotunn-kin, shape-changer, or anything the community names as not-human) enters a human community, roll **1d6 for the crowd's dominant reaction** — per *group or notable individual* — or choose by context. **There is no "they take it in stride" result. That reaction does not exist.**

**"Visibly or known"** (expansion): the trigger fires on *either* condition. *Visibly* = the nature can be seen (pointed ears bared, draugr's blackened flesh, a jotunn's stature). *Known* = the community has been told truly, by a credible voice — a goði's naming, a völva's word, the being's own admission. Rumor alone does not trigger the roll; rumor triggers *whispers*, and whispers are the GM's scene-dressing, not dice. A being *passing undetected* as human triggers nothing — until discovery (see below).

**Examples at the threshold** (expansion):
- An álfr with ears bared walking the market road: *visibly* — roll.
- A draugr whose nature the völva announces at the Thing: *known* — roll, even if it looks human.
- A whispered rumor that the new trader is jotunn-kin: neither — whispers, not dice. The GM plays the suspicion as atmosphere.
- A shape-changer living three years as a karl, discovered mid-deception: roll at +1 extremity, vengeance claim, and the story travels (see below).

| d6 | The reception |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Panic.** Doors barred, children snatched indoors, the alarm raised. Some flee; some seize weapons in terror. |
| 2 | **Terror-anger.** "Ill-omen!" Stones and spears; the goði is summoned to name what it is and what it wants. |
| 3 | **Dread-awe.** They kneel at a distance, whisper, leave offerings where it will find them. None will meet its eyes. |
| 4 | **Obsessive reverence.** They follow, beg blessings, press gifts on it, treat every word as prophecy. It cannot be alone. |
| 5 | **Possessive wonder.** They crowd and stare and touch; the thing becomes the settlement's marvel and will not be let to leave. |
| 6 | **Holy dread.** It is declared landvættir or god-sent. A vé may be raised around it — or it may be begged, firmly, to go before the spirits grow angry. |

**Rolling a settlement, step by step** (expansion — procedure for the GM):
1. **Identify the reactors.** Split the community into groups (the watch, the market crowd, the goði's household) plus notable individuals (the jarl, the völva, the loudest farmer). Roll per group/individual.
2. **Score each.** Class weight + alignment weight, exactly as ruled. When in doubt about an NPC's alignment, the GM assigns it openly — "these folk are lawful; custom is load-bearing here."
3. **Roll the dice.** (1+score) dice keep most extreme; (1+|score|) keep least extreme; 0 → one die. Roll openly — the table should see fate being weighed.
4. **Offer wary respect.** Ask each spirit-touched PC/NPC: do you attempt the save? One attempt per being per community.
5. **Play the results.** Use the scene guidance below. Contradictory results between groups are *correct* — the settlement is of several minds, loudly.
6. **Record it.** Note each group's reception. It persists until familiarity shrinks it or events change it.

**What each result does in play** (expansion — scene guidance; the dice name the weather, the GM plays it):
- **Panic (1):** the settlement is *non-functional* for the scene — trade stops, doors bar, the being cannot buy, hire, or parley. Anyone approaching the being must get past frightened armed folk first. Panic *cools* to Terror-anger or Dread-awe after one full day without incident (GM's call which).
- **Terror-anger (2):** the crowd is *mobilized against* the being. Stones first, spears if it does not leave. The goði is summoned: if the goði names it *ill-omen*, the crowd acts within the hour; if the goði names it *god-sent*, re-roll the reception once (the naming has authority — expansion).
- **Dread-awe (3):** the settlement functions *around* the being — offerings appear where it will find them, paths clear before it, no one meets its eyes. It can move freely but cannot *belong*: no hirelings, no hospitality beyond the offerings, no names exchanged.
- **Obsessive reverence (4):** the being cannot be alone and cannot leave quietly — followers dog its steps, every word is treated as prophecy (the GM should let an idle remark *become* a prophecy the settlement acts on). Gifts pressed on it are debt-tokens of a strange kind: refusing them insults the givers (−1 Orðstír among them, expansion — the community's honor is engaged too).
- **Possessive wonder (5):** the settlement will not let it leave — not by force at first, but by crowding, pleading, blocking the road with bodies and celebration. Leaving requires slipping away unseen or a firm, public refusal (which sours the wonder toward Terror-anger at the GM's discretion).
- **Holy dread (6):** the being is *declared* — landvættir or god-sent — and the declaration has legal weight: harming it is now a frith-breach against the vé (expansion: the community's naming *consecrates*). A vé may be raised around it, with offerings and a warden. Or the settlement may beg it, firmly and with gifts, to go before the spirits grow angry — refusal risks the dread curdling into Terror-anger.

**Who is reacting matters.** Each reactor has an **extremity score**, summed from two axes (Volmarr's ruling, reproduced exactly):

*By calling — how they live:*
- **Iron-minded (+1)** — Fighter, Barbarian, Rogue, Ranger, Monk (*drengr, húskarl, veiðimaðr, smiðr*). No frame for the uncanny, so it hits them harder.
- **Spirit-touched (−1)** — Cleric, Druid, Wizard, Sorcerer, Warlock, Bard (*goði, völva, seiðmaðr, skald* — the skald knows the old stories of such beings). They have seen, or sung of, stranger things.

*By orderliness — how tightly they hold the world together:*
- **Lawful (+2)** — the world has a shape, custom is load-bearing, and this thing breaks the shape. *Much* more extreme.
- **Chaotic (−1)** — the world was always strange; this is only Tuesday. A *little* less extreme.
- **Neutral** on this axis: 0.

Ordinary folk with no class and no strong alignment weight roll at **score 0** (a single honest roll) unless the GM assigns them — but note what this means: a *lawful* farming village with no adventuring classes still rolls at **+2** (3 dice, keep most extreme). The lawful farmer's terror is the default, not the exception (expansion — this is the ruling's intent, stated plainly).

**The dice rule** (Volmarr's ruling, reproduced exactly): score **above 0** → roll **(1 + score)** dice and **keep the most extreme**; score **below 0** → roll **(1 + |score|)** dice and **keep the least extreme**; score **0** → a single honest roll. So a lawful fighter (+3) rolls 4 dice and keeps the most extreme — the very pillars of his world are cracking — while a chaotic sorcerer (−2) rolls 3 and keeps the mildest, and a chaotic barbarian (+1 −1 = 0) simply rolls once, pulled both ways.

**Poles and middle** (Volmarr's ruling): the **poles** are 1 (Panic), 2 (Terror-anger), 4 (Obsessive reverence), 6 (Holy dread); the **middle** is 3 (Dread-awe), 5 (Possessive wonder). "Most extreme" = the result farthest from the middle (1 and 6 are the most extreme of all); "least extreme" = the result nearest it.

**Tie-break** (expansion): if the kept dice tie at equal extremity on *opposite* poles (a 1 and a 6), the crowd is **split** — half panics, half worships, and the settlement argues with itself, loudly, in front of the being. Do not re-roll the tie; the split *is* the result, and it is the most dangerous one: a split crowd can turn on itself.

**Wary respect.** A **spirit-touched** character may attempt a **DC 13 Wisdom saving throw** to master themselves entirely: on success they react with **wary respect** — no panic, no worship; they make the warding sign, observe, and speak carefully. They become the only ones in the settlement able to parley with the being without the crowd's hysteria poisoning the talks. (Iron-minded characters may not attempt this — they have no frame to steady themselves *with*.) A spirit-touched character may attempt the save **once per being per community** — failure means they ride the crowd's reaction like everyone else (expansion).

**This applies to player characters as well as NPCs:** when the party meets something not-human, each PC's player rolls their own reception by class — the fighter may be reaching for an axe while the seiðr-worker is already reading the signs. The player *plays* the result: a PC who rolled Panic does not get to be calm because the player is. This is the rule's sharpest tooth, and the GM should enforce it kindly but firmly.

**While revealed among humans** (ongoing, Volmarr's ruling): **disadvantage on Persuasion and Deception** (to blend in or negotiate normally); **advantage on Intimidation** (fear is the one language that always translates); **Orðstír cannot be earned** through ordinary deeds while the nature is known — the community cannot weigh the honor of what it cannot comprehend; **Hamingja behaves strangely at the GM's discretion** (the Norns wove no thread for this one).

**Unstable Hamingja** (expansion): at each session's start, the GM rolls 1d6 for each revealed non-human PC; on a 1, their Hamingja is **halved (rounded up)** for the session. The luck-line was woven for humans. A völva can *see* this instability the way she sees Wyrd-debt (§III) — and may say so aloud, which is its own kind of doom.

**Familiarity** (expansion): the reception is re-rolled for each *new* community — the next village has not heard, or does not believe it has. After a full month of peaceful contact with the same community, the GM reduces the number of dice rolled by 1 (minimum 1 die) — the uncanny becomes *known*, never *normal*. The roll itself is never skipped: calm acceptance does not exist. A being that has lived openly in one settlement for a year is a *legend* there, not a neighbor.

**The goði's naming** (expansion):
- When the crowd rolls Terror-anger (2), the goði is summoned *by the rule itself* — the table should play this scene, not skip it.
- The goði names what the being *is* and what it *wants*, before witnesses. The naming is a judgment, and the goði's Orðstír rides on it.
- If the goði names it **god-sent**, re-roll the crowd's reception once (the naming has authority).
- If the goði names it **ill-omen** and the being stays, the goði may demand it leave — refusal is a frith-breach against the community's peace.
- A goði who names *falsely* (declares god-sent what is merely strange, for profit or fear) gains 2 Wyrd-debt when it comes out — the gods notice mislabeling (§III).

**Beings that return** (expansion):
- A being that leaves a community and later returns is *re-rolled* — but with one fewer die than its first reception there (minimum 1), as familiarity. The settlement remembers; memory softens, never erases.
- A being that left under Terror-anger and returns *uninvited* rolls at **+1 extremity** — the crowd remembers the stones it threw, and its arm remembers the throwing.

**The uncanny at the Thing** (expansion):
- The Thing is the assembly of *free humans* (§VII). A revealed non-human cannot bring suit, swear oaths, or stand as oath-helper — the law does not know what to do with it, which is itself the teaching.
- It *may* be spoken for: a human patron (a goði, a jarl, a wary-respect spirit-touched) can carry its cause before the assembly — at the patron's own risk. The crowd's reception applies to the *patron* by association for the session (GM's call on the extremity).
- A being that disrupts the Thing is not fined — it is *named* ill-omen and driven out, and the driving-out is lawful force, not feud-fodder. The law protects itself first.

**Mixed parties** (expansion):
- A party traveling *with* a revealed non-human is judged by association: while the being's nature is known, the whole party suffers **disadvantage on Persuasion** (5E) / **−2 on reaction rolls when parleying** (OSE) with communities that have rolled — the crowd's reaction poisons the humans' words too.
- The party's *own* receptions were rolled when they first met the being (§XXXIII, PCs included) — and they stand. A PC who rolled Terror-anger at their álfr companion does not get to be comfortable three sessions later without the familiarity rule doing its work.
- Splitting the party helps: humans who arrive *without* the being are received as humans. The sagas know this tactic — the strange companion waits in the woods while the humans do the talking. It is not cowardice; it is diplomacy.

**Undetected passing** is a different matter — spies and álfar in disguise are saga fodder, and no roll is made while the disguise holds. But **discovery mid-deception** is treated as a **níð-worthy betrayal** (§XVII): the reception is rolled immediately at **+1 extremity** (expansion: betrayal sharpens every reaction), the deceived community gains a vengeance claim, and the being's *next* reception in any community starts one step more extreme (the story travels ahead — expansion).

#### At an OSE Table

- The 1d6 table, the extremity weights, and the most/least-extreme dice rule are **identical** — the table is system-neutral, and OSE's Lawful/Neutral/Chaotic needs no conversion.
- **Classes:** iron-minded = **Fighter, Barbarian, Thief, Ranger**; spirit-touched = **Cleric, Druid, Magic-User, Illusionist, Bard** (OSE Advanced Fantasy). A 5E Monk at an OSE table counts as iron-minded — a martial-discipline calling with no frame for the uncanny (GM's call, expansion).
- **Wary respect:** **Save vs. Spells** (instead of the DC 13 Wisdom save), spirit-touched only, once per being per community.
- **While revealed:** **−2 on reaction rolls** when parleying or blending in, **+2 on reaction rolls** when threatening; **no Orðstír earned** while the nature is known; Hamingja unstable as written.
- **Everything else is identical** — the uncanny is an event in any century.

#### Worked Example

*Illustrative only — hypothetical rolls.* An álfr-woman, revealed and known, walks into a market town. The GM rolls reception per group.

The town watch (six fighters, Lawful): extremity +1 (iron-minded) +2 (lawful) = **+3** → roll 4d6, keep the most extreme. The dice come up 2, 3, 5, 6 — the 6 is farthest from the middle: **Holy dread**. The watch kneels in the street, and their captain begs her, firmly and with gifts, to leave before the landvættir grow angry.

The market crowd (commoners, Lawful, no class): +0 +2 = **+2** → 3d6, keep most extreme: 1, 4, 4 → **Panic** — stalls overturned, children snatched indoors, the alarm bell ringing while the watch kneels. The town is now of two minds, loudly.

One spirit-touched hedge-wise woman (Neutral): score −1 → 2d6, keep least extreme. She also attempts wary respect: DC 13 Wisdom save, rolling 11 + 3 = 14 — **success**. She makes the warding sign, steps through the panicking crowd, and speaks carefully to the álfr-woman — the only parley in the town not poisoned by hysteria. If the álfr-woman stays the month, the dice shrink — but the roll never goes away.

### III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

The player learns what the sagas assumed: in this world, the uncanny is never furniture. It is always an *event*. A table of elves and tieflings could never feel the old fear, and the old fear is load-bearing — which is why the casting law exists, and why this episode's mechanics refuse to soften it. The lawful farmer's terror teaches the deepest lesson of all: to these people, the world *has an order*, and the uncanny is what happens when the order breaks. The extreme reaction is not bigotry — it is cosmology. When the PCs themselves roll their own reception and the fighter's player watches their character reach for an axe they didn't choose to reach for, the lesson lands in the body, not the notebook — which is exactly where the sagas always aimed.

## Oaths — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 11)

**Video:** `videos/ep11-oaths.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep11-oaths.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §IX` — this file expands that section; additions the codex 
leaves open are labeled.

### I. Grounding — Why It Is True

The *eiðr* — the spoken oath — really was the load-bearing beam of Norse society. There were 
no written contracts in the 9th century; a man's sworn word, witnessed, was the contract. The Edda 
itself treats the oath sworn on a ring as *the most sacred of oaths*: Hávamál's stanza 110 has 
Óðinn himself swearing on his ring, and Bellows's note records that Óðinn "violated the most 
sacred of oaths, that sworn on his ring" [PE: Hávamál 110]. If the gods answer for broken oaths, 
men answer for them too.

The saga model is Haraldr Fairhair's vow, sworn before his father's old friend Guthorm at a feast: 
*"I make the solemn vow, and take God to witness... that never shall I clip or comb my hair until I 
have subdued the whole of Norway, with scat, and duties, and domains; or if not, have died in the 
attempt"* [Heim, ch. 4]. Ten years later, his hair uncut and uncombed, Earl Ragnvald cut it and 
gave him the name Fairhair [Heim, ch. 23]. The vow was public, conditional, and terminal — it 
ended only in triumph or death. That is the shape of a Norse oath: it names the exact terms, it 
names the power it is sworn by, and witnesses carry it.

What is Volmarr's homebrew: the *mechanic* — advantage on one check per day while pursuing the 
oath, the numeric Orðstír drop to 1, the 3 Wyrd-debt, the GM holding players "to the letter." No 
saga hands out dice bonuses. The history is the shape of the oath; the numbers are the game making 
that shape *bite*.

### II. The Complete Playable Rule

#### At a 5E Table

**Swearing an oath.**
1. The oath must be **spoken aloud by the player, in exact words, before witnesses** (at least one 
named NPC or fellow PC who hears and can repeat it). It names the **power sworn by**: a god, one's 
ancestors, one's weapon, the oath-ring on the altar, or the drink in the cup.
2. The oath must be **verifiable**: the table can tell whether it has been fulfilled or broken. "I 
will protect her" is vague — vague oaths bind vaguely, and the GM is empowered to rule a vague 
oath half-fulfilled. "I will see her safe to Kaupang by the next full moon, or my life is the 
price" is an oath.
3. **Record it.** The GM writes the exact words in the campaign record. Anyone at the table may 
later read it back aloud.

**The strength it lends.** While the oath remains unbroken and unfulfilled, the PC has **advantage 
on one check per day** that directly serves the oath. "Directly serves" means the action's success 
or failure materially advances the sworn task — the GM decides, and announces it ("the oath lends 
you strength") so the table hears it. The oath can serve one oath at a time: a PC pursuing two 
sworn oaths must choose which one the day's strength serves.

**Keeping it.** An oath is fulfilled when its letter is satisfied before witnesses. Fulfillment 
before witnesses grants **+1 Orðstír** (keeping an oath at cost is a deed before witnesses, §I) 
— +2 if the cost was real and public (danger, great expense, public shaming risk).

**Breaking it.**
- The breaker is *eiðrofi*, oath-breaker: **Orðstír drops to 1 immediately**, no gradual decay.
- All **debt-tokens owed *to* them are void** (§VI) — who honors a debt to an oath-breaker?
- The breaker gains **3 Wyrd-debt** (§III) — the GM notes it; wyrd remembers.
- The table remembers: NPCs will speak the old oath back at them for years. The GM should make this 
happen at least once per story arc, before witnesses.

**Restoration.** Only a great public atonement begins restoration:
- Pay **triple weregild** (weregild.json ladder) to the party wronged by the broken oath, publicly 
at the Thing, with witnesses; or
- Perform a **deed of saga-weight** — defined as a deed that would on its own grant +3 Orðstír 
(a great battle, saving the district, a gift of kingly scale).
Restoration is only the *beginning*: Orðstír then rebuilds at the normal rate, and the breaker 
never again gains the one-check-per-day strength from *that* oath.

**Oath conflicts.** Two oaths in direct conflict: the earlier-sworn oath takes precedence in law 
(the later was sworn foolishly, and that foolishness is itself a deed before witnesses — −1 
Orðstír). The GM must warn a player *before* the second oath is spoken that it conflicts.

**The braga-goblet.** Boasts sworn over the cup at sumbel (§IV) are full oaths — exact words, 
witnesses, all of it. The GM writes them down. The table will hold the player to them. Sumbel 
boasts are the most common way PCs swear accidentally.

**False-swearing and compurgation.** At the Thing (§VII), an oath sworn falsely is discovered only 
by wyrd — but the GM notes it, and Wyrd-debt accrues. Oath-helpers (*compurgators*) do not make 
an oath stronger mechanically; they make it *publicly expensive* — each oath-helper of good 
Orðstír who stands with a false oath risks their own standing if the falsehood surfaces.

#### At an OSE Table

- The spoken form — exact words, witnesses, named power — is **identical**; a vague oath binds 
vaguely in any century.
- The strength it lends: **+2 on one roll per day** directly serving the oath (or roll twice, keep 
the better, if the table prefers) — `SYSTEM_VARIANTS.md` Ep. 11.
- Breaking: *eiðrofi* — Orðstír to 1 immediately, debt-tokens owed *to* them void, 3 
Wyrd-debt; atonement as codex §IX. **Identical.**
- Fulfillment before witnesses: +1 Orðstír (+2 at real cost) — Orðstír is system-neutral.
- Restoration, oath conflicts, and the braga-goblet: **identical** — the law is the law.
- False-swearing at the Thing: Wyrd-debt accrues as in 5E; compurgators as codex §VII.

#### Worked Example

*Illustrative only — hypothetical rolls, not a real session.* Áskell swears, before the whole 
hall at the sumbel: "By Þórr's hammer I name him, I will return Snorri's stolen sword before 
Yule, or my spear-arm is forfeit." The GM writes it down. Two days later Áskell trails the thieves 
through a storm; the GM calls for Wisdom (Survival). Áskell's player says "my oath lends strength" 
— with advantage, a roll of 14 and 4 is played as 14; +3 = 17 beats DC 15. The oath held. He 
returns the sword at the Yule feast before witnesses: +2 Orðstír (cost was real). Had he instead 
sold the sword and denied it, he would be *eiðrofi* — Orðstír to 1, three debt-tokens owed him 
voided, 3 Wyrd-debt — and the GM would have the stolen sword's owner speak his exact words back 
at him in the next session.

**Conditional oaths.** Haraldr's form — "until X, or death" — is the strongest oath-shape: it 
names its own ending. A conditional oath (fulfilled when X happens) lends its strength only while X 
is still achievable. If X becomes impossible (the sworn man dies of fever, the ship is lost with 
all hands), the oath is **released by wyrd** — no penalty, but no strength either, and the table 
should mark the release aloud ("wyrd has taken the oath from you").

**Oaths by the dead and the unborn.** Swearing by one's ancestors binds the *kin*, not just the 
swearer: if the oath concerns kin-honor, the GM may rule that breaking it costs every sworn kinsman 
at the table 1 Orðstír. This is why men thought twice before naming their fathers.

**The oath-ring.** Districts keep an **oath-ring** on the altar or the goði's arm — swearing 
with a hand on the ring is the heaviest form. An oath sworn on the ring, broken, adds **+1 
Wyrd-debt** (4 total) — the ring remembers. Stealing or desecrating an oath-ring is sacrilege: 
treat as oathbreaking by the thief, with the district as the wronged party.

### III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

Modern players promise constantly and casually — "I'll come," "I'll do it," "I swear" — and 
nobody holds them to anything. The oath mechanic reverses the polarity: the moment a player learns 
that a sloppy boast at the sumbel becomes a recorded legal obligation, the whole table's speech 
*changes*. Players start qualifying their words, pausing before they speak, weighing whether they 
can pay. That pause — the weight of words becoming actions — is the Viking Age entering the 
player's nervous system, not as lecture but as learned caution. When the GM finally reads a PC's 
old oath back to them in a crowded hall, the player doesn't learn a fact about Norse culture; they 
*feel* it.

## Flyting — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 12)

**Video:** `videos/ep12-flyting.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep12-flyting.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §X` — this file expands that section; additions the codex 
leaves open are labeled.

### I. Grounding — Why It Is True

The divine template of flyting is the *Lokasenna*, where Loki trades verse-for-verse insults with 
the whole pantheon [PE]. The technique is exactly the codex's "Loki's rule" — strike the live 
nerves. Óðinn accuses Loki: *"Winters eight wast thou under the earth, / Milking the cows as a 
maid, / (Ay, and babes didst thou bear; / Unmanly thy soul must seem)"* [PE: Lokasenna 23]; Loki 
answers by striking Óðinn's nerve in turn: *"They say that with spells in Samsey once / Like 
witches with charms didst thou work; / And in witch's guise among men didst thou go; / Unmanly thy 
soul must seem"* [PE: Lokasenna 24]. The accusation that ended the exchange in this world was 
*ergi* — unmanliness — and even a god answered it, immediately and hotly. That is the whole of 
flyting in one stanza: formal, witnessed, escalating, and aimed at what the culture could not bear 
to hear.

The human art is shown in Egil's saga, where Egil raises the níðstöng — the scorn-pole — a 
hazel pole with a horse's head, speaking the curse aloud in solemn form against King Eirik and 
Queen Gunnhilda, turning the head landward against the land's guardian spirits [Eg]. The insult is 
*ritualized, spoken aloud, and performed before witnesses* — violence done with words and objects 
instead of steel, and the saga treats it as a weapon of full weight.

What is Volmarr's homebrew: the dice structure — best of three contested Charisma rounds, 
GM-judged escalation, the fixed ±2/±1 Orðstír stakes, the níð-marks system. No saga scores 
flyting in rounds. The history is the form (spoken, witnessed, escalating, answer-or-be-shamed); 
the rounds are the game making that form playable at a table where no one is a professional poet.

### II. The Complete Playable Rule

#### At a 5E Table

**Challenging.** Either party may call a flyting when an insult has been spoken before witnesses 
(or when an insult demands answer, §XVIII *ergi*). The challenge must be accepted in the scene — 
silence is an answer (see below). Both parties must be **free and present**; no flyting by proxy 
except the skald's exception below.

**The contest.** A flyting is **best of three exchanges** (rounds):
1. Each round, both parties roll **Charisma** — Performance *or* Intimidation, the attacker's 
choice each round.
2. **The form must be spoken aloud by the player**: a real insult, in character, before witnesses. 
The player may not say "I insult his mother" — they must *do it*. A player who cannot or will not 
speak the insult automatically loses that round.
3. **Escalation (Loki's rule):** each round the insult must cut *deeper* than the last. The GM 
judges whether the stakes were raised — new ground, sharper nerve, more public knowledge. A 
repeated insult, a weak insult, or an insult that retreats to safer ground is an **automatic loss 
for that round**. The culture's live nerves: ergi, cowardice, kin-shame, oath-breaking, stinginess, 
the dishonor of one's father or house.
4. Highest roll wins the round (ties reroll that exchange). First to **two rounds** wins the 
flyting. If one party flees, falls silent, or refuses to continue, they lose the flyting 
immediately.

**Outcome.**
- The **winner gains +1 Orðstír** (publicly, witnessed).
- The **loser** must either **answer with holmgang** (§XI) — challenged there and then, before 
the same witnesses — or **lose 2 Orðstír publicly**.
- If either party speaks *níð* (accusation of ergi, cowardice, or perversion) and it goes 
**unanswered by dusk**, the accused is *níðingr* until they answer **in blood** (holmgang) **or 
in law** (a suit at the Thing). Unanswered níð does not fade — it sits on the accused like a 
mark.
- **Answering níð well:** if the accused answers the níð-charge with a flyting-round victory 
*in that same flyting*, the charge rebounds — the *accuser* is judged to have spoken níð 
falsely and takes a níð-mark (cf. Episode 5, níð-marks).

**The skald's proxy.** A skald (or any PC with a composed praise-poem or níðvísa ready — 
Episode 19) may enter a flyting **for another**, speaking the verses in the principal's name. The 
proxy's Orðstír is at stake exactly as if they were the principal; the principal's is untouched 
unless the proxy loses by fleeing (then both lose 2). Verse is a weapon you can lend — and a 
hired tongue that runs is twice the shame.

**Edge cases.**
- **Audience:** flyting before no witnesses is a quarrel, not a flyting — no stakes, no Orðstír 
changes. Minimum: two witnesses besides the parties.
- **Against a non-combatant or thrall:** flyting down is *níðingr*-work-adjacent; the GM may rule 
the challenger loses 1 Orðstír for the cruelty of it, whatever the dice say.
- **Interrupting a flyting** (shouting down, drawing steel) is a grave frith-breach within the 
circle (§II) — the flyting is a *rite*, and the rite has peace.
- **Women flyting:** saga women flyt as sharply as men (and often sharper). No mechanical 
difference; the social nerves differ (kin-shame, hospitality-failure, oath-cowardice) but the 
stakes are identical.

#### At an OSE Table

- The spoken form — a real insult aloud, in character, before witnesses, cutting deeper every 
round — is **identical**; no dice can do that part (`SYSTEM_VARIANTS.md` Ep. 12).
- Each round: both parties roll **1d20 ≤ Charisma**. A trained speaker (skald, or a PC with a 
composed poem ready) shifts the effective score **−2 in their favor** (easier roll-under); an 
attacker with nothing but hot air shifts it **+2 against them**.
- Escalation: a repeated or weak insult is an automatic round loss — exactly as codex.
- Outcome: loser answers with holmgang (§XI) or loses 2 Orðstír publicly; winner +1; *níð* 
unanswered by dusk → *níðingr* until answered in blood or law. **Identical** — the stakes are 
honor, not mechanics.
- The skald's proxy and all edge cases: **identical.**

#### Worked Example

*Illustrative only — hypothetical rolls, not a real session.* At the winter feast, Ketill calls 
Þórðr a coward before the hall — *"I saw thee hide behind the shields at Hafrsfjord while 
better men bled."* Þórðr answers: flyting, now. Round one: Ketill rolls Charisma (Intimidation) 
— a roll of 14 + 3 = 17. Þórðr rolls (Performance) — a roll of 16 + 2 = 18, and speaks his 
insult aloud: *"My father held his shield-wall at Hafrsfjord; thine sold his spear for a Danish 
bribe — ask the Danes, they keep receipts."* Þórðr takes round one — he struck kin-shame, 
the deeper nerve. Round two must cut deeper: Ketill accuses ergi outright — *"And yet 'twas thee 
I saw milking another man's cows, maid-fashion."* The room goes still. If Þórðr's round-two 
answer is weak or repeats round one, he loses the round automatically; the níð-word hangs on him 
until dusk. This is the whole lesson in one exchange: the dice are the least dangerous thing in the 
room.

**Lines of attack (for players who need a starting nerve).** The GM may offer these as the 
culture's known live nerves — but the player must still *speak* the insult themselves:
- *Ergi:* unmanliness — cowardice, fleeing, woman's work named as shame.
- *Kin-shame:* the father's cowardice, the mother's price, the brother's hanging.
- *Oath-cowardice:* the vow sworn and never kept (the GM's campaign record is ammunition).
- *Stinginess:* the closed hand, the thin feast, the gift never returned.
- *Corpse-shame:* the man who struck a fallen foe, or fled and left his lord's body.
Escalation means moving from the general nerve to the *specific, named* wound — from "thy kin are 
cowards" to "thy father dropped his shield at Hafrsfjord and I have the shield."

**The audience's judgment.** After the flyting, the GM may poll the witnesses (NPCs) aloud: a 
one-line verdict each ("Ketill spoke truer," "Þórðr's tongue is the sharper"). This costs 
nothing mechanically and teaches everything socially — the flyting's real prize was always the 
room's memory.

### III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

Flyting teaches the honor culture's central paradox from the inside: *words are violence, and 
violence has rules.* Players who are used to insulting NPCs freely — the tavern-brawl mouth — 
discover that a spoken insult before witnesses is a loaded weapon with a legal fuse. The mandatory 
escalation is the key teaching: the game *forces* players to find the culture's live nerves, which 
means they must *learn* what those nerves are — ergi, kin-shame, cowardice, stinginess — not 
from a handout but from the need to wound effectively and survive the answer. And the níð-by-dusk 
rule teaches the asymmetry of the whole system: you can *say* the unsayable, but only if you're 
willing to bleed for it. The table learns discipline of tongue the way the culture learned it — 
by watching someone pay for an undisciplined one.

## Gift & Debt — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 13)

**Video:** `videos/ep13-gift-debt.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep13-gift-debt.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §VI` — this file expands that section; additions the codex 
leaves open are labeled.

### I. Grounding — Why It Is True

The obligation economy of the North is stated as plainly as any law in the Hávamál: *"Friends 
shall gladden each other with arms and garments... Gift-givers' friendships are longest found, if 
fair their fates may be"* — and the binding corollary — *"To his friend a man a friend shall 
prove, and gifts with gifts requite"* [Háv 41–42]. The gift is not generosity; it is *contract*. 
To accept a gift and not return a greater one was to accept *inferiority* — the ledger of honor 
that no one wrote down and everyone kept. Silver moved by weight — hacksilver, arm-rings, dirhams 
— because the metal was the memory.

The formalized end of the ledger is weregild, the man-price, and the saga gives its first instance: 
when the gods cover the slain otter's skin with gold to ransom Loki's life, Regin closes the tale 
— *"since that time gold is called Ottergild, and for no other cause than this"* [Vols]. Gold 
that *covers the wound*. The feud ends not with forgiveness but with weighed silver, paid publicly, 
witnessed — the same mechanics as friendship, run in reverse.

What is Volmarr's homebrew: the debt-token system itself (physical stones/beads at the table, the 
1-ounce threshold, the seasonal-turn deadline, the ±Orðstír bookkeeping) and the simplified 
weregild ladder. No saga issues debt-tokens. But the shape — gift binds, reciprocation must 
exceed, refusal is insult, weregild is public gold ending blood — is straight from the sources.

### II. The Complete Playable Rule

#### At a 5E Table

**The debt-token.**
- Any **gift of real value** creates a debt-token: GM's threshold, roughly **1+ ounce of silver** 
(an arm-ring, a good weapon, a war-horse, a valuable thrall's freedom-papers — whatever the 
district would call generous).
- The token is **held by the giver against the receiver** — the giver holds the claim, and it is 
**physical at the table**: a stone, a bead, a marked chip, sitting in front of the giver. Visible, 
like honor.
- A gift given *secretly* creates no token — but neither does it buy standing. Secret generosity 
is invisible to the ledger.
- A gift given as **payment for services rendered** (hired work, agreed price) is trade, not gift 
— no token. The table must be able to tell the difference: if a price was named beforehand, it is 
trade.

**Reciprocation.**
- The receiver **must** reciprocate with something of **greater value** — not equal, *greater* 
— **before the next seasonal turn** (§XII). "Greater" is the giver's honor judged by the table: 
the GM decides whether the return clears the bar, and the table witnesses it.
- Failure: at the seasonal turn, the receiver loses **1 Orðstír per unanswered token**, and the 
**giver gains +1 Orðstír** (generosity witnessed — the giver is now publicly the greater 
friend).
- Reciprocation is itself a gift and creates its own token *unless* the receiver explicitly frames 
it as settlement: "this settles the debt between us" before witnesses. Settling cleanly ends the 
chain; settling *grudgingly* (the GM's call, based on the player's tone at the table) halves the 
Orðstír the giver would otherwise gain.
- A token may also be **transferred**: the giver may hand their claim to a third party ("my debt is 
now owed to my brother") before witnesses. Debts, like honor, are public property.

**Refusing a gift.**
- Refusing a gift outright is itself an **insult**: **−1 Orðstír to the refuser**, unless a 
reason is spoken before witnesses ("I will not take a ring from a man who owes my kin weregild" — 
spoken reasons are honored; silent refusal is contempt).
- Refusing is the correct play when the gift is a **trap**: a gift given to *indebt* you 
deliberately (the giver's intent is judged by the GM). A spoken refusal before witnesses of a 
trap-gift costs nothing and may *gain* +1 Orðstír — the table respects a man who sees the hook.

**Weregild — the gift that ends a feud.**
- Paid in **weighed silver** (gp in game terms), **publicly at the Thing**, before witnesses, with 
a feast of reconciliation. Payment ends the feud **only if accepted** before witnesses — refusal 
keeps the vengeance-claim alive (see Episode 20, Feud).
- The table's ladder (`engine/data/homebrew/weregild.json`) — ⚠️ simplified; historical sums 
varied by district and law-code:

| Rank | Price |
|---|---|
| Thrall | None — the killer compensates the *owner* the market value (typically 1–3 marks) |
| Leysingi (freedman) | 6 marks |
| Karl (free householder) — the base unit | 12 marks |
| Húsfreyja (lady of the house) | 12 marks — she holds the keys and weaves the frith |
| Skald / master smith / renowned craftsman | 18 marks — skill raises the price |
| Goði / völva of renown | 24 marks — holy standing doubles the claim |
| Jarl | 48 marks — few can pay it; jarls' killings become wars |
| King | Priceless — no weregild exists; only feud, exile, or war |

- **Wounds and thefts have scheduled prices:** minor wound 1–3 marks by severity; maiming (hand, 
foot, eye) half the victim's full weregild; facial disfigurement one-third (the face is a man's 
public self); beating of a free man 3 marks, doubled before witnesses; **stealth-theft triple the 
value stolen** — stealth-theft is níðingr-work; unanswered níð-accusation: full weregild *or* 
holmgang.
- **Weights:** 1 mark = 8 aurar (ounces) = 24 ertogar. A karl's 12 marks = 96 ounces of silver.
- **Underpayment** is worse than nonpayment: paying less than the ladder before witnesses is a 
public declaration that the dead were worth less. The GM should treat it as a fresh insult.

#### At an OSE Table

- The debt-token economy is **identical** — honor is honor in any century; tokens are stones and 
beads at the table either way (`SYSTEM_VARIANTS.md` Ep. 13).
- Gifts, reciprocation deadlines, transfers, and all Orðstír gains/losses are system-neutral.
- Refusing a gift without a spoken reason: −1 Orðstír in both. Refusing a trap-gift well: +1, 
in both.
- Weregild: paid in **gp** — the OSE economy already speaks this language; the silver-weight 
ladder needs no conversion (1 gp ≈ the table's silver unit; keep the *marks* as the in-world 
measure and let the GM price the exchange).

#### Worked Example

*Illustrative only — hypothetical rolls, not a real session.* At the autumn feast, the jarl 
publicly gifts Bjorn a gold arm-ring worth well over an ounce of silver. A bead goes in front of 
the jarl's player: debt-token. Bjorn must return something *greater* before the next seasonal turn 
(the winter Thing). Come the season-rite, Bjorn presents... a good sword — valuable, but the 
table judges it *less* than the arm-ring. The token stands unanswered: Bjorn loses 1 Orðstír; the 
jarl gains +1, and every witness saw it. The *next* time Bjorn offers the jarl a gift, the jarl may 
refuse it with a spoken reason — "I do not trade rings with men who answer gold with iron" — 
costing nothing, and Bjorn will feel the hook in the gift for the first time.

**The seasonal ledger rite.** At each season-rite, the GM performs the reckoning aloud, token by 
token: *"Bjorn holds one token against Ketill — unanswered. Ketill, −1 Orðstír; Bjorn, +1."* 
The rite takes two minutes and does more for the economy's reality than any handout. Tokens older 
than **two full turns** (a full year) unanswered become a **grievance claim** — the giver may 
bring it to the Thing as a suit (Episode 14), and the crowd-mood starts at +1 for the giver. A 
year-old debt is no longer a gift; it is an insult with paperwork.

**Gifts between PCs.** The system applies in full between player characters — and this is where 
it bites hardest. A PC who showers another with gifts is *buying* leverage, and the table will see 
it. The GM should never soften this: the debt-token between two PCs, sitting as a bead between 
their character sheets, has ended more parties' easy friendships than any monster.

**Weregild paid in goods.** Silver is weighed, but the codex allows goods or land — price them by 
the table's shared judgment before witnesses. A sword "worth" 3 marks must be *named* as 3 marks 
aloud, and the receiver may dispute the valuation before accepting. Disputed valuations are settled 
by a third party of good Orðstír, whose word is final. Quibbling over the price of a dead man is 
itself a small shame (−1 Orðstír to the quibbler, GM's call).

### III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

The modern player's instinct is the pouch: gold pieces go in, nothing comes out, wealth is a number 
that grows. The debt-token system quietly murders that instinct. Every transaction becomes a 
*relationship* — visible, witnessed, expiring — and hoarded wealth becomes a liability sitting 
on the table in someone else's hand. Players learn the Viking-Age truth that *money is memory*: 
silver is not for keeping, it is for *moving*, and the richest man in the hall is the one whose 
gifts have bought him the most friends, not the fullest chest. The moment a player *dreads* 
receiving a gift — because they know the hook is in it — the lesson has landed. They have 
stopped thinking like a modern consumer and started thinking like a 9th-century householder, for 
whom every ounce of silver was a sentence in a conversation that never ended.

## The Thing — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 14)

**Video:** `videos/ep14-thing.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep14-thing.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §VII` — this file expands that section; additions the codex 
leaves open are labeled.

### I. Grounding — Why It Is True

The *þing* — the assembly of free men — was the beating heart of Norse liberty, and Njáls 
saga is its textbook: suits are *announced* in formal words before the court — *"I give notice of 
this suit... of full forfeiture and outlawry"* [Njál] — witnesses are *made to swear an oath ere 
they bore witness* [Njál], and cases are pleaded with every procedural flaw exploited by men who 
knew the law the way duelists know the blade. The sentence for killing, when law ran its course, 
was outlawry — and the sagas show both grades in the flesh: Grettir, after killing Skeggi, is 
outlawed for three years [Grett, ch. XVI], and later the full sentence is passed upon him at the 
Althing itself [Grett, ch. XLVI].

Procedure was not bureaucracy; it was theater the whole district watched. The law had no police and 
no prisons — it had *witnesses*, *oaths*, and *the crowd*. A judgment nobody witnessed was a 
judgment nobody enforced. That is why the Thing session in this system is a structured social 
encounter and not a single die roll: the form *is* the function.

What is Volmarr's homebrew: the four-phase structure as a game encounter, the crowd-mood tracker, 
the skill-challenge framing, the advantage for a well-spoken announcement. The sagas give the 
theater; the phases give the players a script to perform it in.

### II. The Complete Playable Rule

#### At a 5E Table

**Scope.** A Thing session is a **structured social encounter** for one suit (one charge, one 
accuser, one accused). Run it when a killing, theft, níð-charge, or broken oath comes to law. The 
whole district attends — this is the campaign's public stage.

**Phase 1 — Announcement.**
- The accuser states the charge **before the assembly**, in formal words, naming the accused, the 
deed, and the law claimed.
- Roll **Charisma (Performance)**. The GM sets the DC by the charge's clarity (DC 10 for a plain 
true charge; DC 15+ for a tangled or politically dangerous one).
- **A well-spoken charge grants advantage later:** on success, the accuser has advantage on their 
first Pleading roll in Phase 3. On a failure by 5+, the crowd is bored or offended — the accuser 
has *disadvantage* on their first Pleading roll instead.
- The accused may not speak in this phase. That is the law.

**Phase 2 — Oath.**
- Both parties **swear**, each with **oath-helpers** (compurgators) — free men of good standing 
who swear beside them that the oath is true.
- The oath's weight is the **combined standing behind it**: count oath-helpers with Orðstír 5+. 
Each such helper grants the party **+1 on their first Pleading roll** (max +3). Oath-helpers below 
5 Orðstír add nothing — a crowd of nobodies is a crowd of nobodies.
- Standing as an oath-helper is itself a risk: if the oath is later proved false (by wyrd — the 
GM notes it, and Wyrd-debt accrues, §III), every oath-helper loses 1 Orðstír for having lent 
their name to a lie.
- An oath sworn falsely is **discovered only by wyrd** — no Insight roll in this phase can expose 
it. The GM notes the falsehood silently.

**Phase 3 — Pleading.**
- A **skill challenge**: each side needs **3 successes before 3 failures** (best-of-five exchanges, 
alternating sides, accuser first).
- Each exchange, the pleading party chooses their approach and the player must **actually argue it 
aloud** at the table:
  - **Insight** (catch a lie or a flaw in the pleading) — DC 13 against an honest case, DC 16+ 
against a well-built falsehood;
  - **Persuasion** (move the crowd) — DC 13;
  - **History** (cite precedent — a saga, a former judgment) — DC 15; success here counts 
double toward moving the crowd-mood tracker (see below);
  - **Deception** (spin the facts) — DC 15; if it later surfaces (wyrd), the deceiver takes a 
níð-mark.
- **The crowd's mood** is a **visible tracker** at the table: start at 0; +1 for each accuser 
success, −1 for each accused success (range −5 to +5). At **+3 or more**, the crowd is with the 
accuser (advantage on the final judgment roll for the accuser's preferred outcome); at **−3 or 
less**, with the accused. At 0, the law-speaker decides on law alone.
- Witnesses speak between exchanges at the GM's discretion — each witness is one automatic 
success for the side they support, but a witness caught lying (Insight, DC 15) flips to two 
failures for that side.

**Phase 4 — Judgment.**
- The **law-speaker** (or the jarl, if no law-speaker) pronounces, weighing the crowd-mood and the 
pleading result:
  - **Weregild** (§VI, weregild.json ladder) — the standard settlement; ends the feud only if 
accepted before witnesses with a feast of reconciliation.
  - **Outlawry** (see below).
  - **Reconciliation** — sealed with gifts and a feast; both parties gain +1 Orðstír (the 
district saw peace made).
- If the pleading ended 3–0 or 3–1 (decisive), the judgment follows the winner. If it ended 
3–2 (close), the law-speaker may split the difference — partial weregild, or weregild with a 
public apology.

**Outlawry grades.**
- *Fjörbaugsgarðr* (lesser): **exile for three years** — may not be fed or sheltered at home; 
property held (not forfeit); −3 Orðstír until lawful return; may return after time served.
- *Skóggangr* (full, "the wolf's outlawry"): **killable by anyone without weregild**; **none may 
feed or shelter** them on pain of sharing the sentence; **goods forfeit**; name struck from the 
community's frith. A PC under full outlawry **loses all Orðstír** and gains the status *vargr* 
(wolf).
- Lifting: lesser ends by time served; full ends only by pardon, a worse outlaw's lawful death, or 
a saga-ending deed (see Episode 22, Outlawry).

**Þingfriðr — the Thing's peace.** **Weapons are peace-bound** in the circle. Drawing steel in 
the circle is a **grave frith-breach** (§II): the drawer's suit is forfeit immediately, and the 
assembly may declare lesser outlawry on the spot. The law protects itself first.

**Edge cases.**
- A party that **fails to appear** forfeits by default — the suit proceeds with the present party 
pleading unopposed (automatic 3 successes), and the absent party is judged as if guilty.
- **Women** plead at the Thing through a male kinsman or appointed spokesman in most districts 
⚠️ (practice varied; the GM decides the district's custom — a woman pleading in her own voice 
where custom forbids it is bold, not illegal, and the crowd-mood starts at −1 for the breach of 
custom).
- **Feud-heat:** if the suit concerns a killing and the feud-temperature is Burning (Episode 20), 
the GM may rule the crowd-mood starts at +2 toward vengeance — the district is already angry.

#### At an OSE Table

- The four phases and the crowd-mood tracker are **identical** — procedure is procedure in any 
century (`SYSTEM_VARIANTS.md` Ep. 14).
- **Announcement:** Charisma (Performance) becomes a **2d6 reaction roll**; a well-spoken charge 
grants **+2 on the pleading rolls** (advantage's OSE analogue).
- **Oath:** oath-helpers of good Orðstír weigh exactly as codex — +1 each on the first pleading 
roll (max +3).
- **Pleading:** catch lies → **roll 1d20 ≤ Wisdom**; move the crowd → **2d6 reaction roll**; 
cite precedent → **roll 1d20 ≤ Intelligence**. Deception → **1d20 ≤ Charisma**, harder by 
+2 (the lie is a heavier lift). Three successes before three failures, crowd-mood tracker identical.
- **Judgment and outlawry:** as codex §VII; additionally **no hirelings will serve you and no 
stronghold will take you in** under full outlawry.
- Drawing steel in the circle: grave frith-breach (*þingfriðr*) — the law protects itself 
first, either system.

#### Worked Example

*Illustrative only — hypothetical rolls, not a real session.* At the spring Thing, Hrafn accuses 
Eirik of killing his brother. **Announcement:** Hrafn's player speaks the charge in formal words 
— rolls Charisma (Performance): a roll of 14 + 3 = 17 beats DC 15. Advantage banked. **Oath:** 
Eirik swears with four oath-helpers of Orðstír 6+ — but one of them, the GM notes silently, 
knows Eirik is lying. **Pleading:** Hrafn opens with Insight to catch the false oath-helper — a 
roll of 12 + 4 = 16 beats DC 15; the helper stammers, the crowd-mood swings +2 to Hrafn, and 
Eirik's side takes a failure. The challenge runs to 3–2 for Hrafn — close. **Judgment:** the 
law-speaker splits it — full weregild for the brother (12 marks, the karl's price), paid 
publicly, plus a feast of reconciliation. Eirik pays. The feud ends. And the GM's silent note about 
the false oath sits in the campaign record, accruing Wyrd-debt, until wyrd collects.

**The law-speaker's script.** The GM speaks the judgment in the old form — naming the law, the 
finding, and the sentence, each as its own sentence: *"The law says a killing unatoned is outlawry 
or weregild. The Thing finds Eirik the killer. The sentence is twelve marks, paid before witnesses, 
with a feast of reconciliation."* The formality is the point: law spoken plainly, where all can 
hear it, is law nobody can later claim to have misunderstood.

**Counter-suits.** The accused may, in Phase 3, open a **counter-suit** ("he struck first," "the 
níð was false") — this runs as a second pleading inside the first, with its own 3-before-3 
challenge, argued in the same exchanges (each side's exchange may address either suit). If the 
counter-suit succeeds decisively (3–0 or 3–1), it *absorbs* the original: the accuser becomes 
the judged. Njáls saga runs on exactly this engine — suits eating suits until the procedure 
itself is the battlefield.

**The Thing's calendar.** The great Althing meets at midsummer; district Things meet in spring and 
autumn. A suit announced at the autumn Thing may be *deferred* to spring — and deferral is a 
weapon: the accused gains a winter of *plotting* (§XII), the accuser's witnesses may die, flee, or 
be bought. The GM should offer deferral as a tactical choice, not a delay.

### III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

The Thing teaches the single most alien fact about the Viking Age: *law without a state*. There are 
no police, no prisons, no king's men — only procedure, witnesses, and the crowd. Players who are 
used to "the guards handle it" discover that *they* are the guards, the jury, and the enforcement, 
all at once. The pleading-as-skill-challenge teaches rhetoric as a survival skill: the player who 
can argue, cite precedent, and read a room is literally more powerful than the player with the 
bigger sword — inside the circle, anyway. And the crowd-mood tracker makes the deepest lesson 
visible: justice here is not blind. It *watches*. It is swayed, it remembers, it can be moved — 
and a verdict the district doesn't believe in is a verdict nobody enforces. Law was procedure, and 
procedure was theater, and theater needed an audience that bought the ending.

## The Seasonal Wheel — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 15)

**Video:** `videos/ep15-seasonal-wheel.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep15-seasonal-wheel.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §XII` — this file expands that section; additions the codex 
leaves open are labeled.

### I. Grounding — Why It Is True

The Norse year really did turn on two hinges. Winter closed the sea and opened the long indoor 
season — craft, feasting, storytelling, lawsuits, and plotting; summer opened the sea for 
*víking*, trade, and exploration. The blóts marked the hinges: Yule at midwinter — the great 
feasting season, when kings held court and the drinking was deepest [Heim] — with the Dísablót 
toward spring and the Sigrblót, the victory-blót, before the summer's sailing ⚠️ (exact 
calendrics of the pre-Christian blóts are debated; the *names* and the *seasonal logic* are 
attested, the dates are tradition, not fact).

The crucial correction the system teaches: the *víkingr* was a summer profession, the farmer a 
year-round identity. Farming fed everyone; raiding fed no one in February. The sagas are full of 
men who went a-freebooting in summer and came home in autumn — Egil's saga shows the rhythm 
plainly: *"Many summers they were out a-freebooting, but the winters they spent"* ashore [Eg]. 
Winter was not downtime. It was the season of the Thing, the wedding, the lawsuit, the 
debt-collection, and the skald's training.

What is Volmarr's homebrew: the two-turn structure itself (Winter/Summer as game turns), the 
season-rite at the table, the automatic failure of seafaring in winter, the four winter-works and 
four summer-farings as a closed menu. History gives the rhythm; the turns make the table *march* to 
it.

### II. The Complete Playable Rule

#### At a 5E Table

**The turn.** Campaign time advances in **seasonal turns**: *Winter* (roughly November–March) and 
*Summer* (April–October). The GM advances the turn with a short **season-rite** at the table: 
each player, in turn, answers aloud — *"What did you do all winter?"* / *"What did you do all 
summer?"* — one or two sentences, witnessed. The rite is the record: what is spoken at the 
season-rite is what happened.

**Winter turn — the sea is closed.**
- **Seafaring checks automatically fail.** Not at disadvantage — *fail*. A ship that puts to sea 
in winter is lost (the GM narrates the wreck; survivors wash up where wyrd wills, see §XIII). 
⚠️ Exception: short coastal hops in sheltered fjords may be attempted at the GM's mercy — and 
the GM is encouraged to have none.
- Each PC chooses **one winter-work** (the season's labor; a PC may split the winter between two 
works but gains the full benefit of neither — half-measures are half-measured):
  - **Craft** — make something of real value: a sword, a poem, a ship's part, a hall's carving. 
Resolved with a relevant tool/ability check (DC 13 for good work, DC 17 for masterwork). A 
masterwork item grants +1 Orðstír when first shown publicly — the district sees the maker's 
hands.
  - **Hospitality** — host feasts through the dark months: spend silver (at least 5 marks for the 
season), gain **+1 Orðstír**; spend 10+ marks lavishly, gain +2. The Yule feast is the crown of 
it — whoever hosts the district's Yule gains an extra +1.
  - **Plotting** — advance a feud or prepare a suit for the spring Thing: gain **advantage on the 
first Pleading roll** (or one preparation roll) at the spring Thing; or move a feud's temperature 
one step in the direction you choose (Episode 20).
  - **Learning** — train with a master: seiðr, galdr, skaldcraft, a weapon, a craft. **This is 
the only time long training happens** — no skill or art advances meaningfully during the sailing 
season. One winter = one art advanced (the GM defines the advance: a new galdr rune-song, a skaldic 
form mastered, a weapon style learned).
- **Debts come due** (§VI): at the winter season-rite, every unanswered debt-token is reckoned — 
−1 Orðstír per token, the giver +1. Winter is when the ledger is balanced, before the Yule 
blót.
- **The Yule blót is held** (§IV): the district's great midwinter rite — minni-toasts, the 
braga-goblet, oaths sworn over the cup. A PC who *boasts* at Yule and fails pays the oathbreaker's 
price by spring.

**Summer turn — the sea opens.**
- Each PC declares a **summer-faring** (one per PC; the party may split — and often must, because 
someone has to farm):
  - ***Víking*** (raid) — resolved with the seafaring rules (§XIII); profit is silver and 
renown, cost is wyrd and enemies.
  - ***Kaup*** (trade voyage) — buy low where the district has surplus, sell where it is scarce; 
resolved with one Charisma (Persuasion) voyage-check per market (DC 13: profit 1d6 marks; DC 17+: 
profit 2d6 marks; failure: loss and a story).
  - ***Landnám*** (exploration/settlement) — sail for new shores; the GM's prepared unknown. The 
only faring with no fixed resolution — it is discovery, and discovery is wyrd-shaped.
  - ***Heim*** (stay and farm) — **honorable, and someone must.** The farm is resolved with a 
**single season-check**: the farming PC rolls Wisdom (Survival) — DC 13.
    - **Success (good harvest):** wealth (+2d6 marks of surplus) and **+1 Orðstír as a provider** 
— the district eats because of you.
    - **Failure (bad harvest):** hunger. The household eats short all winter: −1 Hamingja for the 
farming PC, and the GM gains one hard choice to offer ("the seed-corn or the winter — choose").
    - **Failure by 5+ (famine):** hunger *and* −1 Orðstír — a provider who cannot provide is 
diminished before witnesses.
- A PC who sails on *víking* or *kaup* while their own farm goes untended rolls the harvest at 
**disadvantage** — the land knows it was abandoned. Hiring farm-hands (2 marks for the season) 
removes the disadvantage but not the check.

**The teaching engine.** The turn structure *forces* the rhythm:
- Raid in winter → the sea takes the ship. No roll, no heroics — the culture knew this the way 
it knew fire burns.
- Never farm → the harvest check eventually fails → hunger → hard choices. The table learns 
that every raid is weighed against the harvest left untended.
- Never learn in winter → the arts do not advance. The skald who sails every summer is still 
reciting the same three verses at forty.

**Year-tracking.** The GM keeps a visible year-count (winters are how the North counts age — a 
man is "thirty winters"). Oaths with deadlines, debts, feud-temperatures, and outlawry sentences 
(Episode 22) all tick on the seasonal turn. A three-year outlawry is six turns. Time is the one 
currency no one can hoard.

#### At an OSE Table

- The two-turn year and the season-rite are **identical** — the calendar does not care what dice 
you roll (`SYSTEM_VARIANTS.md` Ep. 15).
- **Winter:** seafaring checks automatically fail in either system; winter-work (craft, 
hospitality, plotting, learning) resolved at the table exactly as codex.
- **Craft check:** **1d20 ≤ the relevant ability** (DC 13 → standard; DC 17 → shift +4 
against, i.e. harder). Masterwork shown publicly: +1 Orðstír.
- **Hospitality:** silver spent, Orðstír gained — system-neutral.
- **Plotting:** advantage on the first pleading → **+2 on the pleading roll** at the spring Thing.
- **Summer-faring:** *víking*, *kaup*, *landnám*, *heim* — system-neutral choices; raiding 
resolves through OSE's own naval and morale rules (§XIII); the *kaup* voyage-check is a **2d6 
reaction roll** (9+ profit 1d6 marks in gp; 11+ profit 2d6; 6 or less: loss and a story).
- **Farming:** the single season-check is **1d20 ≤ Wisdom**; untended farm → roll with **+2 
against** (disadvantage's analogue); farm-hands (2 marks/gp) remove the penalty but not the check. 
Good harvest / hunger / famine stakes identical.
- Year-tracking in winters: identical — outlawry sentences and feud-temperatures tick on the turn 
either system.

#### Worked Example

*Illustrative only — hypothetical rolls, not a real session.* The campaign opens at the autumn 
season-rite. **Winter:** Astrid chooses *learning* — she spends the winter with the old völva, 
and by spring she knows her first true seiðr-trance (Episode 16/24). Brand chooses *hospitality*, 
spending 6 marks on feasts: +1 Orðstír, and he hosts the district Yule for another +1. Ketill 
chooses *plotting* — his feud with the neighbors is Warm, and he spends the winter gathering 
witnesses for the spring Thing (+2 on his first pleading roll, OSE table). His two unanswered 
debt-tokens come due: −2 Orðstír, the givers +1 each. The ledger is balanced before Yule. 
**Summer:** Astrid declares *heim* — someone must farm — and rolls the harvest: a roll of 9 ≤ 
her Wisdom 13 — good harvest, +2d6 marks surplus, +1 Orðstír as provider. Brand and Ketill sail 
*víking*; Ketill's farm, untended, will roll at +2 against in autumn — the land knows. The year 
has a shape now, and every choice has a price the table can see.

**The year-sheet.** The GM keeps one visible sheet per campaign year: the year's number (counted in 
winters), each PC's winter-work and summer-faring, debts reckoned, oaths sworn and their deadlines, 
feud-temperatures, outlawry sentences ticking down. When a new year begins at the autumn 
season-rite, the GM reads the old year's line aloud — *"In the winter of the wolf, these things 
were done"* — and the table hears its own saga accumulating. This is the cheapest epic-making 
tool in the system.

**Aging.** Men are counted in winters. Every **ten winters** of campaign time, each PC makes a 
single aging check (Constitution, DC 10 + 1 per decade past thirty): failure means the body keeps a 
tally — the GM assigns one lasting mark (a bad knee: −5 ft. speed in snow; failing eyes: 
disadvantage on far Perception; old wound: −1 on one physical check per day, player's choice 
which). The North respected old men and buried them; the mechanics should make age *felt*, not 
feared.

**The blót calendar (⚠️ tradition, not fact).** The hinges of the year carry rites the table 
may play: **Yule** at midwinter (the great feast, minni-toasts, braga-goblet oaths); the 
**Dísablót** toward spring (the disir honored, the women's rite, the year's protection asked); 
the **Sigrblót** before the summer sailing (victory asked, the ships blessed). Each blót properly 
held grants the district's PCs **+1 Hamingja** for the coming season — the gods answer in luck, 
never in miracles. Skipping a blót the district expects: −1 Hamingja until the next hinge. The 
gods are not vending machines; they are neighbors, and neighbors notice.

### III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

The Seasonal Wheel is the system's quietest and most radical lesson: it makes the Viking Age *stop 
being about Vikings*. The turn structure forces every player, every year, to answer the farmer's 
question — who tends the land? — before the raider's question. Players who arrived wanting 
"pirates forever" find themselves budgeting seed-corn, dreading the harvest roll, and choosing 
*heim* while their friends sail — and discovering that the +1 Orðstír *as a provider* feels as 
good as any raid's silver. The winter-work menu teaches the second truth: winter was not the 
off-season, it was the *other* season — the season of craft, law, learning, and debt, when the 
year's real business got done indoors. By the third year of campaign time, the players don't need 
to be told that raiding was a summer profession. Their bodies know it — because their 
*characters'* winters were full, and their summers were always, always weighed against the harvest.

## Seiðr, Galdr, Trolldom — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 16)

**Video:** `videos/ep16-seidr-galdr-trolldom.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep16-seidr-galdr-trolldom.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §V` — this file expands that section; additions the codex
leaves open are labeled.

### I. Grounding — Why It Is True

The threefold division is not a game convenience — it is how the sources present magic. Seiðr is the
art of the *völva* and, in the mythic register, of Freyja herself: Völuspá's seeress declares she
knew the art, and the tradition ties it to trance, chanting, and the high seat from which the witch
spoke [PE: Völuspá]. For men it was poisonously gendered. Loki's taunt to Óðinn in *Lokasenna* —
*"with spells in Samsey once, like witches with charms didst thou work… unmanly thy soul must seem"*
— shows that even the All-father could be shamed for it [PE: Lokasenna 24]. The historical register
is grimmer: King Haraldr burned his own son Ragnvald with eighty warlocks, "which work was much
praised" [Heim] — male witchcraft could be answered with fire. The ergi-taint cost in this system is
Volmarr's mechanical translation of that recorded dread.

Galdr, by contrast, is the *respectable* art: runes carved *and sung*, protection and healing and
victory, the province of chieftains and goðar. Sigrdrífa's rune-catalogue — victory-runes,
ale-runes, birth-runes, wave-runes — describes each rune as a practical working with rules of use
[PE: Sigrdrífumál]. And the myth gives galdr its deepest law: Óðinn hung nine nights on the tree and
*paid* for the runes with himself — magic-as-price-paid is the foundation of the art [PE: Hávamál
138–141]. The three steps of Volmarr's *Galdrabók* — **Intent → Ritual → Incantation** — are his
codification of that principle: nothing here is free, silent, or instant.

Trolldom, the hearth-magic of wards and workings, is drawn from Volmarr's own trolldom chart
(purification *hreinsa*, wards *hlífðarverk*, road-opening *vegaopnun*, prosperity charms) — that
chart is canon for this table, and the domestic scale of its effects is deliberate: the wise-woman's
art protected the farm, not the battlefield. The absolute taboo on coercion, with its
fylgja-backlash and Wyrd-debt accrual, is Volmarr's system-wide law (§III, §XVIII) — historically
grounded in the culture's terror of the hidden knife and the binding of wills, mechanically his
invention.

### II. The Complete Playable Rule

#### At a 5E Table

**Shared law — Intent → Ritual → Incantation (all three arts).** No silent, instant casting, ever.
Every working requires all three steps, declared in order: the practitioner states intent aloud
(even if alone), performs the ritual (minutes to hours, per the art), and speaks/sings the
incantation (audible — whispered if secrecy is the intent, but never silent). Materials are required
and consumed or dedicated: carved staves, herbs, and a real offering (silver of real weight, blood,
or a thing of value — see Blót §IV for the gift-logic). Witnesses *or* deliberately chosen solitude:
a working done in hiding gains nothing extra, and a hidden working discovered is judged exactly as
harshly as a public one — secrecy buys no protection from the consequences.

**The coercion taboo (absolute).** Any working that overrides, binds, or coerces another's will —
love-binding, thought-reading against resistance, compelled obedience, cursing a named target — is a
taboo act. Mechanically:

- **All coercive magic accrues +1 Wyrd-debt** (§III), success or failure. The debt is silent and cumulative; at 5, the Norns come calling.
- **The caster makes a Wisdom saving throw** (DC set by the severity of the coercion: 12 for a minor compulsion, 15 for binding a will, 18 for soul-level violation). On failure, the working **rebounds** — the GM describes the fylgja's price. Rebound effects are never trivial: the compulsion turns on the caster, the target's fetch-guardian visits the caster in dream and takes something (a point of Hamingja, a memory, a scar), or the gods answer with an omen of disfavor.
- **The target may also resist** with a Wisdom save (DC = 8 + caster's proficiency + caster's Charisma or Wisdom modifier) — but the target's resistance does not cancel the caster's taboo save. Coercing and failing is still coercing.
- **Edge cases:** coercing a willing volunteer (a healing trance entered freely) is not coercion. Coercing an animal or beast is minor — DC 12, no Wyrd-debt unless the beast is someone's sworn companion. Coercing the already-dead (§XXV) is grave — DC 18, always +1 Wyrd-debt. Group coercion (binding many wills at once) raises the DC by 2 per additional target beyond the first.

**Seiðr — the witch's art.** Not a class feature; it is *learned* from a practitioner at great cost:
a quest or service for the teacher, a worthy payment (no less than a season's keep), and weeks of
study. The learner emerges with the art but not the standing.

- **Effects:** divination (the future, answered in riddles — coordinate with §XXIV, never a plan), fate-nudging (one ally gains advantage on one roll within the next day, chosen by the seiðr-worker), weather-working (a storm hesitates or a wind rises — one step on the GM's weather scale), spirit-counsel (a brief converse with a summoned spirit or the dead, who answer in their own idiom), cursing (a named target suffers −1 Hamingja until rites of cleansing — cursing is coercion and always triggers the taboo above).
- **The trance** requires the high seat (*seiðhjallr* — raised platform), at least two chanting assistants, and a full hour. Interrupting the trance breaks it; the working is lost and the materials spent.
- **The ergi-taint (men):** each *public* seiðr-working costs −1 Orðstír — the community sees, and the community remembers — or −3 if mocked in flyting afterward (§X). A *private* working costs nothing unless discovered (and secrets at a Thing have a way of surfacing). A man whom the community already honors as ***seiðmaðr*** is exempt: this is a GM-granted title, earned after at least three honored workings acknowledged publicly, and it is itself saga-worthy status. **Women practitioners** (*völva*, *seiðkona*) are exempt from the taint entirely: true prophecies earn +1 Orðstír each, but NPCs keep their distance — hospitality is given warily (§VIII), and invitations are politely rare.

**Galdr — the rune-songs.** The "wizardry" of this world, deliberately not D&D wizardry. Each
practitioner begins with **3 rune-songs** and learns more only from teachers or grimoires — one week
of study per song, and the teacher names their price. Each song = one rune + one narrow effect.
Casting = carve the rune (a material focus: wood, stone, or flesh — the carving takes the full
minute), chant aloud (audible, 1 minute), and make a **Charisma or Wisdom check** (the caster's
choice): DC 12 for common workings, DC 15 for strong ones, DC 18 for the exceptional. A song may be
sung once per target per day — the runes do not repeat themselves for the impatient.

- ***Týr* (victory in lawful combat):** one ally gains advantage on their first attack roll of the next fight, provided the fight is lawful (holmgang, open battle — not ambush).
- ***Bjarkan* (healing):** one wounded creature regains 1d6 + caster's Wisdom modifier hit points. Once per creature per day.
- ***Naud* (enduring hardship):** one ally gains advantage on their next Constitution save against cold, hunger, or exhaustion.
- ***Sól* (sight):** the caster sees truly through one deception or darkness for the next hour — advantage on one Wisdom (Insight) or (Perception) check.
- ***Fé* (prosperity):** a farm, ship, or venture gains advantage on its next seasonal or voyage check (§XV) — the rune blesses honest work, not theft.
- ***Íss* (stilling):** one raging or panicked creature must make a Wisdom save (DC = caster's check result) or be calmed for 1 minute. Used on an unwilling intelligent target, this is coercion.
- **Effects are subtle and bounded:** no fireballs. A wound knits, a storm hesitates, a foe's courage falters. Galdr is socially *respectable* — goðar practice it openly, and no Orðstír cost attaches to honest rune-singing.

**Trolldom — the hearth art.** Anyone may learn it; the wise-woman's and householder's craft. Small
workings, small costs — herbs, a smoking bowl, a carved door-ward, an hour of the evening:

- **Purification smoking (*hreinsa*):** a dwelling is cleansed of ill-feeling; all within gain advantage on their next save against fear or disease within the day.
- **Ward-stave (*hlífðarverk*):** carved over a door or cradle; the first hostile act against the warded place within a week suffers disadvantage (GM's call on what counts — the ward is not a wall).
- **Prosperity bowl (*gæfa*):** set out at the year's turning; the household gains advantage on one seasonal check that year (§XV).
- **Untroubled sleep:** one night of dreamless rest — no draumr rolls (§XXIII), for good or ill, and one level of exhaustion may be shaken off without a long rest's full benefit.
- **Cost is material and time, never spiritual** — but a trolldom-worker who meddles in seiðr's business (curses, love-coercion, spirit-binding) is **judged as a seiðr-worker**: the coercion taboo, the ergi-taint, and the community's fear all apply in full.

#### At an OSE Table

- **Casting law identical:** Intent → Ritual → Incantation, minutes to hours, materials, witnesses or chosen solitude.
- **Coercion:** the caster makes a **Save vs Spells** (with a −2 penalty for soul-level violations, −1 for binding a will; minor compulsions unmodified). On failure the working rebounds — the fylgja's price, described by the GM. The target's resistance is a **Save vs Spells** against the caster. All coercion accrues **+1 Wyrd-debt**, identical to 5E.
- **Seiðr identical:** learned, not class-granted; the hour-long trance with high seat and chanting assistants; the male ergi-taint (−1 Orðstír per public working, −3 if flyting-mocked; *seiðmaðr* exemption); *völva* gains and wary hospitality. Fate-nudging grants **−2 on one roll-under check** within the next day.
- **Galdr:** 3 rune-songs at start; carve + chant aloud 1 minute + **1d20 ≤ Charisma or Wisdom** (common workings at the score, strong at −2, exceptional at −4). *Týr*: one ally rolls their first attack twice, keeping better; *Bjarkan*: heal 1d6 + Wisdom modifier hp, once per creature per day; *Naud*: −2 on the next save vs cold, hunger, exhaustion; *Sól*: −2 on one check to pierce deception; *Fé*: −2 on one seasonal/voyage check; *Íss*: target Saves vs Spells or is calmed 1 turn (unwilling = coercion). Subtle and bounded, exactly as codex.
- **Trolldom identical:** purification (one reroll on a save vs fear or disease that day — or +2, table's choice); ward-stave (the first hostile act within a week is made at −2); prosperity bowl (−2 on one seasonal check); untroubled sleep (no draumr, shake one exhaustion level). Meddling in seiðr's business = judged as a seiðr-worker, either system.
- Full dual-system reference: `SYSTEM_VARIANTS.md` (Appendix — Codex §V).

#### Worked Example

A 5E example (illustrative). A PC, a man of the district, seeks out a *seiðkona* to learn
weather-working — he wants to still a storm threatening the fleet. He serves her a season as agreed
(the learning cost, played out), and she teaches him the art. When the storm comes, he sets up the
high seat on the shore with two chanting assistants and works the trance for an hour (witnesses: the
whole fleet's crews). The storm hesitates — the GM gives the fleet advantage on its storm-saves. But
the working was *public*, and he is a man: −1 Orðstír for the ergi-taint (someone mutters in the
hall that night). Later, a rival mocks him in flyting for "witch's work" — the table judges the
mockery landed, so −3 instead of −1. Three such honored workings, acknowledged before the district,
and the GM may name him *seiðmaðr* — after which the taint no longer attaches. At an OSE table the
same scene plays identically, with the trance and the costs unchanged.

### III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

Players learn that magic had a **social price tag** and a **gendered shape** — and that the most
powerful magic in the Norse world was the kind a wise man was *ashamed* to practice. The ergi
mechanic teaches more about Norse masculinity in one session than any lecture, because it is not a
lecture: it is a −1 on a public sheet, witnessed by the table, and the player feels the culture's
hand. They also learn the Galdrabók's deeper lesson — that power here is *never* free, silent, or
instant — and they learn to fear the coercion taboo the way the culture feared it: not as a rule,
but as a debt that follows you home.

## Death & the Afterlife — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 17)

**Video:** `videos/ep17-death-afterlife.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep17-death-afterlife.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §XIV` — this file expands that section; additions the codex
leaves open are labeled.

### I. Grounding — Why It Is True

The Norse did not share one afterlife — they shared a **geography of the dead** [SnE; PE:
Grímnismál]. The battle-slain chosen by Óðinn go to **Valhöll** — spear-raftered, shield-roofed,
*"five hundred doors and forty there are, eight hundred fighters through one door fare"* when they
go to war with the wolf [PE: Grímnismál 23]. Freyja takes her chosen to **Fólkvangr** — *"the ninth
is Folkvang, where Freyja decrees"* the seating of the slain [PE: Grímnismál 14]. The drowned fall
into **Rán's net** — and sailors' offerings to Rán are an attested custom, which is why the codex
gives them a mechanic. The ordinary dead — the straw-dead, the sick, the old — rest in **Helheim**:
not a place of punishment but a vast, quiet realm of diminished existence. How you died *routed*
you; your funeral rites *equipped* you.

The funeral forms are attested, not invented. Ynglinga saga remembers two ages of the dead: the
**Age of Burning** (cremation, ashes under standing stones) and the **Age of Cairns** (mound burial
with goods, horse, armor) — the dead were *sent off with their tools* [Heim]. This is why the codex
insists the dead *take* their share of the party's wealth: the goods laid with the body are
equipment for the journey, and withholding them is withholding the journey. The darkest routing —
oathbreakers and *níðingar* to **Náströnd**, the corpse-shore — comes from Völuspá's own blackest
image [PE: Völuspá 38], and the codex rightly presents it as ⚠️ priestly warning rather than
verified geography. The goði's certainty about all of this in-world is Volmarr's teaching device:
the table *believes* absolutely, because the culture did.

### II. The Complete Playable Rule

#### At a 5E Table

**Death rites are played, not skipped.** When a character dies, the table holds a short funeral —
burning (Age of Burning) or mounding (Age of Cairns), the district's custom or the dead's stated
wish deciding. The procedure, in order:

1. **The laying-out:** the body is prepared — washed, dressed in the best clothes, weapons and tools laid beside it. This takes an hour of game time and is done by kin or companions; skipping it is the first cut against the rites.
2. **The goods:** the dead *take* their share. Record what is laid with them — at minimum their weapon and armor, plus **10% of their personal wealth** (silver, treasure, trade goods) — and remove it from the party's wealth. This is not a tax; it is equipment for the road. A character buried without their tools arrives unarmed, and the goði will say so plainly.
3. **The sending:** burning on a pyre (ashes under a standing stone or scattered on the wind) or mounding (a cairn raised, goods within). Either way, the rite takes half a day and the whole party attends — attendance is part of the rite.
4. **The remembrance goblet:** at the next blót (§IV), a minni-cup is drunk to the dead, with true words spoken over it (§XIX: minni kept grants +1 Hamingja to those who drink truly).

**Skipping the rites** risks the dead walking: *draugr* are not monsters here — they are
**unfuneraled obligations** (§II's *dauðrafriðr*, frith with the dead). If the table skips any step
above, the GM rolls secretly: **1–2 on 1d6**, the dead walks (per §XXV: a wight or ghast stat block,
reskinned — it has *business*, and it cannot be laid until the business is settled *and* the rites
are performed). Even a skipped remembrance goblet leaves the dead restless — dreams of the
unremembered (§XXIII) until the cup is drunk.

**Routing — told by the goði, believed absolutely.** The player does not choose; the *manner of death* routes:

- **Slain in battle, weapon in hand, facing the foe** → the valkyries choose between **Valhöll** and **Fólkvangr**. The choosing is the GM's secret — the table never knows which, and it doesn't matter. *Chosen* matters: a warrior who fell bravely but whose death was witnessed as cowardly (fleeing, begging) is not routed here — the valkyries watch the *manner*, not just the field.
- **Drowned at sea** → **Rán's net**. Feared. A silver offering to Rán before sailing (at least **one ounce of silver**, cast overboard with a spoken word) grants **advantage on one storm-save** during that voyage — one offering, one voyage, one save.
- **Straw-death, sickness, age** → **Helheim's quiet halls**. Not shameful for the old — the elder who dies in bed with kin around them is honored — but a young warrior's dread. A warrior who *chooses* the bed (refusing battle to die quietly) is judged by the culture as cowardice; route them to Helheim, and let the hall's silence say the rest.
- **Oathbreakers and *níðingar*** → **Náströnd**, the corpse-shore. Presented as priestly warning, not verified geography — the goði speaks of it to frighten the living into honor, and the table treats it as the culture treated it: a sermon, not a map. ⚠️
- **Edge cases:** death in holmgang (§XI) counts as battle-death — the duel is lawful combat, weapon in hand. Death by seiðr-curse is routed by *manner*, not cause — a cursed warrior who dies fighting still goes to Valhöll. Death at sea by violence (not drowning) is battle-death if weapon was in hand. Suicide: the culture gave it no honored routing — the goði is evasive, the table understands. Death by draugr or by the dead's business is its own warning: attend to the rites.

**The good death (§III).** A PC who dies in battle, keeping oaths, protecting kin — weapon in hand,
facing the foe — earns a **saga-death**: the table composes a short verse of their deeds (or the GM
delivers one); **half their Orðstír** (rounded down) passes to their named heir or sworn companion
as starting Orðstír (§I); and the *player's next character* begins with **Inspiration and +2
Orðstír** — "born under a bright name." If a drápa was performed at the funeral (§XX), the heir
gains **+1 more** starting Orðstír — the poem is part of the burial goods. The table *celebrates* a
good death the way other tables celebrate a level-up. A PC who dies by cowardice, oathbreaking, or
straw-death (young) leaves nothing: no verse, no inheritance, no bright beginning — and the table
knows it.

#### At an OSE Table

- **The funeral rite identical** — laying-out, the dead's share (10% of wealth plus arms, recorded and leaving the party's wealth), burning or mounding, the remembrance goblet at the next blót (+1 Hamingja for those who drink truly, per §XIX).
- **Skipped rites identical:** GM rolls secretly, 1–2 on 1d6 the dead walks — use the OSE **wight or ghoul** entry, reskinned; it has *business* and cannot be laid until the business is settled *and* the rites are performed.
- **Routing identical**, believed absolutely in-world: Valhöll/Fólkvangr for the battle-slain (the valkyries' choosing — the GM's secret); Rán's net for the drowned; Helheim's quiet halls for the straw-dead; Náströnd as priestly warning for oathbreakers. Edge cases (holmgang = battle-death, cursed-but-fighting = Valhöll, the evasive suicide) identical.
- **Rán's offering identical:** one ounce of silver before sailing grants **+2 on one storm-save** (or a re-roll, table's choice) — one offering, one voyage, one save.
- **The good death identical:** saga-verse, half Orðstír to the heir (rounded down), funeral drápa adds +1 — and the player's next character begins with **one free reroll** (Inspiration's OSE form) **and +2 Orðstír**, born under a bright name. The table celebrates it either way.
- Full dual-system reference: `SYSTEM_VARIANTS.md` (Appendix — Codex §XIV).

#### Worked Example

A 5E example (illustrative). A PC warrior falls holding a bridge against raiders — weapon in hand,
facing the foe, kin behind her. The table holds the funeral: her sword and byrnie are laid with her,
10% of her silver goes into the mound (recorded; it leaves the party's wealth), and at the next blót
the party drinks the remembrance goblet with true words (+1 Hamingja each, per §XIX). The GM
secretly decides the valkyries' choosing — Valhöll or Fólkvangr, the table never learns which. The
skald performs a drápa at the mound. Her named heir begins with half her Orðstír (rounded down) +1
for the funeral poem; the player's next character begins with Inspiration and +2 Orðstír. Had the
party *skipped* the rites to chase the raiders, the GM would have rolled 1d6 in secret — on a 1 or
2, she walks, with business, until the rites are done. At an OSE table the same: the share leaves
the wealth, the heir's Orðstír is computed identically, and the next character gets the free reroll.

### III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

Death stops being failure and becomes **authorship**: the player writes their character's ending the
way the skalds wrote endings — and learns why a culture that believed this feared the bed more than
the battlefield. The funeral procedure teaches the economy of the dead (the dead *take* their share
— no hoarding against the journey), and the routing teaches the culture's values by geography:
courage goes up to the spear-raftered hall, ordinary life goes quietly down, and dishonor goes to
the corpse-shore the priests warn about. The celebration of the good death rewires the table's
instincts — which is exactly what the belief rewired in the culture.

## The Ergi Taboo — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 18)

**Video:** `videos/ep18-ergi.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep18-ergi.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §XVIII` — this file expands that section; additions the codex
leaves open are labeled.

### I. Grounding — Why It Is True

This section is not sanitized, because the source is not. In *Lokasenna*, Loki taunts Óðinn himself:
*"they said you practiced seiðr on Samsø, and beat on the drum as witches do — in the likeness of a
wizard you journeyed among men, and that I thought was ergi"* [PE: Lokasenna 24]. *Ergi* meant
unmanliness in its fullest, ugliest sense: cowardice in battle *and* sexual shame — the passive, the
yielding, the perverse as the culture defined it. It was the one accusation a man could not let
stand. The law treated the ergi-words (*ragr*, *argr*, and the acts they implied) as the deepest
insults known ⚠️ — the taboo's roots run older than the law-codes that record them (the 13th-century
codes are later than our period, but the dread is older).

Note the subtlety the sagas preserve: Óðinn practiced seiðr and *remained* Óðinn. The shame lay not
in the art but in *how and why* a man used it — for cowardly, coercive, underhand ends (§V). This
file treats ergi strictly as the culture's own taboo category — a historical fact about how the 9th
century policed masculinity — and keeps everything non-explicit: the acts are named as the culture
named them (fleeing, hiding, coercing, refusing the lawful fight), never lingered on.

### II. The Complete Playable Rule

#### At a 5E Table

**Ergi-accusation is a formal flyting charge** (§X). It may be leveled only **in public, before
witnesses, naming the act**. The chargeable acts, as the culture defined them:

- **Fleeing battle and leaving comrades** — witnessed flight, abandoning the shield-line.
- **Refusing holmgang when lawfully challenged** (§XI) — the lawful duel refused is cowardice made formal.
- **Using seiðr or trolldom to coerce rather than to counsel** (§V) — binding a will, cursing unseen, unmanning a rival by hidden means.
- **Striking from hiding what should be faced openly** — the night-knife, the ambush on an unsuspecting man, the poison in the cup.

A *vague* accusation — naming no act — is itself answerable: if the accusation is proven false or is
made without a named act, the **accuser** takes **1 níð-mark** for weaponizing the culture's deepest
shame (§XVII). The form protects the charge's gravity.

**The accused must answer within the scene** — by deed, by holmgang, or by counter-flyting:

- **By deed:** the accused meets the charge with action before the witnesses — takes up the challenge, rights the wrong openly, does the brave thing the accusation says he would not do. The GM and the table must agree the deed answers the charge; a token gesture is a weak answer.
- **By holmgang (§XI):** the accused demands the island-duel, there and then or at the appointed time. Demanding it *is* the answer — the fight's outcome is separate, but the courage to demand it washes the charge in the moment.
- **By counter-flyting (§X):** the accused answers word with word — best of three spoken exchanges, the table judging the stronger. The insult must be spoken aloud, in character; dice alone cannot speak it. If the accused's flyting is judged the stronger, the accusation dies. If it is the weaker, the penalties below apply *and* the accusation stands proven in the hearers' eyes.
- **Silence, flight, or a weak answer = 1 níð-mark + −2 Hamingja, immediately.** The table witnesses it; shame, like honor, needs an audience. There is no "later" — the scene *is* the court.

**A *proven* ergi-act** (witnessed cowardice, witnessed coercive magic — proof before the community,
not rumor): **1 níð-mark, −2 Orðstír, −1 Hamingja**, and the word follows the character — NPCs who
have heard it react with **open contempt until atonement**. Open contempt is mechanical: the marked
character has disadvantage on Charisma (Persuasion) checks with those who know, and no one will
stand as their oath-helper at the Thing.

**Atonement** (until atonement, the contempt stands): the marked character must perform a great deed
before witnesses, win holmgang vindication (§XI), or fulfill a sumbel-vow of conspicuous courage
(§XIX) — the table must witness the deed that washes the word, and the GM sets the price in blood,
silver, or poetry (§XVII). Atonement removes the contempt and *one* níð-mark; further marks require
further deeds.

**The seiðr nuance (§V).** The table — and the GM, *publicly* — judges intent, because the culture
did. A man practicing seiðr for **prophecy, healing, or counsel at need** suffers **no ergi-taint**;
a man using it to **unman a rival, bind another's will, or strike unseen** *does*. Practical
guidance for the judgment:

- Divining the fleet's weather before sailing: no taint.
- Healing a wounded kinsman by trance: no taint.
- Spirit-counsel sought openly for the district's good: no taint.
- Cursing a named rival from the high seat: taint — and the coercion taboo (§V) on top of it.
- Binding a rival's courage before a duel: taint — and cowardice besides.
- The GM announces the judgment aloud, with the reasoning, in front of the table. No private rulings on public shame.

**Who may accuse, and where.** Any free person may level the charge — it is not the privilege of
rank — but it must be public: a hall, a feast, a Thing, a battlefield with witnesses. An accusation
whispered in private is gossip, not law, and treating it as law is itself níð-worthy. Women of the
community may level it too — and a man mocked for *being accused by a woman* has no recourse but
answering; the culture's cruelty here is the lesson.

#### At an OSE Table

- The ergi-accusation's form is **identical** — public, before witnesses, naming the act; a vague or false accusation earns the **accuser** 1 níð-mark, either system.
- Answer within the scene — by deed, by holmgang (§XI), or by a stronger counter-flyting — or take **1 níð-mark and −2 Hamingja**, immediately; níð-marks, Orðstír, and Hamingja are system-neutral currencies.
- Counter-flyting at an OSE table: best of three, each round both roll **1d20 ≤ Charisma**, the insults spoken aloud in character; the table judges the stronger — the dice support the words, they do not replace them.
- A proven ergi-act: **1 níð-mark, −2 Orðstír, −1 Hamingja**, with open NPC contempt until atonement — **identical**; contempt = **−2 on reaction rolls** with those who know, and no oath-helper will stand for the marked.
- Atonement **identical**: a great deed before witnesses, holmgang vindication, or a sumbel-vow of conspicuous courage — witnessed by the table, priced by the GM; removes the contempt and one níð-mark.
- The seiðr nuance and the GM's *public* judgment of intent are **identical** at an OSE table — the customs officers work every shift. The practical guidance (prophecy/healing/counsel = no taint; unmanning/binding/striking unseen = taint) carries over unchanged.
- Full dual-system reference: `SYSTEM_VARIANTS.md` (Appendix — Codex §XVIII).

#### Worked Example

A 5E example (illustrative). In the hall, a rival stands and charges a PC warrior with ergi before
the whole feast: he names the act — "you fled the shield-line at Hrafnfjörðr and left your brother's
son to die." The accused must answer *now*. He chooses counter-flyting: three rounds of spoken
insults, each side rolling Charisma (Performance). Say the accused rolls 14 + 5 = 19 against the
rival's 8 + 3 = 11 in the deciding round, and the table judges his final verse the stronger — the
accusation dies, and the rival's standing takes the wound instead. Had the accused stayed silent, he
would have taken 1 níð-mark and −2 Hamingja on the spot, and the hall would have remembered. Had
witnesses *seen* him flee, the charge would be proven regardless of words: 1 níð-mark, −2 Orðstír,
−1 Hamingja, and open contempt until he atones — perhaps by demanding holmgang against the next man
who repeats it. At an OSE table, the flyting rounds are 1d20 ≤ Charisma, and the penalties are
identical.

### III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

Players encounter a moral world that is not theirs — where courage is gendered, where *how* you
fight matters as much as *whether* you win, and where the worst thing is not cruelty but *shameful*
cruelty. The discomfort is the lesson: the 9th century is a foreign country, and the mechanics are
its customs officers. The accusation procedure teaches something subtler still — that this culture
*formalized* its cruelty. Ergi was not a slur thrown in anger; it was a legal charge with forms,
witnesses, and defenses, and the table learns to handle it the way the hall did: publicly,
seriously, and at their peril.

## Skaldcraft — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 19)

**Video:** `videos/ep19-skaldcraft.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep19-skaldcraft.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §XX` — this file expands that section; additions the codex
leaves open are labeled.

### I. Grounding — Why It Is True

The skald was not an entertainer — he was a **mint**. A **drápa**, the formal praise-poem with its
refrain (*stef*), made a chieftain's deeds permanent and portable: in a world without written
histories, the poem *was* the record, and a king without a skald was a king the future would forget.
The reverse was equally true: **níðvísur**, scorn-verses, could flay a reputation alive — poetry as
a weapon with legal standing (§XVII). The saga proof is Egill Skalla-Grímsson: condemned to die by
King Eiríkr Bloodaxe, he saved his own head in a single night by composing *Höfuðlausn* —
"Head-Ransom" — a drápa so magnificent the king let him live [Eg]. Poetry was legal tender in the
economy of renown, and the codex's head-ransom rule is its one built-in miracle *because the saga
records it*.

This is also why the system demands the player **speak the stanza aloud** at the table: the poem's
power lived in performance, in breath and hearing. The saga economy ran on *heard* words — Hávamál's
own law, *"a noble name will never die, if good renown one gets"* [Háv 76–77]. The commission price
— "no less than a good sword" — is the culture's own valuation: a great poem was worth a great
weapon, because both won battles, one in the field and one in memory.

### II. The Complete Playable Rule

#### At a 5E Table

**Composing.** In downtime (one evening of work), or through the night before the Thing, the player
declares a subject and a kind — **drápa** (praise) or **níðvísa** (scorn) — and makes a **Charisma
(Performance)** check, **DC 15**. The player must speak **at least a full stanza aloud at the
table** (four lines minimum; kennings encouraged). Each apt kenning the GM accepts grants **+1 on
the check (maximum +2)** — the GM keeps a kenning-list as reward-bait, and hearing a good one is its
own pleasure. Aided composition: a second person may assist (advantage) by keeping the beat,
supplying kennings, or holding the mead — the sagas knew the skald's craft was sometimes a duet.
Success mints the poem as an **item**: *"Drápa for Jarl X, by [skald]"* — a trade good of the renown
economy, sellable, giftable, stealable (stealing a poem is theft of honor — answerable).

**Drápa effects:**

- The **subject** gains **+1 Orðstír** among all who hear the poem performed. The skald chooses where to perform it — halls, Things, feasts. A drápa may be performed in multiple communities: the subject gains the +1 **once per distinct community, up to +3 total per poem** — renown spreads, but a single poem's praise thins with distance.
- The **skald** gains **+1 Orðstír per notable patron praised (max +3 per poem)**. Patrons *pay* for this — in silver, gifts, or protection — and the GM should price a commissioned drápa at **no less than a good sword** (treat a good sword as a valuable martial weapon, roughly 50+ gp in trade value; the price is a floor, not a ceiling — kings paid farms).
- A drápa performed for a *fallen* warrior at his funeral (§XIV) grants his **heir +1 starting Orðstír** — the poem is part of the burial goods.
- **Edge cases:** a drápa praising one who holds **2+ níð-marks** is an insult to the hearers — the skald risks **−1 Orðstír** for praising the shamed (the hall will say so). A drápa for the dead of *another* district earns the skald friends there — the GM should note it. Two rival drápur for rival patrons in the same hall: perform both, and let the table judge — the loser of the evening's favor loses nothing mechanically, but the hall remembers.

**Níðvísa effects:**

- The **subject** loses **1 Orðstír** among all who hear it, and must answer — by flyting (§X), holmgang (§XI), or a **counter-poem** (resolved as a flyting exchange, §X) — or take a **níð-mark** for letting scorn stand (§XVII).
- A níðvísa composed **falsely** (the skald *knows* the charge is untrue) earns the **skald 1 níð-mark**: the weapon cuts both ways. The GM determines falsity by the skald's knowledge, not the world's — a skald who believes a lie he was told is a fool, not a liar, but the verse still wounds.
- **Answering by counter-poem** is the skald's proper duel: both compose, both perform, the table judges — winner's verse stands, loser's is laughed out of the hall (loser −1 Orðstír among the hearers).

**Head-ransom rule.** A condemned PC (or an NPC the party champions) may, as Egill did, compose
through the night before execution. One Performance check at **disadvantage** (no sleep, terror,
dawn coming), **DC 20** — kennings still grant their +1s (max +2), because even at the edge of death
the craft is the craft. Success: the judge **must hear the poem**, and any ruler of honor is **bound
by custom to spare the skald** who has so honored him — the GM plays this straight. A *dishonorable*
judge (Orðstír below 5, or 1+ níð-marks) may break custom — but doing so costs **him** −2 Orðstír
before all witnesses, because the hall sees what the custom is worth to him. This is the codex's
**one built-in miracle**, and it works **once per judgment** — there is no second dawn.

#### At an OSE Table

- **Composing:** **1d20 ≤ Charisma** (DC 15 in 5E terms); the player still speaks the full stanza aloud — kennings encouraged, and each apt kenning grants **−1 on the roll (maximum −2)**; the kenning-list is still reward-bait. An assistant grants **−2** (advantage's OSE form) by keeping the beat.
- A successful poem becomes the item *"Drápa for Jarl X, by [skald]"* — **identical**; commissioned drápur priced in **gp**, no less than a good sword (roughly 50+ gp in trade value — a floor, not a ceiling).
- **Drápa effects identical:** subject +1 Orðstír among hearers (once per distinct community, up to +3 per poem); skald +1 Orðstír per notable patron (max +3); funeral drápa grants the heir +1 starting Orðstír. Praising one with 2+ níð-marks risks the skald −1 Orðstír — identical.
- **Níðvísa effects identical:** subject −1 Orðstír among hearers, answer by flyting, holmgang, or counter-poem, or take a níð-mark; a false níðvísa earns the *skald* the níð-mark. Counter-poem duels resolved as flyting exchanges (§X), **1d20 ≤ Charisma** per round.
- **Head-ransom:** compose through the night; one check at **disadvantage** (roll twice, keep the worse) against a hard target — success binds the honorable judge to spare the skald, played straight; a dishonorable judge who breaks custom loses 2 Orðstír before the witnesses. Once per judgment — identical, exactly as codex.
- Full dual-system reference: `SYSTEM_VARIANTS.md` (Appendix — Codex §XX).

#### Worked Example

A 5E example (illustrative). During winter downtime, a PC skald composes a drápa for the jarl who
sheltered the party. She speaks four lines aloud at the table, working in two kennings the GM
accepts ("whale-road" for the sea, "battle-sweat" for blood) — that's +2 on her Charisma
(Performance) check. She rolls 14 + 5 + 2 = 21 against DC 15: the poem is minted as an item.
Performed at the jarl's Yule feast, the jarl gains +1 Orðstír among all who hear; performed again at
the spring Thing in a neighboring district, +1 there too (two communities, +2 of the +3 maximum).
The skald gains +1 Orðstír for the notable patron, and the jarl pays her a war-axe and silver — no
less than a good sword, as custom demands. Later, she turns the same craft to scorn: a níðvísa
against a oath-breaking rival, performed in his own hall. He loses 1 Orðstír among the hearers and
must answer by flyting, holmgang, or counter-poem — or wear the níð-mark. At an OSE table, the
composing roll is 1d20 ≤ Charisma with the kennings as −2; everything else identical.

### III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

Players learn why Viking kings kept poets the way modern states keep mints — and why insulting a
skald was dangerous. The party will start *commissioning* poems about themselves, which means they
start caring about being worth a poem about. The economy of praise does the teaching: honor becomes
something you can *hear* moving through a hall, and the table learns, in its bones, that in this
world a verse could ransom a head and a stanza could kill a name. The spoken-stanza rule is the
deepest teaching of all — it makes the player *do* the thing the culture did, with their own breath.

## Feud & Revenge — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 20)

**Video:** `videos/ep20-feud.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep20-feud.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §XXI` — this file expands that section; additions the codex
leaves open are labeled.

### I. Grounding — Why It Is True

The feud is the saga's engine and the society's wound. *Njáls saga* is, at its heart, a manual of
how killings compound: a death demands weregild or blood; blood demands blood; each settlement that
fails raises the price — until the burning of Bergþórshvoll, where the whole ledger goes up in fire
[Njál]. Vengeance was not rage — it was **obligation**. A kinsman who did not pursue a killing was
himself shamed; the debt was owed to the kin-group, and the kin-group *was* the person. This is why
the codex records the debt against the kin-group rather than the killer: in this world, the family
was the legal unit.

But the system was not mere savagery — it *worked*, until it didn't. A feud settled honorably
(weregild paid in full, at the Thing, before witnesses) ended with handshakes and sometimes
marriage; the law's machinery of weregild, oath-helpers, and public settlement was a genuine peace
technology [Njál]. The codex's temperature ladder — Cool, Warm, Burning — is Volmarr's mechanical
translation of that observed arc: debts that cool when honored and burn when neglected. The hidden
knife's níð-mark is the culture's own verdict, not the game's: the sagas despise the secret killing
precisely because it evades the ledger — no announcement, no witnesses, no law.

### II. The Complete Playable Rule

#### At a 5E Table

**The debt.** Every **unlawful killing** — outside holmgang (§XI), open battle, or lawful execution
(Thing-sentenced, §VII) — creates a **blood-debt** against the killer's **kin-group**, owed to the
victim's **kin-group**. The kin-group = blood kin, foster-kin (§XXVI), and sworn household — the
people whose honor rises and falls with yours. Record every debt in the open ledger: ***who* (the
killer, named), *of whom* (the victim, named), *when* (the season), *witnessed by whom*.** The debt
has a **price** — the weregild ladder (§VI, `weregild.json`) — and a **temperature**:

- ***Cool* (negotiable):** the killing is known; the victim's kin have named their price or signaled willingness to talk. Messengers can travel between the groups.
- ***Warm* (insults exchanged, Thing demanded):** words have been said in public; one side or both have demanded the Thing take it up. Hospitality between the groups is withdrawn (§VIII). Travelers go armed.
- ***Burning* (raids, counter-killings, no talk):** messengers are turned away or killed; the groups strike at each other's people, farms, and cattle. No negotiation is possible until the temperature falls.
- ***Settled*:** weregild paid in full at the Thing, publicly. The debt is closed — it cannot be reopened for the same killing.

**Temperature rises** one step per month the debt goes unaddressed (no messenger sent, no Thing
demanded, no payment offered), or **immediately** upon any new insult, theft, or violence between
the groups. **Temperature falls** one step when both sides attend the Thing peacefully and speak to
the debt (even without settling — the forms of law cool the blood), or when a weregild payment is
made in proper public form (§VII).

**Edge cases on what creates a debt:**

- Killing in **holmgang, open battle, or lawful execution**: no debt. The law has already spoken.
- Killing a **thrall**: a debt of *property*, not blood — owed to the thrall's owner at the thrall's price (§VI), and it does not carry temperature; it is a fine, not a feud.
- Killing **by accident** (a hunting mishap, a shipboard mischance): the debt exists, but begins at *Cool* and the killer's open admission *and* immediate offer of weregild can settle it before it warms — the culture distinguished mischance from murder, but the dead still have kin.
- **Killing the wrong man** in vengeance (striking one who holds no share of the debt): a *new* blood-debt, recorded separately, against the avenger's kin-group. The ledger does not forgive arithmetic errors.
- **Deaths at sea** between feuding groups: if witnessed as deliberate, a debt; if the sea took them, the sea's business — the goði will say the valkyries and Rán sort their own (§XIV).

**Feud-turns.** At *Burning*, the GM runs **feud-turns**: each session, roll **1d6** — on **4+**,
the feud acts. The players may be the debtors, the creditors, or caught between. What the feud does
(roll 1d6, or the GM chooses what the story demands):

- **1–2:** *Theft and spite* — cattle driven off, a storehouse emptied, a boat's lines cut. Wealth bleeds; no blood yet.
- **3–4:** *Fire and wounding* — a farm burned, a kinsman left bloodied at the door as a message. A wounded kinsman is an insult that warms everything it touches.
- **5–6:** *Ambush* — a kinsman is slain. This creates a **new blood-debt** in the other direction — the ledger grows, exactly as the sagas show.

Each feud-turn at *Burning* also risks escalation beyond the two groups: neighbors take sides, the
Thing is petitioned, and the district's frith (§II) begins to fray.

**Settlement (§VII).** Weregild **paid in full at the Thing, publicly** — the silver weighed before
witnesses, the forms spoken — cools the feud to *Settled*. Both sides gain **+1 Orðstír** (honorable
closure is honorable). **Underpayment, late payment, or payment without the proper public forms**
keeps it *Warm* — the debt is acknowledged but not closed, and the temperature will rise again. A
settlement may include marriage-alliance, fosterage (§XXVI), or other bonds — the sagas' handshakes
— at the table's invention; the mechanics require only the full weregild and the public forms.

**Vengeance taken** (blood for blood, done **openly, announced** — the avenger declares the killing
before witnesses, naming the debt it answers): the debt is **transferred, not erased** — the
killer's kin now hold the claim, and the ledger turns its page. Open, announced vengeance costs **no
níð-mark** (it is lawful in the culture's eyes) but grants **no Orðstír** either; it is duty, not
glory. **Secret vengeance** (murder by night, denial after) = **1 níð-mark + the debt stands** — the
culture despised the hidden knife, and the ledger does not recognize what the law cannot see.
Announced vengeance against one who holds *no share* of the debt is murder, not vengeance — a new
debt, as above.

**Letting it fester.** Any PC whose kin-group holds an **unsettled blood-debt at *Burning* for three
months** loses **1 Hamingja per month** — the feud poisons the luck-line (§XVI), as the sagas show
again and again. The loss continues until the debt cools below Burning or is settled. A PC may
*leave* the kin-group's feud only by formal renunciation before the Thing — which costs 2 Orðstír
and the kin's regard, because the culture understood exactly what desertion was.

#### At an OSE Table

- **Recording the debt** — who, of whom, when, witnessed by whom — is **identical**; the ledger is system-neutral. The kin-group (blood, foster-kin, sworn household) is identical.
- **Temperature and its rises are identical:** Cool → Warm → Burning, one step per unaddressed month or on any new insult, theft, or violence; one step down for peaceful Thing attendance or properly formed payment.
- **Edge cases identical:** holmgang/battle/lawful execution = no debt; thrall-killing = property fine; accidents begin Cool; killing the wrong man = a new debt; deaths at sea at the goði's discretion.
- **Feud-turns identical:** at Burning, each session roll **1d6** — on 4+ the feud acts; the 1d6 severity table (theft / fire and wounding / ambush) is **identical** — OSE morale and hireling rules make the fallout bite, but the trigger is the same die. A slain kinsman creates a new blood-debt in the other direction, either system.
- **Settlement identical:** weregild paid in full at the Thing, publicly, cools to Settled, both sides +1 Orðstír — weregild paid in **gp** from the `weregild.json` ladder; underpaid, late, or improper payment stays Warm.
- **Vengeance identical:** open, announced vengeance *transfers* the debt (no níð-mark, no Orðstír); secret vengeance = 1 níð-mark + the debt stands. Renunciation before the Thing costs 2 Orðstír, either system.

- Full dual-system reference: `SYSTEM_VARIANTS.md` (Appendix — Codex §XXI).

#### Worked Example

A 5E example (illustrative). At a feast, a drunken PC strikes down a man of a neighboring kin-group
over an insult — no holmgang, no battle, no law: an unlawful killing. The GM records the blood-debt
openly: *who* (the PC, named), *of whom* (the slain man, named), *when* (this winter), *witnessed by
whom* (the whole hall). The debt begins at *Cool*, priced from the weregild ladder. The PC's kin
send no messenger and offer nothing for a month: the debt warms. At the spring Thing, insults are
exchanged publicly and the victim's kin demand judgment: still *Warm*, but now the forms are
engaged. Then a hothead of the victim's kin burns the PC's family's boathouse — new violence between
the groups: the debt goes *Burning*, immediately. Each session now, the GM rolls 1d6 for the
feud-turn: on a 4+, the feud acts — say a 5, an ambush, and a kinsman of the PC's group is slain.
That is a *new* blood-debt, now owed the other way. The wise move was always the same: weregild, in
full, at the Thing, before witnesses — Settled, +1 Orðstír both sides, handshakes. At an OSE table
every die and every step is identical; only the currency of the weregild (gp) needs no translation.

### III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

The feud turns the party's violence into **politics with a ledger**. Players learn to ask, before
the sword comes out, *"who will own this killing tomorrow?"* — which is the single most Viking
question in the codex. They learn that vengeance was obligation rather than rage (no glory in it, no
Orðstír — only duty done or duty shirked), that the hidden knife was despised precisely because it
cheated the law, and that the system *could* end feuds — the handshake and the weregild were real —
which makes the Burning of Bergþórshvoll land as tragedy rather than inevitability. The festering
rule teaches the saga's deepest pattern: the feud doesn't just kill men, it *poisons luck*, and the
whole kin-group pays.

## Seafaring — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 21)

**Video:** `videos/ep21-seafaring.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep21-seafaring.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §XIII` — this file expands that section; additions the codex
leaves open are labeled **(Expansion)**.

### I. Grounding — Why It Is True

Norse seamanship was supreme without a single fantasy aid. No compass — and the famous "sunstone" is
debated enough that the codex flags it: leave it out of your game ⚠️. No charts as we know them.
Navigation was *embodied knowledge*: the sun and the stars, headlands memorized like faces, the
color and smell of the water, bird flight at dusk, whale roads, the sounding-lead. Saga voyages are
told in time, not distance — so many nights at sea between known shores [Heim; Njál] — and the codex
systematizes that telling into the game's unit of planning: the *day's sail*, with the *dægr*
(half-day) beneath it. The daily sailing check and *hafvilla* as a named game-state are Volmarr's
design; the methods the check tests are the history.

Ships were beached, not harbored. Fleets were drawn up on shore each night, and a ship left riding
at anchor unwatched was a ship that could be cut loose — by thieves, by rivals, by the tide's own
mischief. And a ship was worth more than a farm: a seaworthy hull represented years of timber, iron,
and skilled labor, the single costliest thing most households would ever own. Losing one was not an
inconvenience. It was ruin.

Storms were not weather. They were wyrd wearing a grey cloak. Sailors feared Rán's net for the
drowned [codex §XIV], and even seiðr weather-working — the tradition's one answer to the wind — took
hours of ritual and only *asked*; it never commanded. The three storm-choices (run, beach, ride out)
are the codex's own, and they are honest: every option costs something, because the sea always
collects.

### II. The Complete Playable Rule

#### At a 5E Table

**Planning the voyage.** The steersman (*stýrimaðr*) and the GM lay the route in legs between known
landmarks, using the realm's real geography (Birka to Hedeby, Kaupang to Dublin; see canon location
tables). **(Expansion)** Each leg is assigned a length in day's sails by the GM: a coastal leg is
rarely more than 3 day's sails; an open-sea crossing runs 2–6. A laden ship carries provisions
measured in *days* — 14 days is a standard load **(Expansion)** — and every day's sail burns one,
fair or foul.

**The sailing check.** Each day's sail, the steersman rolls **Wisdom (Survival)**. Advantage if the
crew includes a seasoned *leiðsögumaðr* (a pilot who knows these waters) or if landmarks are
visible; disadvantage in fog, storm, or unknown waters. **(Expansion)** Sailing by night is at
disadvantage unless hugging a familiar lit coast. If the steersman is incapacitated, another crew
member may take the steering-oar at disadvantage unless proficient with water vehicles.

**Hafvilla.** Failure is not "lost at once" — it is *hafvilla*, sea-lost: off course, supplies
burning, the GM rolling secretly. **(Expansion)** On a failed check the GM secretly rolls 1d4 extra
day's sails burned and 1d8 for drift direction. On a *second consecutive* failure the ship is truly
lost — landfall unknown — and only bird-flight reading (see below) or a pilot's local knowledge can
recover the course. A natural 20 on the sailing check finds a kindly current: one day's sail is
gained back.

**Reading the sea.** Any crew member, once per day: **Intelligence (Nature)** — bird flight at dusk
(landward), whale spouts (deep water), water color over shallows, cloud banks over unseen islands.
Success gives the steersman advantage on the next sailing check. *This is the Norse GPS.*
**(Expansion)** Advantage from multiple readers does not stack; but success by 5 or more also
reveals one extra true fact about nearby waters (a skerry, a current, a reef-line). Reading the sea
is the only way to recover from true hafvilla without a pilot.

**Storms.** Storms are wyrd, not random encounters. **(Expansion)** The GM calls for a storm when
the saga demands it, or rolls 1 in 6 per day's sail in storm season; severity is 1d6: 1–3 squall,
4–5 gale, 6 great storm. The crew chooses:
- *Run before it:* lose course — **(Expansion)** 1d3 day's sails off course — and risk the ship
  (gale or worse: the ship drops one hull-state, see below).
- *Beach the ship:* lose time, save lives — **(Expansion)** 1d3 days lost finding and making a
  beachable shore; requires a coast that can be beached, which the GM may deny on cliff-bound
  shores.
- *Ride it out:* every crew member makes a Constitution save vs. exhaustion — **(Expansion)** DC 10
  (squall), 13 (gale), 15 (great storm) — and the ship is tested (see below).

There is no *control weather* shortcut at this table — even seiðr weather-working (§V) takes hours
of ritual and only *asks* the wind.

**(Expansion) Hull-states.** A ship is Seaworthy, Strained, or Crippled. A failed ride-out or
running before a gale drops it one state. A Crippled ship must be beached and repaired: a
shipwright, timber, and 1d6 days; cost in silver set by the GM against the weregild ladder
(`engine/data/homebrew/weregild.json`). A Strained ship sails at disadvantage on storm saves until
repaired.

**Rán's offering** (§XIV): a silver offering poured to Rán before sailing grants advantage on one
storm-save. The old custom, given teeth.

**Beaching.** Ships are hauled ashore each night where possible. **(Expansion)** A posted
anchor-watch keeps an anchored ship safe; a ship left anchored *unwatched* may be cut loose — by
thieves, by a rival's knife, by spite — and the GM is entitled to make it so. A ship is worth more
than a farm: guard it like one.

**Landfall protocol.** **(Expansion)** Custom demands the carved figureheads be unshipped before making
landfall, lest the sight frighten the landvættir (§XXV). A crew that beaches with heads up in
inhabited waters risks the land noted *cold* until a greeting-offering is made — the §XXV honoring
rite, triggered by carelessness. Wise steersmen ship the heads before the coast is sighted.

**The leadsman's cast.** **(Expansion)** In unknown coastal waters one crew member tends the
sounding-lead. This counts as the day's Reading the Sea attempt, focused on shallows and reefs: on
success the steersman learns the safe channel and gains advantage on the next sailing check exactly
as the codex's reading grants.

#### At an OSE Table

- Planning, provisions, and legs: **identical** — the whale-road is measured in day's sails either
  system.
- The sailing check: **1d20 ≤ Wisdom** each day's sail; **−2** (easier) with a seasoned pilot aboard
  or visible landmarks, **+2** (harder) in fog, storm, unknown waters, or night sailing off a
  strange coast. Hafvilla and true-hafvilla work exactly as above.
- Reading the sea: **1d20 ≤ Intelligence**, once per day per reader; success gives the steersman
  **−2** on the next sailing check (advantage's OSE voice); success by a wide margin reveals one
  extra true fact.
- Storms: the three wyrd-choices **identical**; ride-it-out Constitution saves become **Save vs
  Death** (one roll per crew member; the severity table still sets how hard the GM should be —
  squall/gale/great storm). No *control weather* in either system.
- Hull-states: **identical** — Seaworthy / Strained / Crippled; a Strained ship imposes **+2** on
  storm saves until repaired.
- Rán's offering: a silver offering grants **+2 on one storm-save** (or a re-roll), per
  `SYSTEM_VARIANTS.md` Ep 17.
- Beaching and the anchor-watch: **identical** — a ship is worth more than a farm either system.

#### Worked Example

*Illustrative — hypothetical rolls, not a real session.* Astrid steers a knarr on the third day of a
Kaupang–Hedeby leg. Fog rolls in (disadvantage), but the ship's boy read the sea at dawn and
succeeded (advantage) — the two cancel, so Astrid rolls straight Wisdom (Survival): she rolls 9, +5
modifier = 14 against the GM's DC 15. *Hafvilla.* The GM secretly rolls 1d4 = 3 extra day's sails
burned and notes the drift. The crew now has a decision: burn supplies finding their way by
bird-flight, or press on and risk a second failure — true hafvilla. At an OSE table the same beat is
one roll: 1d20 ≤ Astrid's Wisdom, +2 for the fog, −2 for the reading — straight roll-under, same
cold arithmetic.

### III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

Players learn navigation as *literacy* — reading birds, water, and sky the way a reader sounds out
letters — and they learn it under pressure, because every day's sail spends real provisions and
every failure visibly burns them. Nobody who has sweated through a hafvilla, counting days of food
against a grey horizon, will ever again treat a voyage as a loading screen. They feel in their gut
why the Norse called the sea the *whale-road*: it was a road you had to know by heart, because there
was nothing else to know it by.

## Outlawry — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 22)

**Video:** `videos/ep22-outlawry.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep22-outlawry.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §XXII` — this file expands that section; additions the codex
leaves open are labeled **(Expansion)**.

### I. Grounding — Why It Is True

The law had two exiles, and they were not the same country. **Fjörbaugsgarðr** — "lesser outlawry" —
meant three years' banishment: property confiscated, but life protected if the exile stayed away
[Njál]. **Skóggangr** — "forest-walking," full outlawry — meant the man was *outside the law
entirely*: any man might kill him without penalty, none might feed or shelter him on pain of sharing
his fate. The sagas' great witness is Grettir Ásmundarson, sentenced at the Althing and hunted for
nineteen years — the saga says it plainly: his outlawry "had only lasted nineteen years" [Grett] —
the great survivor of the tradition, and its great warning. Outlawry was not freedom. It was the
cold edge of the world, and the law designed it to feel that way.

The teaching logic is the codex's own and it is sound: outlawry teaches the value of the law by
*removing* it. The lesser exile narrows the world — property gone, honor docked, luck capped, kin
allowed one mercy a year. The full exile deletes the person from the legal order: no frith-circle
covers him (§II), no Thing will hear his case, no door may open to him knowingly. What the codex
systematizes — the Exposure track, the bounty as a wandering encounter table, the shadow-reputation
among the discontented, the three narrow doors home — is Volmarr's game design built on the saga
record. The history supplies the terror; the mechanics supply the play.

### II. The Complete Playable Rule

#### At a 5E Table

**Sentencing (at the Thing, §VII).** **(Expansion)** The grade follows the crime: *lesser outlawry*
for killings done in hot blood after provocation where weregild was refused, for repeated theft, for
frith-breaking short of killing; *full outlawry (skóggangr)* for murder by stealth, for killing
after a settlement, for killing within the Thing's circle, for níðingr-acts the law cannot price.
The GM announces the grade; the sentence is read aloud before witnesses.

**Lesser outlawry (fjörbaugsgarðr).** The PC must leave the district for three years — hand-wave the
calendar or play it in montage. Property is forfeit to the injured party. While exiled:
- **−3 Orðstír**, recoverable on lawful return. **(Expansion)** Recovery is +1 per year of lawful
  conduct after return — or immediate and full if the return comes by pardon or a voted deed.
- **Hamingja capped at 5** — the world is narrower now. **(Expansion)** Gains above 5 are lost while
  the cap holds; the cap lifts on lawful return.
- Shelter from kin is permitted *once* per year without penalty to the shelterer; more, and the
  shelterer answers at the Thing.
- **(Expansion)** Returning to the district before the three years are served upgrades the sentence
  to full outlawry. The law does not warn twice.

**Full outlawry (skóggangr).** The sentence is read, and the PC becomes *óbotamaðr* — a man with no
price, because no price can buy him back.
- **At sentencing the outlaw's Orðstír is reduced to 0 — *vargr*, wolf** (per the Thing's law,
  `SYSTEM_VARIANTS.md` Ep 14). The +1 below is a *separate shadow ledger*, not a restoration.
- **No frith** (§II): none of the eight circles protect you. Anyone may kill you without weregild;
  no Thing will hear your case. **(Expansion)** Killing an outlaw earns the killer no Orðstír
  either — there is no honor in it — and no debt. It is simply uncounted.
- **No shelter:** any NPC who feeds or houses you *knowingly* risks the same sentence.
  **(Expansion)** *Knowingly* is the hinge: shelter given in ignorance is no crime — but once the
  shelterer learns, they must turn the outlaw out or share the sentence. The GM should make every
  door a decision.
- **Survival play:** the outlaw lives by hunting, fishing, and the charity of the brave few. Track
  **Exposure**: each week in the wilds without proper shelter, **Constitution save DC 12 + 1 per
  consecutive week**, or gain one level of **exhaustion**. **(Expansion)** Proper shelter means a
  roof, a cave with fire, a dugout, a ship's tent — not a cloak under a tree. Winter doubles the
  *total* DC — week three in winter is DC (12+2)×2 = 28: Grettir's saga is a winter saga for a
  reason, and the arithmetic should frighten the table. **(Expansion)** While exiled, each day's
  foraging is a Wisdom (Survival) check; a failed day makes that week's Exposure save at
  disadvantage — hunger and cold hunt together.
- **The price on your head:** the GM sets a silver bounty. **(Expansion)** A working scale: 1–3× the
  weregild that would have settled the crime (`engine/data/homebrew/weregild.json`).
  Bounty-hunters are a wandering encounter table — **(Expansion)** roll 1d6: 1, a lone tracker; 2,
  a pair with hounds; 3–4, a huscarl troop (1d4+2); 5, a rival outlaw hoping to buy pardon with
  your head; 6, the wronged kin themselves, and this one is personal. *Killing* a hunter in fair
  fight earns no new debt (you are outside the law; the law cannot count your kills), but it earns
  **+1 Orðstír among outlaws, thieves, and the discontented** — a shadow-reputation with its own
  uses: fences who will buy, hideouts that will open, outlaw markets that will trade.
  **(Expansion)** Shadow-Orðstír is worthless outside those circles — and flashing it among lawful
  folk marks you as what you are.

**Lifting the sentence.** Lesser outlawry ends by time served. Full outlawry ends only by:
- *Royal pardon* — **(Expansion)** a king or jarl's price: triple weregild plus a named service, and
  the pardon must be spoken before witnesses;
- *Lawful killing of a worse outlaw* — **(Expansion)** the dead outlaw must be full-outlawed and
  worse than you (the GM judges); the head is presented at the Thing;
- *A deed so great the Thing itself votes you back* — **(Expansion)** saga-ending by definition:
  breaking a raiding fleet, laying a draugr that plagued the district (§XXV), and it requires a
  speaker of good Orðstír to plead the case (§VII) — no one pleads their own pardon.
The GM sets the price, and it should feel like a saga-ending. **(Expansion)** An outlaw taken alive
may be brought before the Thing, which may confirm the sentence or — rarely, and only for a deed
already done — commute it. The law keeps one door unbarred, barely.

**Hunter frequency.** **(Expansion)** The GM rolls for bounty-hunters when the saga demands it, or 1 in
6 per week while the outlaw hides in inhabited districts; in true wilderness the roll is 1 in 8 —
even hunters fear the deep woods, and Grettir survived as long as he did by knowing it.

**The outlaw's kit.** **(Expansion)** An outlaw keeps what he can carry: weapons, a cloak, fishing
line, a fire-steel. Anything more is a cache — and caches are found. After any hunter encounter, the
GM may rule a known cache compromised. Travel light or travel loudly; there is no third way.

#### At an OSE Table

- Sentencing grades and the **(Expansion)** crime-to-grade guidance: **identical** — the law is
  system-neutral.
- Lesser outlawry: three years banished, property forfeit, −3 Orðstír until lawful return (recovery
  as above), Hamingja capped at 5, kin shelter once per year, early return upgrades to full —
  **identical**.
- Full outlawry: Orðstír to 0 (*vargr*); no frith; killable without weregild and without honor or
  debt to the killer; no knowing shelter — **identical**; additionally **no hirelings will serve
  you and no stronghold will take you in** (per `SYSTEM_VARIANTS.md` Ep 14/22).
- Exposure: **Save vs Death** each week in the wilds without proper shelter, or gain one exhaustion
  level; DC 12 +1 per consecutive week, winter doubling the total — **identical**; foraging
  failures impose disadvantage on the week's save, as above.
- Bounty in **gp**; the 1d6 hunter table **identical**; fair-fight kills earn no debt but +1
  shadow-Orðstír among the discontented — identical.
- Lifting the sentence: **identical** — time served for the lesser; pardon, a worse outlaw's lawful
  death, or a Thing-voted deed for the full.

#### Worked Example

*Illustrative — hypothetical rolls, not a real session.* Brand is three weeks into skóggangr, hiding
in autumn woods. His Exposure save: DC 12 + 2 (two consecutive prior weeks) = 14. He rolls
Constitution: 11 + 3 = 14 — he holds, shivering, one more week. Had it been winter, the DC would be
(12+2)×2 = 28 — Grettir's arithmetic, and the table goes quiet doing it. Later a lone tracker finds
his fire. Brand kills him in fair fight: no debt accrues (the law cannot count the kills of the
lawless), and Brand gains +1 shadow-Orðstír — the next outlaw camp he finds will know his name, and
lawful folk must never hear it. At an OSE table the same week is a Save vs Death at the same rising
cost; the cold does not care what dice you roll.

### III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

Outlawry teaches the value of the law by *removing* it, and the lesson lands in the body before it
lands in the head. Players who have lived three sessions as the hunted — counting weeks, watching
doors close, feeling the arithmetic of winter turn murderous — will never again treat the Thing as
set-dressing. They will understand, physically, what *friðr* was: the warm circle, and the cold
outside it. And when they finally stand inside the circle again, lawful and heard, the relief at the
table is real. That relief is the entire point of the mechanic.

## Draumr — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 23)

**Video:** `videos/ep23-draumr.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep23-draumr.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §XXIII` — this file expands that section; additions the codex
leaves open are labeled **(Expansion)**.

### I. Grounding — Why It Is True

The sagas treat dreams as *dispatches*, not metaphors. The great case is Guðrún Ósvífursdóttir in
*Laxdæla saga*: she dreams four dreams, and the wise man Gest Oddleifsson interprets each — the saga
gives the scene its own chapter, "Of Gest Oddleifson and Gudrun's Dreams" — and each comes true, to
the letter, across her four marriages: the golden helm she thought too heavy to bear becomes the
fourth husband who will be heavy to bear [Lax] (verified in corpus). Dream after dream in the sagas
warns of death, betrayal, and doom — and the wise *listen*; the doomed do not. To ignore a true
dream was not skepticism. It was *negligence*, and the sagas bill it accordingly.

That is the whole of the mechanic's moral logic, and it is historical: the dream-world was the gods'
postal service, and contempt for the post was contempt for the senders. What the codex builds on it
— the secret 1d6 dream-roll at significant rests, the interpretation check with its muddled-failure
state, the heeding economy (+1 Hamingja and the softened disaster), the mockery penalty, and
*dream-debt* (the dream-deaf dead earn no inherited honor) — is Volmarr's design. The history
supplies the premise that dreams are intelligence; the game supplies the discipline of earning the
reading.

### II. The Complete Playable Rule

#### At a 5E Table

**The dream-roll.** At each long rest taken in a *place of significance*, the GM rolls secretly: on
**1–4 on 1d6**, the sleeper dreams *true*. **(Expansion)** Places of significance: the night before
battle; the night after a killing; at a holy site, mound, or shore of the dead; during Yule; the
night after swearing an oath; aboard ship on the eve of a crossing. Ordinary inn-nights do not
qualify — the GM may add sites, never subtract the list's meaning. **(Expansion)** The GM chooses
which sleeper dreams (or rolls randomly among the PCs); one dream per rest, no matter how
significant the ground — the gods' post is limited.

**The dream.** The GM gives it as imagery — never as instruction. Draw from
`engine/data/homebrew/dreams.json`, or invent in-kind. **(Expansion)** A working image-bank: white
bears, blood on snow, a broken sword, the dead speaking, drowning water, a hall burning, ravens on a
roof-ridge, a severed head smiling, iron gone soft as wax. The GM maps each image to a real coming
threat *before* speaking it — the image never names the threat, and the GM never explains it. That
is the players' work.

**Interpretation.** Any character may attempt to read the dream — **Wisdom (Insight) DC 13**,
advantage if a völva, goði, or recognized wise one does the reading (§XXIV). **(Expansion)** One
reading per dream: the dreamer chooses the reader, and the first reading stands — a failed reading
cannot be retried, because the words are spoken and the hall has heard them. Success: the GM states
the warning plainly (*"Do not take the mountain road," "Your oath-helper means you harm"*). Failure:
the reading is muddled — the GM gives two meanings, one true (the GM notes which; the table never
knows).

**Heeding.** Acting on a rightly-read dream — changing the plan, posting a guard, refusing the
voyage — grants **+1 Hamingja** (§XVI; Hamingja caps at 10). The warned-against disaster, if it was
coming, is *softened*: the GM reduces its force. **(Expansion)** Softened, quantified: halve the
ambushers, or remove their surprise; grant advantage on the key save against the storm or the
poison; the traitor's knife finds mail — the first treacherous attack is made at disadvantage with
warning given. **(Expansion)** If no disaster was coming, the +1 Hamingja stands anyway — the
universe rewards those who listen even when the wolf stays home. **(Expansion)** Half-measures are
the GM's call: heeding means acting on the *warning*, not on convenience — one guard posted against
a warned ambush is not heeding; it is decoration.

**Ignoring.** A rightly-read dream ignored = **−1 Hamingja** and the disaster arrives at *full*
force. **(Expansion)** Hamingja cannot drop below 0 — at 0, the full force of the disaster *is* the
price, and the GM should make it felt.

**Mocking.** A dream *mocked* ("dreams are wind") = **−1 Orðstír** among all who heard the mockery.
**(Expansion)** "All who heard" means everyone present at the mocking — the penalty is witnessed and
local, not universal. **(Expansion)** A mocker cannot later gain Hamingja from heeding *that* dream;
contempt, once spoken, cannot be unspent.

**Dream-debt.** If a PC dies having ignored a true dream that warned of that very death, the saga
records it — the survivors' retelling grants the dead no inherited Orðstír bonus (§I: the dying well
pass half their Orðstír to the heir). The culture's epitaph for the dream-deaf: *he was warned.*
**(Expansion)** If the dream warned of *another's* death and the dreamer ignored it, the dead lose
the bonus and the dreamer takes −1 Hamingja for the negligence — the debt follows the deaf ear, not
only the corpse.

**(Expansion) A d8 dream-image table** — roll or choose when the GM needs an image quickly. Each is
imagery only; the GM maps it to a real coming threat *before* speaking it:

1. A white bear walking upright, wearing a man's belt.
2. Blood on snow, steaming, with no wound in sight.
3. A broken sword, the break fresh and bright.
4. The dead speaking — a known voice from a closed mouth.
5. Drowning water, black and rising, in a dry hall.
6. A hall burning, and the fire is patient.
7. Ravens on a roof-ridge, all facing the same road.
8. A severed head, smiling, that knows your name.

**When the dream warns about a PC.** **(Expansion)** If the true reading implicates a player character
(the oath-helper who means harm is sitting at the table), the GM states the warning plainly to the
*reader* — and lets the table do the rest. The codex's rule doesn't change; the drama sharpens. The
accused PC's player is not told the reading is about them until the fiction reveals it.

**The un-dreamed rest.** **(Expansion)** On a 5–6 — no true dream — the GM may describe ordinary
sleep, or nothing at all. Silence is also information: the table learns that not every night is
significant, which makes the significant ones land harder.

#### At an OSE Table

- The dream-roll: **identical** — secret 1d6 at each long rest in a place of significance (the
  defined list above), 1–4 dreams true, one dream per rest; imagery, never instruction.
- Interpretation: **1d20 ≤ Wisdom** (DC 13 in 5E terms), **−2** (easier) if a völva, goði, or
  recognized wise one reads; one reading per dream, no retries; success states the warning
  plainly, failure gives two meanings, one true — identical.
- Heeding: +1 Hamingja (cap 10) and the disaster softened per the quantified guidance above —
  **identical**; Hamingja is system-neutral.
- Ignoring and mocking: −1 Hamingja and full force (never below 0), or −1 Orðstír among the hearers;
  the mocker cannot earn back Hamingja on that dream; dream-debt for the dream-deaf dead —
  **identical** either system.

#### Worked Example

*Illustrative — hypothetical rolls, not a real session.* The party camps on a burial mound the night
before marching on a rival hall. The GM secretly rolls 1d6 = 2 — true dream. She tells the
watch-leader: *"You dream of ravens on the roof-ridge of a burning hall — and the hall has your own
door."* The dreamer asks the party's goði to read it. Wisdom (Insight) with advantage: the player
rolls 16 and 7, taking 16, +2 = 18 against DC 13 — the GM states it plainly: *"Do not march at dawn;
the hall is watched, and the watchers expect you."* The party waits a day and scouts instead: +1
Hamingja, and the ambush the GM had prepared arrives *softened* — half the ambushers, no surprise.
Had they marched anyway, it would have been −1 Hamingja and the full weight of the trap. At an OSE
table the reading is 1d20 ≤ the goði's Wisdom at −2; the ravens mean exactly the same thing.

### III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

Dreams become the party's early-warning radar — but one they must *interpret*, never one that hands
them answers. Players learn the saga-mind: the world is always speaking; wisdom is the discipline of
listening. And because the reading can fail, because heeding costs a changed plan, because mockery
is priced in honor — the table learns that listening is not passive. It is a skill, a gamble, and a
duty, all at once. After a few sessions, players start *asking* for the dream-roll the way sailors
ask for the wind. That is the culture taking root.

## Spá & Útiseta — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 24)

**Video:** `videos/ep24-spa-utiseta.mp4` · **Transcript:** `transcripts/ep24-spa-utiseta.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §XXIV` — this file expands that section; additions the codex
leaves open are labeled **(Expansion)**.

### I. Grounding — Why It Is True

The völva's spá — the prophecy spoken from the high seat — is the oldest recorded voice in the
tradition: *Völuspá*, "the Seeress's Prophecy," opens the Poetic Edda with a witch's vision of the
world's making and unmaking, summoned by Óðinn himself and beginning, in Bellows's rendering,
*"Hearing I ask from the holy races"* [PE] (verified: Poetic Edda, Völuspá st. 1). *Spá* was not
fortune-telling as entertainment; it was *consultation with consequence*. The seeker came with a
real question and paid a real price — and the answer came veiled, because fate does not speak
plainly to those it is about to bind.

**Útiseta** — "sitting out" — was the related rite of sitting alone outdoors through the night, at a
mound or crossroads, to win wisdom or speech with spirits. The codex flags this honestly and so do
we: ⚠️ it is attested in later sources and the rite's details are thin — treat what follows as
tradition-shaped mechanics, not history. The one-question limit, the riddle-answer, the small price
and the wyrd-price, the forbidden questions answered with silence — these are Volmarr's
systematization of the tradition's *shape*: divination rationed by cost, because a culture that
believed prophecy worked could not afford to make it cheap.

### II. The Complete Playable Rule

#### At a 5E Table

**Asking the spá** (a völva NPC, or a PC with the seiðr-path, §V). The seeker states *one* question
aloud, before witnesses. **(Expansion)** At least three witnesses, and the question is spoken once,
in exact words — the way oaths are spoken (§IX): exactness matters, because the answer will hold you
to what you *said*, not what you meant. The völva enters trance (an hour; the hall waits — play the
waiting). **(Expansion)** Play the waiting: no one speaks; each witness may once whisper a fear
aloud, which the völva may or may not take up. The hour is part of the price.

The GM answers with a **riddle-answer**: true, but veiled — a verse, an image, a kenning-chain. The
answer is *never* a plan; it is a weather-report of wyrd. **(Expansion)** GM craft: the riddle must
be *answerable in hindsight* — write the true meaning down and seal it (a folded note, a private
file). If the table cannot see, afterward, that the answer was true, it was not a riddle; it was
noise, and the völva owes the price back.

**The price** — fate does no free samples:
- *The small price:* a gift worthy of the answer — silver, a weapon, a service named on the spot
  (§VI). **(Expansion)** The GM judges worth against the question's weight; a question of counsel
  wants silver, a question of doom wants something that hurts to give. Stingy payment = muddled
  answer (the GM gives two meanings, one true — as §XXIII).
- *The wyrd-price:* after any spá, the seeker gains **+1 Wyrd-debt** (§III). Knowing the shape of
  doom binds you tighter to it. **(Expansion)** Wyrd-debt cannot be declined — the binding is in
  the *knowing*, not the asking. Note the interaction: at 5 Wyrd-debt the Norns draw three runes
  openly as an unavoidable doom (`SYSTEM_VARIANTS.md` Ep 6) — the spá-seeker walks toward that
  reading faster than other PCs. Count accordingly.
- *The forbidden questions:* asking *when* you die, or demanding the gods change what is woven — the
  völva answers with silence, and the seeker loses **1 Hamingja** for the presumption. Some
  threads are not for pulling. **(Expansion)** A second forbidden question the same night: the
  völva refuses all further questions, and the presumption, witnessed, costs **−1 Orðstír** among
  the witnesses.

**(Expansion) The völva's risk.** The völva herself takes on spiritual risk — which is why wise
völur sometimes *refuse* to answer, and the culture respected the refusal. If she refuses *before*
the trance, no price is paid and the gift is returned. If she refuses *mid-trance* — the spirits
troubled — the small price is kept but no wyrd-debt accrues: no answer, no binding. If a völva (NPC
or PC) knowingly speaks *false* spá, she takes +1 Wyrd-debt herself and risks a níð-mark for
oath-breaking (§XVII): prophecy is a sworn utterance, and lies in it are perjury against fate.

**Útiseta** (a PC-declared night rite). The character sits out alone from dusk to dawn at a mound,
shore, or crossroads, fasting. **(Expansion)** The rite's conditions, all required: the site must be
a place of significance (§XXIII); *alone* means no companions within sight or earshot; *fasting*
means no food or drink; no sleep, and no leaving a few paces' circle — the sitter is a post planted
in the ground until dawn. **Constitution save DC 13** at dawn, or gain one level of **exhaustion**
(the night is cold and the spirits are not kind). Success — truly sitting the whole night: the GM
grants **one true dream-image** (§XXIII — the interpretation rules apply) *or* a whispered answer to
a single question, at the cost of **+1 Wyrd-debt**. **(Expansion)** The whisper is plainer than spá
— a sentence, not a riddle — but narrower: one image, or one sentence, and nothing more. Útiseta may
not be repeated for the same question — rewording counts; the spirits are not fooled — and the
second sitting brings only wind: no roll, no answer, the night simply spent. **(Expansion)** The
dawn exhaustion save still applies on a failed second sitting. The cold does not care about your
question.

**(Expansion) Three sealed examples** for the GM's shelf — each with the question, the sealed true
meaning, and the riddle as spoken:

1. *"Will my voyage prosper?"* — Sealed: the ship returns; the cargo is lost to wreckers.
   Riddle: *"The keel comes home; the hold does not."*
2. *"Who betrayed my brother?"* — Sealed: his oath-helper did it for silver.
   Riddle: *"The hand that held his shield now holds the knife's price."*
3. *"Should I take the jarl's offer?"* — Sealed: the offer is honest, but the jarl dies within the
   year and his heir will not honor it. Riddle: *"The gift is clean; the giver's thread is short."*

**(Expansion) Spá for the group.** One question, one seeker — but a jarl may ask on behalf of his
people, a steersman on behalf of his crew. The wyrd-debt falls on the *asker* alone. The witnesses
share the hearing, not the binding. Choose who asks the way you choose who bleeds.

#### At an OSE Table

- Asking the spá: one question aloud before witnesses (three at least), in exact words; an hour of
  trance — play the waiting — and a riddle-answer, true, veiled, never a plan; the GM's sealed
  true meaning — **identical**; the rite is system-neutral.
- The small price: a worthy gift (silver, weapon, named service), priced in **gp**; stingy payment =
  muddled answer (two meanings, one true) — identical.
- The wyrd-price: +1 Wyrd-debt after any spá, undeferrable; the Norn's-reading interaction at 5 —
  **identical**; forbidden questions (when you die; demanding the gods change what's woven)
  answered with silence, seeker −1 Hamingja; a second forbidden question refused with −1 Orðstír
  among witnesses — identical.
- The völva's risk and refusal etiquette — **identical**, as above.
- Útiseta: alone from dusk to dawn at a mound, shore, or crossroads (a place of significance),
  fasting, no sleep, no leaving the circle; **Save vs Death** at dawn or gain one level of
  exhaustion; success grants one true dream-image or a whispered answer at +1 Wyrd-debt; never
  repeated for the same question — the second sitting brings only wind, and the cold still bites —
  **identical**.

#### Worked Example

*Illustrative — hypothetical rolls, not a real session.* Before a winter voyage, the steersman asks
the völva: *"Does the whale-road take my ship or spare it?"* — spoken once, before five witnesses,
with a silver arm-ring laid at her feet. The hall waits the hour. The GM's sealed meaning: *the ship
survives; the steersman's brother, who sails with him, does not.* The riddle-answer: *"The road
keeps what it is given; count your crew when you come home, and count again."* The seeker gains +1
Wyrd-debt — the binding is in the knowing. Had he asked instead *"When do I die?"* — silence, and −1
Hamingja for the presumption, the hall watching. Later, his sister sits útiseta at her father's
mound for the same question about the voyage: at dawn she rolls Constitution, 12 + 1 = 13 — she
holds — and the GM whispers one true image: a helm floating. The interpretation rules of §XXIII do
the rest. At an OSE table the dawn save is Save vs Death; the wind charges the same price either
system.

### III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

Divination becomes *expensive*, which teaches why the culture rationed it. Players learn to save
their one question for the one that matters — and to fear the answer slightly, which is the correct
relationship with prophecy. The riddle-answer trains a second skill: living with a true sentence you
cannot yet parse, the way the saga characters lived with theirs. And útiseta teaches the loneliest
lesson in the codex: that some knowledge can only be bought with a night of your own life, spent
cold and alone — and that fate does not enjoy being nagged.

## Landvættir & the Dead — Full Rule Expansion (The Norse Way, Episode 25)

**Video:** `videos/ep25-landvaettir-dead.mp4` · **Transcript:**
`transcripts/ep25-landvaettir-dead.md`
**Codex source:** `HOMEBREW_CODEX.md §XXV` — this file expands that section; additions the codex
leaves open are labeled **(Expansion)**.

### I. Grounding — Why It Is True

The land was *inhabited*. The **landvættir** — spirits of place — followed great men in crowds
[Heim], and they were owed respect as a matter of custom: ships were to unship their carved
figureheads before making landfall, lest the sight frighten the spirits ⚠️ (Landnámabók tradition —
later record, old custom). Egill Skalla-Grímsson knew their power well enough to weaponize it:
raising his níðstöng against King Eiríkr, he turned the curse explicitly *"á landvættir þær, er land
þetta byggva"* — "on the land-spirits who inhabit this land" — bidding them hound the king from the
country (verified in the Old Norse text, `old_norse/egils-saga-sagadb-old-norse.txt`). You do not
aim a curse at nothing.

The dead, too, remained neighbors. The mound was a dwelling, and the unquiet dead — the **draugr** —
walked when burial was denied, rites were skipped, or the dead had unfinished business. Glámr, the
shepherd who died unshriven and unmourned, rose to terrorize a valley until Grettir wrestled him
[Grett] (verified in corpus). Þórólfr bægifótr haunted his own farmstead from a botched burial
[Eyrb]. The lesson of both, and of the whole section, is the codex's own sentence: *the dead are a
maintenance obligation.* The honoring rite, the insult and its haunting, the draugr's business, the
seasonal ancestor-maintenance — these are Volmarr's mechanics built on that sentence. The history
says the neighbors are real; the game teaches you to pay the rent.

### II. The Complete Playable Rule

#### At a 5E Table

**Honoring the landvættir.** When entering new territory, founding a farm, or before a voyage: a
small offering — food, drink poured out, a silver bit — and a spoken greeting. **(Expansion)** "New
territory" means crossing into a district where you are unknown, or ground no one of your kin has
walked. Cost: trivial — a mouthful, a poured cup, a silver bit (a few sp at most). Effect: **+1
Hamingja** (§XVI; caps at 10), and the GM notes the land as *friendly*. **(Expansion)** Friendly
land, in play: finding shelter is at advantage; weather-rolls tilt kindly (the GM steps one storm
severity down, or grants +2 on related checks). The note lasts the season, or until you leave.
Skipping it where custom demands: **−1 Hamingja** and the land as *cold* — the GM is entitled to one
petty misfortune. **(Expansion)** Roll or choose 1d6: 1, a horse goes lame; 2, a food cache spoils;
3, a tool or oar breaks at the worst moment; 4, a water skin fouls; 5, the trail is lost — half a
day's sail wasted; 6, a sudden squall on a fine day. The misfortune should be *traceable*: the table
should be able to connect it to the skipped greeting. That connection is the lesson.

**Insulting the landvættir.** Fouling a holy spring, cutting a sacred grove, raising a níðstöng
*falsely* on their ground: **−2 Hamingja** and a **haunting**. **(Expansion)** An *honest* níðstöng
— raised for a true grievance per §XVII — does not insult the landvættir; it *enlists* them against
the target. Falseness is the insult. The GM runs minor phenomena: livestock sicken, tools break,
milk sours, sleep is troubled — no benefit from one long rest in seven — until atonement is made: a
proper blót at the site (§IV) plus a gift of real value. **(Expansion)** "Real value" means at least
the price of a good weapon, or a service the GM names; the blót must be public, before witnesses —
private apology does not reach the spirits. **(Expansion)** The haunting falls on the household or
ship's company, not only the insulter: the spirits fine the roof, not just the hand. One haunting
per insulted site; each needs its own atonement; the long-rest penalty does not stack, but neither
does one blót cover two sites.

**The unquiet dead.** Any death without proper rites (§XIV) — no funeral, no remembrance-cup, the
body left unburied — risks the dead *walking*. The GM rolls secretly: on **1–2 on 1d6**, the dead
returns as a **draugr** (use the wight or ghast stat block, SRD, reskinned — bloated, grave-cold,
impossibly strong). A draugr is not a random monster: it has *business*, and it cannot be
permanently laid until the business is settled *and* the rites are performed. **(Expansion)** Roll
or choose the business 1d6: 1, it guards its grave-goods; 2, it punishes its killer; 3, it finishes
its feud; 4, it claims an unpaid debt; 5, it was denied its inheritance; 6, it died unavenged and
demands it. Laying it means settling that business *in the world* — paid, answered, or fought out —
and then the rites: burning or mounding with goods, and the remembrance-cup drunk at the next blót
(§XIV). **(Expansion)** There is no spell that lays the unquiet; only settlement and rites. Killing
it twice without rites just makes it angrier: each destruction without rites, it returns in 1d6
nights with +2 to attack and damage — and now it haunts the killer's household too.

**Ancestor maintenance** (the reward side). Keeping a family's mound, drinking minni to the dead at
sumbel (§XIX), and speaking their names at the Thing: each observed season of this grants the
household **+1 Hamingja** (the luck-line, §XVI) — the dead, properly kept, *lend* their luck to the
living. **(Expansion)** Observed at the seasonal turn (§XII/XV): the mound tended (repaired, swept,
offered to), the minni drunk, the names spoken — each participating household member gains +1
Hamingja (cap 10), once per season. Neglect for a full year: **−1 Hamingja** and restless dreams
(§XXIII) until the mound is tended — **(Expansion)** the GM runs a dream-roll at every long rest
taken at home until the tending is done. **(Expansion)** A landless household honors the dead at the
Thing's common mound or a roadside shrine; the luck-line does not require land, only attention.

**Moving the mound.** **(Expansion)** When a household relocates, the ancestors may be carried: a stone
from the old mound, set in the new with offerings and the names spoken aloud, keeps the luck-line
unbroken — but the first season at the new mound grants no +1 Hamingja. The dead are settling in,
and the living must give them time.

**Shipboard observance.** **(Expansion)** Before a voyage, the greeting-offering may be poured to Rán
and the sea-vættir together with the §XIV silver offering — one rite, both customs honored. The GM
notes the sea *friendly* for the crossing: storm severity stepped down once, as §XXV's friendly-land
effect. Sailors who honor the road are remembered by it.

#### At an OSE Table

- Honoring the landvættir: a small offering and spoken greeting (new territory, farm-founding,
  before a voyage) — +1 Hamingja (cap 10), land noted *friendly* (shelter at advantage; weather
  tilts kindly); skipped, −1 Hamingja and the land *cold* with its 1d6 petty misfortunes —
  **identical**.
- Insulting the landvættir: −2 Hamingja and a haunting (minor phenomena; no benefit from one long
  rest in seven) until a proper public blót plus a gift of real value, priced in **gp**; honest
  níðstöng enlists rather than insults; one haunting per site — **identical**.
- The unquiet dead: secret 1–2 on 1d6 after a death without rites → the dead walks as a draugr — use
  the OSE **wight or ghoul** entry, reskinned; the 1d6 business table **identical**; it cannot be
  permanently laid until the business is settled *and* the rites performed; each rite-less
  destruction returns it in 1d6 nights at +2 attack and damage, haunting the killer's household —
  identical.
- Ancestor maintenance: mound kept, minni at sumbel, names at the Thing → +1 Hamingja per
  participating household member per observed season; a full year's neglect → −1 Hamingja and
  restless dreams (a dream-roll at every home rest) until tended; the landless honor at the common
  mound — **identical**.

#### Worked Example

*Illustrative — hypothetical rolls, not a real session.* The party founds a farm on a new fjord. The
goði pours out ale, speaks the greeting, lays a silver bit on a stone: +1 Hamingja each, and the GM
notes the land *friendly*. Two seasons later a hot-headed PC cuts oaks in the grove above the spring
for a hall-beam — a sacred grove, and everyone knew it. −2 Hamingja, and the haunting begins: the
goats sicken, an axe-head flies off mid-swing, and one long rest in seven gives no benefit until
atonement. The atonement blót is public, the gift is a good sword laid at the spring's edge.
Meanwhile an NPC farmhand dies in a rockfall and the party, hurried, skips his funeral. The GM
secretly rolls 1d6 = 1 — he walks. The draugr's business (rolled 2): it punishes its killer — but
there is no killer, only neglect; the GM rules its business is *the denied rites themselves*, and it
will not rest until they are performed with goods and the remembrance-cup. The party burns it once
without rites — it returns in four nights, stronger, and now it comes to *their* hall. The second
time, they do it right. At an OSE table every number above is the same; only the draugr wears the
wight-or-ghoul entry instead of the SRD's.

### III. Teaching Beat — What the Table Learns

The landscape becomes *populated* — every hill potentially watched, every ancestor a creditor.
Players learn the animist economy: the world is full of persons, most of them not human, and all of
them keep accounts. The genius of the section is that it prices *both* directions: honor is cheap
and profitable, insult is expensive and haunting, neglect compounds like interest. After a season of
play, parties start greeting new land unprompted — not because the rules say so, but because the one
time they didn't, the goats sickened. That is not superstition at the table. That is the culture,
learned the way the culture learned it: by keeping the accounts.
